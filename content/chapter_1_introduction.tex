This introductory chapter will attempt to build the foundation for the explorations that follow by providing a historical overview of chromatin accessibility based on historical accounts\sjcitep[,]{slackConradHalWaddington2002, olinsChromatinHistoryOur2003, cobbWhoDiscoveredMessenger2015, deichmannChromatinItsHistory2015, deichmannEpigeneticsOriginsEvolution2016, lis50YearHistory2019, roeder50YearsEukaryotic2019} and frame it within the ongoing research on decoding gene regulation. Then, we illustrate the state of the art of single-cell chromatin sequencing technologies when I began this work, and delve into the capabilities and limitations of several breakthrough techniques. This context serves to highlight the importance of the subject matter, and to motivate our subsequent discussions and findings.

\section{Early research}
The word "cell" was first used in 1665 by Robert Hooke to describe the hexagonal walls he observed in a thin slice of cork through a self-made microscope\sjcitep[.]{hookeMicrographia1665} It took several more centuries of research until the concept of cells as the foundational building blocks of life began to take shape. Between 1817 and 1826, Baltic-German polymath and explorer Karl Ernst von Baer, made the first accounts of egg cells, development of the blastula, the notochord and the three germ layers. Only in 1839, Theodor Schwann proposed that animals, just like plants, were composed of cells\sjcitep[,]{schwannMikroskopischeUntersuchungenUber1839} and Rudolf Virchow and Robert Remak later added that cells were the product of division of pre-existing cells\sjcitep[.]{rudolfCellularpathologieIhrerBegrundung1858} While studying this process of division in 1879, Walther Flemming first used the term "chromatin" to describe the filament-like threads between two dividing cells in 1879\sjcitep[.]{flemmingBeitrageZurKenntniss1879} Flemming's observations did not differ much from his colleague's Wilhelm Waldeyer's description of "chromosomes"\sjcitep[.]{deichmannChromatinItsHistory2015} These early findings directly stemmed from the technological refinement of microscopic lenses, which had by now become accurate enough to permit detailed studies of developing embryos. This early area of cell biology produced a number of fundamental questions that would engage the minds of biologists to this day:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
	\item How can a single fertilised egg cell divide and self-organise into an entire organism?
	\item Where is the information that steers this process encoded, and how is it interpreted?
	\item How can this process go wrong, leading to disease, and can we manipulate it? \footnotetextwithoutmarker{\sjcitep[]{tagarelliHansSpemannOne2004}A. Tagarelli, et al. At that time, the concept of physical store and transfer of genetic information was not yet widely accepted, and the function and identity of Flemming and Waldeyer's chromosomes was still unknown. In search of answers, marine embryologist Thomas Hunt Morgan began studying inheritance in \textit{Drosophila melanogaster} (fruit fly) in 1908. After years of breeding flies, a mutant with white eyes (aptly named \textit{white}) appeared. Through careful tracking, Morgan discovered that these pigmentless eyes followed a sex-dependent inheritance pattern which could be reliably predicted across generations\sjcitep[.]{morganSexLimitedInheritance1910} More than half a century earlier, Moravian friar Gregor Mendel had demonstrated the exact same principles in \textit{Pisum sativum} (sweet pea), but his work was initially ignored and later disputed by the scientific community upon its rediscovery in 1900. By 1909 however, Danish botanist Wilhelm Johannsen had experimentally repeated much of Mendel's work, and coined the term "gene" to describe the abstract "unit of heredity" that is used to pass on traits between generations\sjcitep[.]{johannsen1909erblichkeitslehre}

Morgan, together with his undergraduate student Alfred Sturtevan, discovered that co-inheritance of genes also followed reproducible patterns ("linkage analysis")\sjcitep[.]{sturtevant1913} This finding supported the idea that genes were located on a linear carrier. A second breakthrough came when Morgan demonstrated that the chromosomes observed by Flemming and Waldeyer were in fact the physical bodies responsible for the transfer of phenotypic traits\sjcitep[.]{morganRandomSegregationCoupling1911} Armed with experimental evidence gathered from thousands of generations of fruit flies, Morgan formally unified the theory of Mendelian inheritance with the physical transfer of genes on chromosomes in 1915\sjcitep[.]{morganMechanismMendelianHeredity1915}

In parallel, the field of embryology began to consolidate. Early views such as "preformationism", the idea that all parts of the body were present in miniature form in the embryo at conception, were defeated when Hans Spemann began constricting dividing \textit{Triturus} (newt) embryos. Both halves of the embryo could develop into full, though smaller, animals \textit{if} part of the dorsal part were present. Later, Spemann and his student Hilde Mangold, discovered that transplantation of the dorsal lip from one embryo to the ventral side of another embryo would induce the formation of a ventrally conjoined twin (\autoref{fig:spemannmangold})\sjcitep[.]{spemannUberInduktionEmbryonenanlagen1924} Importantly, the pair showed that the conjoined secondary embryo consisted of tissue from the \textit{host}, indicating that the transplant merely instructed its host surroundings to change developmental fate. Spemann and Mangold later named this steering tissue the "organizer field", and multiple labs attempted (unsuccessfully) to purify and characterise the compounds responsible for these processes in an "organizer gold rush"\sjcitep[.]{slackConradHalWaddington2002}

In fact, the storage of genetic information was attributed to proteins at the time, as they made up a much larger portion of the chromosomal dry mass than nucleic acids (DNA, RNA), and were known to be more complex in composition. Phoebus Levene, who had identified adenine, guanine, thymine, cytosine, deoxyribose-phosphate backbone as the basic components of DNA\sjcitep[,]{levene1919structure} 
Nucleic acids were thought to be only of structural importance to chromosomes, akin to the highly repetitive polymers found in cell walls and plant fibers. This notion was overturned in 1944, when Avery, MacLeod and McCarty demonstrated that it was transfer of acids, and not proteins, purified from a non-virulent strain of bacteria that could "transform" a virulent  strain to the non-virulent strain\sjcitep[.]{griffithSignificancePneumococcalTypes1928,averyStudiesChemicalNature1944} The next major breakthrough came when Watson, Crick and Franklin resolved DNA's double-helix structure using X-ray crystallography\sjcitep[,]{watsonMolecularStructureNucleic1953} and for the first time suggested that \textit{"the precise sequence of the bases is the code which carries the genetical information"}\sjcitep[.]{,watsonGeneticalImplicationsStructure1953} It had thus become clear that life stores information in the nucleotide sequence of DNA, and the scientific community began to prepare experiments that would leverage this concept.

\section{The early molecular era}
Although Morgan and Waddington's visions of genes as decision makers for a cell's behaviour were largely theoretical, they were soon substantiated. Jacques Monod, also a former student of Thomas Hunt Morgan, observed that bacteria grown in a medium containing both glucose and lactose would preferentially feed on glucose, and would only begin feeding on lactose once the other had run out. Further research together with François Jacob showed that the production of \textbeta-galactosidase, the enzyme responsible for this metabolic switch, was under the control of a "repressor", which was inactivated by the presence of lactose. Together, the pair published these findings in 1961\sjcitep[,]{jacobGeneticRegulatoryMechanisms1961} in an article which features the first diagram of a \textit{gene regulatory network} (\autoref{fig:lac_repressor}). The Jacob-Monod diagram also explicitly showed the repressor as a protein originating from a gene. Genes thus (indirectly) controlled other genes, and therefore the behaviour of the cell - exactly how Conrad Waddington had prophesised twenty years prior.

Earlier that month, Jacob, together with Sidney Brenner, had also proposed the existence of an unstable form of RNA which acted as a "messenger" to transfer information from DNA-based genes to proteins\sjcitep[.]{brennerUnstableIntermediateCarrying1961} This messenger RNA was already clearly depicted as the intermediate carrier of information between genes and proteins in Jacob \& Monod's gene regulatory network. The exact code through which the sequence of mRNA translates to proteins was deciphered soon after using clever "forward" experiments: Marshall Nirenberg, in the lab of Philip Leder, fed synthetic RNA homopolymer repeats (e.g. UUU\textsubscript{n}, AAA\textsubscript{n}, ...) to cell-free protein synthesis systems in which one of the 20 possible amino-acids were radioactively labeled. The detection of a radioactive protein product then revealed the identity of the amino-acid for which the input homopolymer coded\sjcitep[.]{nirenbergRNACodewordsProtein1964} Har Gobind Khorana combined more complex RNA trinucleotide repeat polymers (e.g. ACA\textsubscript{n}, UAG\textsubscript{n}, ...) with chemical analysis of the resulting proteins to decipher the remaining possible 3-nucleotide codons, producing a finalised code cipher in 1966\sjcitep[.]{khoranaPolynucleotideSynthesisGenetic1966}

Biology was shifting from mutant models to molecular methods. Precise extraction and purification of specific proteins or other macromolecules began to take a center stage. [[later on: quantification]] For example, in 1966 Gilbert and M{\"u}ller-Hill for the first time purified \textit{lac} repressor protein fractions, confirming Jacob and Monod's theoretical model of a proteins acting as repressors[.]{gilbert1966} A year later, in a letter aptly titled \textit{The Lac Operator is DNA}, the pair showed that the the \textit{lac} repressor specifically binds to DNA extracted from \textlambda80 phage carrying the \textit{lac} operon, and that this interaction could be disrupted by exposing the complex to IPTG. 

While codons had been de-coded, a "reverse" experiment in which the sequence of a given DNA or RNA molecule could be sequenced remained elusive. The lab of Walter Fiers (Ghent University) was the first to do so in 1972 by reporting a complete sequence of the bacteriophage MS2 coat protein gene\sjcitep[.]{jouNucleotideSequenceGene1972} Fiers first digested the phage's \textsuperscript{32}P-labeled RNA genome using ribonuclease T\textsubscript{1}, which cuts RNA at G positions, and iteratively separated the resulting fragments based on size and charge through multiple rounds of two-dimensional fractionation. Joël Vandekerckhove and Marc Van Montagu (both also Ghent University) had simultaneously been working on determining the amino-acid sequence of the MS2 coat protein\sjcitep[.]{vandekerckhoveUseFluorescamineSequence1973} Under the guidance of Van Montagu's preliminary amino-acid map and the Nirenberg-Khorana codon dictionary, Fiers and colleagues were able to puzzle together their fragments into a near-complete base sequence of the MS2 coat protein gene.

Finally, in 1973,  Gilbert \& Maxam sequenced the \textit{lac} operon, confirming Jacob \& Monod's model of gene regulation\sjcitep[.]{gilbert1973nucleotide} Restriction enzymes with known cut sites were used to digest a purified RNA copy of the operon, and the fragments were separated on a two-dimensional PAGE developed by none other than Fiers and De Wachter\sjcitep{dewachter1972}. Fragment lengths generated by different restriction enzymes could be used to infer the 27 basepair-long sequence: \textit{5'--TGG\textbf{AATTGT}G\textbf{A}G\textbf{C}G\textbf{G}A\textbf{T}A\textbf{ACAATT} 3-3'}. Gilbert and Maxam remarked that the palindromic symmetry in this short motif could imply that the sequence is bound by a dimer or tetramer of lac repressor proteins.

In 1977, Maxam and Gilbert published a DNA sequencing method \sjcitep[,]{maxamGilbert1977} which relied on comparing the 4 electrophoresis ladders generated by chemically cleaving the DNA of interest at G, C, G+A or C+T sites. Soon after, Fredrick Sanger, who had developed the two-dimensional fractionation used by Fiers\sjcitep[,]{sangerTwodimensionalFractionationProcedure1965} and had been awarded the Nobel prize for the amino-acid sequence of insulin in 1958, succeeded in deterministically sequencing the entire DNA genome of bacteriophage \textphi X174 in 1977\sjcitep[.]{sangerDNASequencingChainterminating1977} Earlier work had shown that dideoxy variants of the four common nucleotides (A, C, G \& T) would inhibit further polymerisation when included into the newly synthesised DNA strand due to the absence of a reactive phosphate. Sanger devised that a polymerisation reaction performed in a mixture of the four common nucleotides combined with a single dideoxy variant will produce a mixture of strands terminated at each position of the dideoxy variant. Sanger further reasoned that performing this reaction for all four dideoxy variants in parallel should yield all the positions of each of the four bases (\autoref{fig:sangersequencing}). Indeed, when applied to fragments of \textphi X174 DNA, the entire phage's genome (5386 basepairs) could be reconstructed. While the function of chromatin's DNA component as the primary store of information had become clear, the relationship between its protein content and nucleic acids was still under dispute. Alfred and Ellen Stedman investigated different histone fractions between different species and tissues, and proposed that \textit{"[...] the basic proteins of cell nuclei are gene inhibitors, each histone or protamin being capable of suppressing the activities of specific groups of genes"}\sjcitep[.]{stedman1951} This idea was confirmed experimentally when purified protein-free DNA reconstituted with chromatin-purified histone proteins was shown to be inactive in RNA synthesis despite presence of RNA polymerase\sjcitep[.]{huang1962} Further study of this inhibitory capacity found that "that relatively minor modifications of histone structure [acetylation], taking place on the intact protein molecule, offer a means of switching on or off RNA synthesis at different loci along the chromosome" \sjcitep[.]{allfrey1964}

By the early 1970s, biochemists had chemically identified five different histone proteins (H1, H2A, H2B, H3, H4), present at ratios of 0.5:1:1:1:1. Histones H2A and H2B were found to be rich in lysine, and H3 in arginine, positively charged amino acids known to bind DNA through electrostatic attraction\sjcitep[.]{delange1972} X-ray diffraction research suggested a DNA "super-helix" structure stabilized by histone proteins\sjcitep[,]{pardon1972} while others found "thin fibrils" and "nodular elements"\sjcitep[.]{slayter1972} It was however known that staphylococcal nuclease would cleave rat liver DNA into fragments of multiples of 200 base pairs in length, heavily hinting that DNA and histone proteins were organised in discrete units\sjcitep[.]{hewish1973} This hypothesis was strengthened by early electron microscopy, which directly visualised chromatin fibers as linear arrays of particles \textasciitilde{\SI{70}{ansgtrom}} in diameter resembling "beads on a string"\sjcitep[.]{olins1974spheroid}

Finally, in 1974, Kornberg and Thomas showed that histone proteins H2A and H2B form dimers, and H3 and H4 formed stable tetramers\sjcitep[.]{kornbergChromatinStructureOligomers1974} When mixed with DNA, these dimers and tetramers returned X-ray diffraction spectra identical to native chromatin. Later research with enhanced resolution X-ray diffraction clearly showed a repeating pattern with with a periodicity of \textasciitilde{\SI{100}{ansgtrom}}\sjcitep[.]{kornbergchromatinstructurerepeating1974a} Kornberg remarked that \textit{"the occurrence of multiples rather than just 200 base pair pieces is presumably due to some cleavage sites being blocked by nonhistone proteins"} - a prescient vision of transcription factors. Histone protein H1 was later identified as a stabilizer, binding to 20-80 bp long DNA links between histone octamers, leading to supercoiling and further chromatin compaction\sjcitep[.]{finch1976} This new "nucleosome" model of chromatin compacts DNA \textasciitilde{7-fold} at the nucleosome level, and further supercoiling explains how mitotic chromosomes achieve a 10 000-fold compaction.

\section{Further lifting the veil}
In hindsight, several pieces of the gene regulatory puzzle had now been discovered:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
	\item Genetic information is stored in the base sequence of a cell's DNA.
	\item This information is transferred to proteins according to a well-defined codon "dictionary" through messenger RNA.
	\item Some proteins, such as the lac repressor, are capable of influencing transcription and thus the behaviour of the cell.
\end{enumerate}

However, mechanistic and molecular insights in \textit{how} genes might be regulated was still sparse. Several breakthroughs came from the lab of Robert Roeder. Roeder, who as a student had purified the polymerase enzymes that catalyze the transcription of messenger RNA from DNA in 1969\sjcitep[,]{roederMultipleFormsDNAdependent1969} showed that purified RNA polymerases could only initiate the transcription of DNA templates in the presence of uncharacterised cellular protein extracts. This finding confirmed earlier ideas that additional, then-unknown factors were necessary to initiate the transcription reaction\sjcitep[.]{ngTranscriptionClonedXenopus1979} Several such initiation factors were purified and characterised within the next year\sjcitep[,]{matsuiMultipleFactorsAre1980} and it soon transpired that a large number of different factors are necessary for transcription to occur.

Parallel research in Roeder's lab led to the discovery of a protein which induced expression of the 5S ribosomal RNA gene in \textit{Xenopus} (clawed frog) egg cells. This factor, later named Transcription Factor III (\textit{TFIIIa}), was the first protein shown to initiate transcription of a \textit{specific} gene in eukaryotes, and not just initiate transcription in general. Roeder and his student Takashi Matsui suggested that \textit{TFIIIa} might \textit{"recognize a unique region of DNA"}, a hypothesis which was explored and demonstrated soon after\sjcitep[.]{engelkeSpecificInteractionPurified1980} However, as part of the ribosomal body, 5S rRNA plays a role in cellular protein synthesis, and is ubiquitously present in all cells across all known organisms. Transcription factors involved in more specialised processes were identified and characterised relatively quickly: examples include the glucocorticoid receptor \textit{Gr}\sjcitep[,]{payvarPurifiedGlucocorticoidReceptors1981} \textit{Sp1}\sjcitep[,]{dynanIsolationTranscriptionFactors1983} and \textit{Gal4}\sjcitep[,]{bramSpecificProteinBinding1985} important transcription factors which are still under scrutiny to this day. In fact, it became clear that some of these factors were extremely powerful: their ectopic expression could convert one cell type to another\sjcitep[,]{davisExpressionSingleTransfected1987} and later specific combinations were found to even revert fully differentiated skin cells back to stem cells\sjcitep[.]{takahashiInductionPluripotentStem2006} By this time, the scientific realised that the factors responsible for the activity of Spemann \& Mangold's organizer were transcription factors, and several of these factors were identified shortly after\sjcitep[.]{choMolecularNatureSpemann1991, lambNeuralInductionSecreted1993}

The human genome project started in 1985 as a joint academic effort between the US National Institutes of Health. Surprisingly, the US Department of Energy also played a major role, with Lawrence Livermore, Los Alamos and Lawrence Berkeley labs providing extensive support in computational analysis and data storage. The ties between DNA, cancer, radiation damage and the immune system were also known already. The project was first expected to take 15 years and cost ~\$3 billion dollars. Early efforts focused on a hierarchical approach: the genome was broken into fragments (~200kbp) and cloned into bacterial artificial chromosomes (BACs). These BACs were then linked into large contigs using a variety of techniques (mainly sequence tagging sites PCR and restriction enzyme fingerprinting), which were in turn mapped to entire chromosomes. Individual BAC clones were then further fragmeted into smaller pieces (1000bp) which could be sequenced via the Sanger method. A number of problems made this approach a slow process: repetitive DNA regions and coverage gaps and made the assembly of contigs difficult, and Sanger's dideoxy method was incredibly labour intensive. Luckily, automation soon followed. One company played a particularly important role: ABI, first with the ABI Prism 373 and 377 automated slab gel electrophoresis analyzers, then with the ABI Prism 310 capillary gel electrophoresis, and finally the ABI 3700 96-plex capillary gel electrophoresis analyzer (1998), which became the workhorse of the human genome project. Each incorporation cycle is imaged, generating imaging data of the oligo lawn. Image processing is used to determine the position of oligo clusters present on the lawn, and these are stored in \verb|.locs| (locations) files. The basecalling algorithm will combine these \verb|.locs| files together with the imaging data to extract fluorescence intensities for all colours used per cluster. Dominant fluorescent signals then determine the base called for each cluster for every cycle.  When no base can be called, \verb|N| is used. When a strand does not incorporate a base during a given cycle, or if it leads ahead by one base, it becomes out of phase with the other strands of that cluster for the rest of the sequencing run. This is called phasing/pre-phasing and is corrected by the basecalling algorithm. Theoretical phasing rates (approximately 0.1\% of strands in a given cluster per cycle) can be used to estimate the contribution of phasing noise and deconvolve true signal from phased noise. Each basecall is also assigned a "Phred" quality score \verb|Q| based on signal-to-noise ratio, phasing, and separation of dense clusters: \( Q = -10 \cdot \log_{10} (P) \) where \verb|P| is the probability of the basecall being correct. A Q score of 30 corresponds to a probability of error of 1 in 1000.  The result is a binary \verb|.bcl| file, which contains the basecalls for each cluster. The binary \verb|.bcl| file is then converted to the ASCII \verb|.fastq| format using \verb|bcl2fastq|.

Each sequenced strand is represented by four lines in the \verb|.fastq| file. The first line is a header, which stores metadata such as sequencer and cluster coordinates. The second line is the sequence itself, followed by a separator (\verb|+|) on the third line, and the ASCII-encoded quality score on the fourth line.

Once data is sequenced, and raw \verb|.fastq| data is available, the first step in data analysis is alignment to a reference genome. A number of algorithms exist for this task, and the optimal choice depends on several factors. Short-read aligners such as \verb|bowtie2| are optimized for reads < \SI{200}{\bp}, while long-read aligners like \verb|minimap2| are better at handling reads > \SI{1000}{\bp}. The type of sequencing technology used also plays a role: sequencing by synthesis (Illumina) generates more indels due to incorporation errors, which are explicitly corrected for by \verb|bwa-mem|. \verb|minimap2| is better suited for handling the higher single-base error rates found in Nanopore/PacBio reads. When sequencing transcriptomic data, RNA-seq specific aligners like \verb|STAR| and \verb|HISAT2| are used to handle spliced reads. Like many classes of algorithms, a trade-off between memory and compute can also be made, for example \verb|STAR| requires large amounts of memory due to its use of an uncompressed genome index, but is faster, while \verb|HISAT2| is more memory-efficient, but takes longer.

The diversity in sequencing technologies and alignment algorithms led to the use of several independent alignment formats, each tied to specific sequencing technologies or alignment algorithms. Analyses on different datasets (e.g. variant calling, genotype analysis) required cross parsing or conversion. The introduction of the Sequence Alignment Map (\verb|SAM|) format as part of the 1000 Genomes Project in 2009\sjcitep[,]{liSequenceAlignmentMapFormat2009} provided a unified standard for storing aligned sequencing data which has since become the de facto standard. Each read is stored in a single line containing 11 mandatory fields including: read name, reference name (chromosome or contig), position, mapping quality, the actual sequence, several other alignment quality parameters as well as optional tags.

\section{From human genome to sequencing assays}
Before the availability of next-generation sequencing technology, studying the genome and gene expression at scale relied heavily on microarrays. Microarrays were, and still are, used to quantify RNA transcripts\sjcitep[,]{schenaQuantitativeMonitoringGene1995} genotype samples using SNP arrays\sjcitep[,]{wang1998large} and even measure DNA-protein interactions using ChIP-on-chip\sjcitep[.]{ren2000genome} While microarrays allow for up to ~\num{50000} probes to be interrogated in a single experiment, limited dynamic range, high false-positive hybridisation and heterogeneous probe hybridisation capacities skew absolute quantification. The probe set also requires intelligent design and thus cannot discover novel sequences. Probeset size is particularly troubling for ChIP-on-Chip, since genome binning limits resolution to around 500-1000 bp - too low for novel TF motif discovery. Nonetheless, large scale, distributed application of microarray technologies culminated in a first draft of the Encyclopedia of DNA Elements (ENCODE)in 2007\sjcitep[,]{ENCODE_Project_Consortium2007-zd} a careful annotation of 30 MBs - around 1\% - of the human genome. The release of this draft was the last project of such scale that did not use next generation sequencing.

The newly available human genome enabled the development of new bioinformatics tools. For example, gene-proximal (e.g. promoters) and distal (identified through comparative genomics or CpG enrichment) regulatory regions could be scanned for enrichment of known TF binding motifs sourced from databases such as TRANSFAC\sjcitep[]{wingender2000transfac, matys2006transfac}, or even parsed for de novo motif identification\sjcitep[.]{thijs2002gibbs} Significantly enriched motifs could in turn be used to reconstruct gene regulatory network candidates\sjcitep[.]{aerts2003toucan,aerts2005toucan} ready for reporter assay testing. In this new era of abundant genomic data, aggregation and statistical integration of novel tools and databases proved to be an effective strategy for investigating biological phenomena. For example, \verb|endeavour|\sjcitep[]{aertsGenePrioritization2006} aggregated data from 8 independent databases (expression data\sjcitep[,]{barrett2005ncbi} GO terms\sjcitep[,]{ashburner2000gene} regulatory elements\sjcitep[,]{matys2006transfac} ...) to rank gene associations with a training set (e.g. genes already known to be associated with a certain disease). This tool allowed researchers to prioritized gene candidates for lab validation.

By the mid 2000s, the availability of the human genome as a public resource and the falling cost of NGS led to the development of a new class of quantitative assays: specific fractions of nucleic acids extracted from tissues could now be sequenced, aligned to the reference genome, and counted. One of the first such assays was histone methylation ChIP-seq, first published in 2007\sjcitep[.]{barskiHighresolutionProfilingHistone2007} By sequencing immunoprecipitated DNA directly rather than using microarrays, methylation could now be assessed genome-wide and with fewer quantitative biases. Performing this technique for a spectrum of 20 different lys/arg histone methylation ChIP-seq variants on CD4\textsuperscript{+} T cells, and correlating the annotations with GNF SymAtlas gene expression microarray data allowed genome-wide classification and discovery of various classes of functional elements. The distribution of methylation signatures could now be systematically correlated to phenotypes, helping understand disease and developmtn. For example, a comparison of histone methylation in mouse embryonic stem cells and embryonic fibroblasts revealed transitory methylation changes in the de-differentiation of ES\sjcitep[.]{mikkelsen2007genome}

In parallel research, ChIP-seq was also applied to individual transcription factors at a genomic resolution of around 50 base pairs, a welcome improvement compared to microarrays. For example, application of ChIP-seq on neuron-restrictive silencer factor (NRSF) in Jurkat T-cells showed that this factor can also bind "weakly" to altered, non-canonical motifs dependent on local chromatin state (methylation, accessibility) and co-factor motif context\sjcitep[.]{johnsongenomewidemappingvivo2007} These findings contradicted earlier ideas of stringent binding to only canonical motifs, confirmed by SELEX and microarray studies, a rejection made possible only through the broadness of NGS. NGS also improved dynamic range compared to microarrays, showing for example that STAT1 motif enrichment was strongly correlated to absolute ChIP-seq peak height\sjcitep[.]{robertsonGenomewideProfilesSTAT12007}

The increased genomic resolution of ChIP-seq was rapidly refining models of gene regulation, but the technique still required a strong antibody and prior knowledge of the protein or histone modification of interest. In contrast, DNase I hypersensitivity (DHS) assays survey chromatin accessibility genome-wide. The technique had already been applied for genome-wide accessibility studies, first by southern blot\sjcitep[,]{wu1980heat} then capillary Sanger sequencing\sjcitep[,]{crawford2004identifying} and later microarrays\sjcitep[.]{crawford2006dnase}. Like ChIP, the technique was soon translated to NGS\sjcitep[,]{hesselberth2009global}, rapidly contributing to even higher resolution (1 basepair) understanding of gene gene regulation. For example, matched DNase-seq and glucocorticoid receptor (GR) ChIP-seq revealed that out of \num{8236} active GR binding sites in murine mammary adenocarcinoma, 95\% were already accessible \textit{prior to} receptor activation\sjcitep[.]{john2011chromatin} These findings rejected prior ideas that GR might act as a pioneer factor, remodeling inaccessible chromatin and exposing its preferred motif after receptor activation. Moreover, pituitary gland cells shared only 11.4\% of the binding sites accessible in adenocarcinoma, suggesting that differential chromatin context underlies differential responses to the same transcription factor. Unsurprisingly, ENCODE's second draft included DNAse-seq data from 125 tissues, along with H3K4me3 and TF ChIP-seq and bisulfite sequencing\sjcitep[.]{thurman2012accessible,encode2012} In total, 2.9 million DNase I hypersensitivity sites were identified and annotated genome-wide, covering almost all previously known regulatory element candidates: 94.4\% of previously known TF ChIP-seq regions were found to overlap with these DNase I sites, and only \num{3692} such sites were found in all cell types assessed, confirming that chromatin accessibility and downstream gene regulation are highly tissue and cell-type specific processes. Combination of these new regulatory readouts with GWAS further confirmed that many disease-related SNPs are located in regulatory regions, accelerating therapeutic target discovery.

By 2008 development of NGS-driven RNA-seq, and subsequent displacement of microarrays in transcriptomics began in 2008, first in yeast\sjcitep[,]{nagalakshmi2008transcriptional} and later in mouse\sjcitep[,]{mortazavi2008mapping} and human\sjcitep[.]{wang2008alternative}. \section{Novel computational tools}
Idea: wealth of information leads to explosion in tools available

The new wealth of RNA-seq data posed a number of challenges. Whereas ChIP-seq data can be directly aligned to the genome, mRNA transcripts overlap discontinuously with the genome due to splicing, and are thus more challenging to align. Aligning short-read mRNA-seq data to the genome required splice site annotations, which required Expressed Sequence Tags (ESTs) or full-length cDNA sequencing to generate. TopHat disrupted the need for this process by introducing computational splice site identification\sjcitep[.]{trapnell2009tophat} The algorithm identifies exons by looking for splice site motifs near the flanks of the reads that only partially map ("initially unmapped") to the reference genome with \verb|bowtie|. Multiple reads are then aggregated to statistically validate a splice site. The resulting read alignments can then be passed to Cufflinks\sjcitep[,]{trapnellTranscriptAssemblyQuantification2010} which assembles them into possible transcript isoforms and can provide a statistical estimate of their relative quantities without long-read cDNA sequencing data.

iRegulon\sjcitep[]{janky2014iregulon} provides a computational alternative for identifying master regulators when experimental TF binding data is unavailable. First, a genome-wide assessment of motif enrichment near TSSs is generated by scoring occurrence of \num{10000} motif PWMs aggregated from various databases using ClusterBuster\sjcitep[]{frith2003cluster}, generating for each PWM a ranking of the genes it may regulate. A set of genes, derived from e.g. differential gene expression or co-expression data, can then be checked for overrepresentation in these PWM-gene rankings using an area under the rank curve approach. This returns a set of motifs that may regulate the gene set, which can then be mapped to TFs using ortholjk/ogy or similarity if annotation is absent. Applying this approach to validated gene sets from 115 ENCODE ChIP-seq datasets returned 94 correct TF hits, and was able to identify 307 direct p53 target genes using ENCODE ChIP-seq data.

Since near-whole transcripts were sequenced, the group also discovered \num{1753} novel splice junctions, and demonstrated for the first time that different isoforms of the same gene can occur in the same single cell.

\section{Screening}
Large screens that investigate the effect of silencing specific genes using RNA interference emerged in the early 2000s\sjcitep[.]{berns2004large,boutros2006analysis} Such perturbation screens follow either an arrayed design, where perturbations are individually plated and tracked in physical space, or a pooled design, where cell lines are transduced with pooled shRNA vector libraries at a given MOI, and survivors are sequenced.

When CRISPR was developed as a molecular tool for rapid and accurate gene editing in 2013\sjcitep[,]{ran2013genome} full genetic knockouts became widely available, producing a pooled genome-wide screen involving 64000 sgRNAs for 18000 gene knockouts just a year later\sjcitep[.]{shalem2014genome, behan2019prioritization, pacini2021integrated} In addition to genetic knockouts, specific genes can also be up- or downregulated using catalytically dead Cas9 fused to transcription activators such as VP64 or p65, or repressors such as KRAB. Both arrayed and pooled CRISPR/RNAi screens have drawbacks: arrayed screens require vast amounts of labour and reagents, and pooled CRISPR-screens are limited to very simple readouts such as cell survival or marker expression.

Around 2015, researchers at the Broad institute devised that perturbations could be pooled and read out at the single-cell level, coupling pooled screens with scRNA-seq readings\sjcitep[.]{dixit2016perturbseq} In their method, named Perturb-seq, each vector is designed to express two transcripts after integrating in the host genome: a first transcript containing the sgRNA, driven by U6, and a second transcript containing a puromycin selection cassette, BFP and a 20 nucleotide long guide barcode (GBC), driven by EF1\textalpha. The GBC transcript is polyadenylated, allowing it to be captured and counted through standard 3' scRNA-seq protocols (10x GEX in this specific case). The \{sgRNA:GBC\} dictionary can then be used to identify the genetic edit present in each captured cell \sjcitep[.]{dixit2016perturbseq} This pooled approach allows rapid parallelisation of perturbation screens with limited labour. Using this system, researchers observed that even within the same perturbation, individual cells can exhibit different responses. In particular, knockdown of the key ER chaperone BiP (HSPA5) caused two possible responses: some cells preferentially activated PERK, while others activated IRE1\textalpha.

Instead of guide barcodes expressed on the selection cassette, a poly-adenylated gRNA which is directly captured by 3' scRNA-seq can also be used\sjcitep[.]{datlinger2017pooled}. In this approach's debut, Drop-seq was also used instead of 10x, showing that the open-source method has sufficient sensitivity to detect the gRNA transcripts. Perturb-seq's costs can be further scaled down by multiplexing perturbations or cells: if gene regulatory networks are assumed sparse in number of participants, then the differential expression of a cell responding to multiple perturbations (high MOI), or a droplet containing multiple single-perturbation cells, can be considered a linear combination of the effect of its components. Pooling cells or perturbations in this manner and demultiplexing the individual terms using PCA and LASSO [double check] can reduce cost ten-fold and quantify interaction terms between different perturbations\sjcitep[.]{yao2024scalable}

Perturbation screens have also been coupled with scATAC-seq readouts. In the earliest protocol, Perturb-ATAC\sjcitep[,]{rubin2019coupled} cells were transduced in pools with Perturb-seq-like vectors. Single cells were then captured and tagmented on the Fluidigm C1, limiting the size of the study to \~4300 cells total. Here, the GBCs are reverse-transcribed, allowing them to be amplified and captured through the Fluidigm scATAC-seq protocol, generating perturbation-coupled scATAC-seq profiles. Dual knockdown of EBF1 and EZH2 (in B lympholbasts), and KLF4 and ZNF750 (in keratinocytes), showed stronger repression than individual perturbations, and this relationship is cell-type specific.

Spear-ATAC\sjcitep[]{pierce2021high} is able to overcome Perturb-ATAC's throughput limitations by using droplet encapsulation (10x Chromium). After pooled lentiviral transduction, cells undergo the regular 10x scATAC-seq workflow, but with an additional primer added to the droplet encapsulation step. This primer anneals to the integrated lentiviral cassette, amplifying its barcode. By using droplet-based methods, 414 sgRNA knockdowns could be profiled across 105k cells (sampled between 3 and 21 days post transduction). Analyzing the resulting time series revealed time-dependent effects of the knockdowns. For example, GATA1 knockdown was found to first upregulate STAT5 motif accessibility, but later RUNX motifs. As expected, these GRNs were also observed to be cell-type specific between K562, MCF-7 and GM12878 cells.

One disadvantage of the screening methods above is that most of the vectors described do not contain Cas9, limiting the studies to transgenic and immortal cell lines. Most perturbation studies so far also deal with CRISPRa/i, CRISPR knockouts, but not yet with synthetic gene expression knock-ins. Such studies are considerably more difficult to implement in a pooled manner, since transcript length has an effect on transfection, packaging and transduction efficiency, which bias MOI. These effects call for the titration of viral concentrations and arrayed screening, which is expensive and laborious.

\section{Multiplexing}
Sample multiplexing for droplet microfluidics has also been achieved through other means. The main strategy is to pre-index or "hash" multiple samples with oligonucleotide barcodes, co-process them through droplet generation together, and use the sample hash to computationally separate cells into sample bins post hoc. A number of different technical approaches to do this exist. Cells or nuclei can be tagged by attaching sample-barcoded oligonucleotides to their membranes, either by antibody-antigen binding\sjcitep[]{stoeckiusCellHashing2018} or lipid/cholesterol embedding\sjcitep[.]{mcginnisMultiSeqSample2019} Within each droplet, the membrane-bound oligonucleotides co-captured together with the associated cell's mRNA or tagmented genome. Antibodies have demonstrated slightly better accuracy, while lipid hashing can be used when high-strength antibodies are not available\sjcitep[.]{mylkaComparativeAnalysisAntibody2022} The increased precision of antibody-based methods could be attributed to the many successive washes involved, but which may also reduce cell viability and integrity.

Another sample multiplexing strategy is scifi-RNA-seq, where cells or nuclei are distributed over 96-well plates and pre-indexed by reverse transcription with a barcoded primer.
