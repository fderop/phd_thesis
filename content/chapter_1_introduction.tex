\chapter{The accessible genome at Single-Cell Resolution}
The work performed in this thesis aims to advance a technical aspect (single-cell sequencing) of an important part (chromatin accessibility) of the gene regulatory puzzle. This introductory chapter will attempt to build the foundation for the explorations that follow by providing a historical overview of chromatin accessibility and frame it within the ongoing research on decoding gene regulation. Then, we frame the state of the art of single-cell chromatin sequencing technologies when I began this work, and delve into the capabilities and limitations of several breakthrough techniques. This context serves to highlight the importance of the subject matter, and to motivate our subsequent discussions and findings.

After several years without results, Morgan observed that some traits, such as the flies' eye colours, could be predictably inherited\sjcitep[.]{morganRandomSegregationCoupling1911} More than half a century earlier, Moravian friar Gregor Mendel had demonstrated the exact same principles using \textit{Pisum sativum} (the common sweet pea), but his work was lost and disputed upon rediscovery. Using his new findings, Morgan verified Mendel's work and formally defined the theory of Mendelian inheritance in 1915\sjcitep[.]{morganMechanismMendelianHeredity1915} A second breakthrough came when Morgan demonstrated that the chromosomes first observed by Flemming and Waldeyer were in fact the physical bodies responsible for the transfer of phenotypic traits, confirming earlier ideas that chromosomes carried genes which contained the genetic information that encoded heritable traits. Together, Morgan's findings inaugurated the 20\textsuperscript{th} century's era of modern molecular genetics and set the stage for many of the major discoveries that ensued. However, until this point, a handful of fundamental questions about the nature of cells and life in general had kept the minds of biologists occupied, but remained completely unanswered:

One of the central, physical entities that underlie gene regulation is chromatin and all its properties. The term "chromatin" was first used by German cytologist Walther Flemming in 1879, who simply wanted to describe the filament-like threads which he had stained using aniline dyes while observing cell division. Chromatin is a complex of DNA and proteins found within the nuclei of eukaryotic cells. Its primary function is to package the long DNA molecules into a more compact, denser shape. This is critical as it allows the DNA to fit into the nucleus and be efficiently organized and regulated.

Chromatin is composed of repeating units called nucleosomes. Each nucleosome consists of a segment of DNA wound around eight histone proteins (a core of two copies each of histone proteins H2A, H2B, H3, and H4). The DNA segment connected to this core is referred to as "linker DNA". Another histone protein, H1, binds to the linker DNA, assisting in the compacting of the chromatin structure.

Chromatin can exist in two forms: euchromatin and heterochromatin. Euchromatin is less compact and is associated with regions of the genome that are actively transcribed into RNA. On the other hand, heterochromatin is more densely packed and is typically associated with regions of the genome that are not being transcribed.

The structure of chromatin is dynamic and can change. This flexibility plays a crucial role in gene expression because it can regulate the access of the transcription machinery to the DNA. When the chromatin is open and the DNA is accessible (a state often associated with euchromatin), transcription can take place. When the chromatin is condensed and the DNA is less accessible (a state often associated with heterochromatin), transcription is less likely to occur.

Thus, the study of chromatin and its structure is fundamental to understanding how genes are regulated within cells.

It is now well established, that the localization of nucleosomes along the length of the DNA can be influenced by a combination of factors, such as sequence features, that repel or attract nucleosomes, transcription factors, the RNA pol II machinery, and chromatin-remodeling enzymes that can evict, slide, or remodel nucleosomes (Chapter 4) (reviewed in [63]). While certain regions tend
to display precise and reproducible nucleosome positioning in a cell population,
others have varying levels of nucleosome positioning up to no positioning
at all [63]. Nucleosome positioning in turn dictates which portion of the
genome is generally more available for transcription factors, which often occupy
binding sites localized within the linker regions [63]. Certain transcription factors,
called pioneer transcription factors, can bind DNA even when it is tightly
wrapped around the nucleosome [63]. Such factors are thus able to remodel,
even compact, repressed chromatin states in collaboration with chromatinremodeling
enzymes, and play important roles during lineage commitment,
differentiation, and epigenetic reprogramming. A term related to, but distinct
from nucleosome positioning is nucleosome occupancy [63]. While most of the
genome is occupied by nucleosomes, certain active regulatory regions, such as
enhancers, promoters, and terminators, show low-nucleosome occupancy or are
nucleosome-free to enable the efficient binding of the transcription machinery
[63]. Nucleosome positioning, spacing, and occupancy thus emerge as important
regulators of cell type–specific gene expression patterns, which can undergo
dynamic changes during differentiation [64]. Taken together, the discovery of the
nucleosome has revolutionized chromatin research by opening up fundamentally
novel questions, such as how nucleosome positioning (Chapter 4), DNA and
histone modifications (Chapters 2–3, 5–6), and the 3D folding of the primary
chromatin fiber (Chapters 7–14, 17) collaborate to affect genomic functions.
