\chapter{The Accessible Genome at Single-Cell Resolution}
This introductory chapter will attempt to build the foundation for the explorations that follow by providing a historical overview of chromatin accessibility based on historical accounts\sjcitep[,]{slackConradHalWaddington2002, olinsChromatinHistoryOur2003, cobbWhoDiscoveredMessenger2015, deichmannChromatinItsHistory2015, deichmannEpigeneticsOriginsEvolution2016, lis50YearHistory2019, roeder50YearsEukaryotic2019} and frame it within the ongoing research on decoding gene regulation. Then, we frame the state of the art of single-cell chromatin sequencing technologies when I began this work, and delve into the capabilities and limitations of several breakthrough techniques. This context serves to highlight the importance of the subject matter, and to motivate our subsequent discussions and findings.

\section{A brief history of cellular genetics}

The word "cell" was first used by Robert Hooke to describe the hexagonal cell walls in a thin slice of cork in the 1600s\sjcitep[,]{hookeMicrographia1665} but it took several more centuries of research until the concept of cells as the foundational building block of life began to take shape. Only in the mid 1800s, Theodor Schwann showed that animals, just like plants, were composed of cells\sjcitep[,]{schwannMikroskopischeUntersuchungenUber1839} and Rudolf Virchow and Robert Remak later added that cells were the product of division of pre-existing cells\sjcitep[.]{rudolfCellularpathologieIhrerBegrundung1858} While studying this process of division in 1879, Walther Flemming first used the term "chromatin" to describe the filament-like threads between two dividing cells in 1879\sjcitep[.]{flemmingBeitrageZurKenntniss1879} Flemming's observations did not differ much from his colleague's Wilhelm Waldeyer's description of "chromosomes"\sjcitep[.]{deichmannChromatinItsHistory2015} At the time, the physical transfer of genetic information was not yet widely accepted, and the exact function of these chromosomes, and how genetic information was stored, was still unknown.

Two breakthrough discoveries were made in the lab of Thomas Hunt Morgan, who in 1908 began studying inheritance by irradiating \textit{Drosophila melanogaster} (fruit fly) and cross-breeding the resulting mutants. After several years without results, Morgan observed that the inheritance pattern of some mutant traits, such as the flies' eye colours, could be reliably predicted across generations\sjcitep[.]{morganSexLimitedInheritance1910} More than half a century earlier, Moravian friar Gregor Mendel had demonstrated the exact same principles using \textit{Pisum sativum} (the common sweet pea), but his work was lost and disputed upon rediscovery. Using his new findings, Morgan verified Mendel's work and formally defined the theory of Mendelian inheritance in 1915\sjcitep[.]{morganMechanismMendelianHeredity1915} A second breakthrough came when Morgan demonstrated that chromosomes, first observed by Flemming and Waldeyer, were in fact the physical bodies responsible for the transfer of phenotypic traits\sjcitep[,]{morganRandomSegregationCoupling1911} confirming earlier ideas that chromosomes carried "genes" which encoded heritable traits. Together, Morgan's findings inaugurated the 20\textsuperscript{th} century's era of modern molecular genetics and set the stage for many of the major discoveries that ensued. However, until this point, a handful of fundamental questions about the nature of cells and life in general had kept the minds of biologists occupied, but remained completely unanswered:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
	\item How can a single fertilized egg cell self-organise into an array of thousands of different cell types?
	\item Where is the information that steers this process encoded, and how is it interpreted?
	\item How can this process go wrong, leading to disease?
\end{enumerate}

Several groups were working on these issues in the early 20\textsuperscript{th} century. It had been known that a small portion of the early vertebrate embryo acted as an "organizing center", and "directed" nearby tissues throughout the early development of the embryo, but the exact nature of this mechanism was still completely unknown. In 1924, Hermann Spemann together with his student Hilde Mangold discovered that, when transplanted from one \textit{Triturus} (newt) embryo to another, this "organizer" could induce the formation of a secondary embryo opposite to the other\sjcitep[.]{spemannUberInduktionEmbryonenanlagen1924} Multiple labs raced to purify and characterize the compound responsible for these processes in an "organizer gold rush"\sjcitep[,]{slackConradHalWaddington2002} but without success. We now know that the compounds responsible for this process are growth factors, small molecules capable of steering a cell's activities when present at only nanogram quantities - far below the detection limit of that era's technologies.

At the time, the link between embryonic development and the new insights in genetics was not yet understood, even dismissed. This changed when a young Conrad Waddington, who had previously been performing grafting experiments on organizers in mammals and birds, visited Morgan's fly lab in 1935. Here, he intensely studied \textit{Drosophila}'s embryonic wing development, and discovered that specific heritable mutations could disrupt this process at specific stages\sjcitep[.]{waddingtonGeneticControlWing1940} Based on these findings, Waddington wrote his seminal work \textit{Organisers and Genes}, which introduced the notion that embryonic development was a decision tree under the control of genes, and that these decisions together formed an "epigenetic landscape"\sjcitep[.]{waddingtonOrganisersGenes1940}

It must be noted that the \textit{genes} Waddington addressed in his book were ill-defined, almost virtual units of heredity, as their exact physical composition had not yet been identified in 1940. In fact, the storage of genetic information was still attributed to proteins at the time, as they made up a large portion of the chromosomal dry mass and were deemed far more complex than nucleic acids, which were thought to be only of structural importance. This notion was overturned in 1944, when Avery, MacLeod and McCarty demonstrated that only DNA, and not proteins, purified from a non-virulent, rough (R) strain of bacteria could "transform" a virulent, smooth (S) strain to the rough R strain\sjcitep[.]{griffithSignificancePneumococcalTypes1928,averyStudiesChemicalNature1944} The Avery-MacLeod-McCarty experiment formally cemented DNA as the genetic storage of information, but another decade would pass before the exact molecular mechanism through which this phenomenon occurred was resolved. This breakthrough came when Watson, Crick and Franklin resolved the double-helix structure of DNA using X-ray crystallography (\autoref{fig:watsoncrickfranklin})\sjcitep[,]{watsonMolecularStructureNucleic1953} and for the first time suggested that \textit{"the precise sequence of the bases is the code which carries the genetical information"}\sjcitep[.]{,watsonGeneticalImplicationsStructure1953}

Equipped with the newly discovered identity of genes as well-defined segments of DNA, Waddington extensively elaborated on his concept of the epigenetic landscape in his eighth book, \textit{The Strategy of the Genes}\sjcitep[,]{waddingtonStrategyGenes1957} in 1957. Here, he formulated the landscape as a multidimensional metabolic state space in which a ball, representing a cell, can roll downwards during development, passing several bifurcations which ultimately determine its "fate" (\autoref{fig:waddington_landscape}a). Beneath the landscape, a series of tension ropes, symbolizing genes, forcefully pull the surface into shape (\autoref{fig:waddington_landscape}b).

 Waddington's ideas were largely theoretical. However, a milestone discovery that played right into his visionary ideas was soon made. Jacques Monod, yet another pupil of Thomas Hunt Morgan, had observed that bacteria grown in a medium containing both glucose and lactose would preferentially feed on glucose, and only begin feeding on lactose once glucose had run out. Further experiments together with François Jacob led to the understanding that the production of \textbeta-galactosidase, the enzyme responsible for lactose catabolism, was under the control of a "repressor", which was inactivated in the presence of galactose. Together, they published these findings in 1961\sjcitep[,]{jacobGeneticRegulatoryMechanisms1961} in a review which features the first diagram of a gene regulatory network (\autoref{fig:lac_repressor}). Jacob \& Monod's diagram explicitly showed the repressor as being a protein originating from a gene. Genes thus (indirectly) controlled other genes, and therefore the behaviour of the cell - similarly to how Waddington had imagined.

 Earlier that month, Jacob, together with Sidney Brenner, had proposed the role of an unstable form of RNA as a "messenger" which transferred information from these DNA genes to proteins\sjcitep[.]{brennerUnstableIntermediateCarrying1961} This messenger RNA was also clearly depicted in Jacob \& Monod's gene regulatory network as the intermediate between genes and proteins, and the exact code in which mRNA translates to proteins was deciphered soon after\sjcitep[.]{nirenbergRNACodewordsProtein1964,khoranaPolynucleotideSynthesisGenetic1966}

 At this point in time, several pieces of the regulatory puzzle had been discovered. The close relationship between DNA, mRNA transcripts, and proteins had been established, but the exact molecular mechanism through which one regulated the other remained unknown.

Several breakthroughs in the lab of Robert Roeder shed light on the basic principles underlying eukaryotic gene regulation. As a student, Roeder had previously purified the polymerase enzymes that catalyze the transcription of DNA into messenger RNA in 1969\sjcitep[.]{roederMultipleFormsDNAdependent1969} Later research in Roeder's own lab showed that purified RNA polymerases could only initiate the transcription of DNA templates in the presence of uncharacterized cellular protein extracts, confirming the idea that additional, then-unknown factors were necessary to initiate this process\sjcitep[.]{ngTranscriptionClonedXenopus1979} Several such initiation factors were purified and characterized within the next year\sjcitep[,]{segallMultipleFactorsAre1980} and it soon transpired that a large number of such factors are necessary for transcription to occur.

In parallel research, Roeder's lab had also discovered a protein which induced specific expression of the 5S ribosomal RNA gene in \textit{Xenopus} (clawed frog) egg cells. This factor, later named TFIIIa, was the first protein that was shown to initiate transcription of a \textit{specific} gene in eukaryotes. Roeder and Segall suggested that TFIIIa might "recognize a unique region of DNA", a hypothesis which was explored and demonstrated soon after\sjcitep[.]{engelkeSpecificInteractionPurified1980} However, as part of the ribosomal body, 5S rRNA plays a role in cellular protein synthesis, and is ubiquitously present in all cells across all known organisms. In the following years, transcription factors involved in more specific biological processes were identified and characterised. Examples include the glucocorticoid receptor\sjcitep[,]{payvarPurifiedGlucocorticoidReceptors1981} Sp1\sjcitep[,]{dynanIsolationTranscriptionFactors1983} and Gal4\sjcitep[,]{bramSpecificProteinBinding1985} important transcription factors which are all still under scrutiny to this day. In fact, it became clear that some of these factors were extremely powerful: their ectopic expression could convert one cell type to another\sjcitep[,]{davisExpressionSingleTransfected1987} and later specific combinations were found to even revert fully differentiated skin cells back to a stem cells\sjcitep[.]{takahashiInductionPluripotentStem2006} By this time, the scientific realised the Spemann-Mangold organizer guided early embryonic development by secreting specific program of transcription factors, and several of these factors were identified shortly after\sjcitep[.]{choMolecularNatureSpemann1991, lambNeuralInductionSecreted1993}

We now understand that fine-tuned gene regulation explains how one cell can give rise to many, that this program is embedded in our genomes through a "regulatory code", and that aberrant gene regulation can explain many of our age's most important diseases. Much progress has been made in unraveling the mechanics of gene regulation. We have models of gene transcription and translation, cell signaling, the roles of several classes of proteins, and so on. However, full and deterministic models which can exhaustively answer our core questions remain elusive, almost unimaginable.
