\chapter{\nohyphens{Molecular tools}}
\label{ch:early}
This introductory chapter will attempt to build the foundation for the explorations that follow by providing a historical overview of chromatin accessibility based on historical accounts\sjcitep[,]{slackConradHalWaddington2002, olinsChromatinHistoryOur2003, cobbWhoDiscoveredMessenger2015, deichmannChromatinItsHistory2015, deichmannEpigeneticsOriginsEvolution2016, lis50YearHistory2019, roeder50YearsEukaryotic2019} and frame it within the ongoing research on decoding gene regulation. Then, we illustrate the state of the art of single-cell chromatin sequencing technologies when I began this work, and delve into the capabilities and limitations of several breakthrough techniques. This context serves to highlight the importance of the subject matter, and to motivate our subsequent discussions and findings.

\section{Early research}
The word "cell" was first used in 1665 by Robert Hooke to describe the hexagonal walls he observed in a thin slice of cork through a self-made microscope\sjcitep[.]{hookeMicrographia1665} It took several more centuries of research until the concept of cells as the foundational building blocks of life began to take shape. Between 1817 and 1826, Baltic-German polymath and explorer Karl Ernst von Baer, made the first accounts of egg cells, development of the blastula, the notochord and the three germ layers. Only in 1839, Theodor Schwann proposed that animals, just like plants, were composed of cells\sjcitep[,]{schwannMikroskopischeUntersuchungenUber1839} and Rudolf Virchow and Robert Remak later added that cells were the product of division of pre-existing cells\sjcitep[.]{rudolfCellularpathologieIhrerBegrundung1858} While studying this process of division in 1879, Walther Flemming first used the term "chromatin" to describe the filament-like threads between two dividing cells in 1879\sjcitep[.]{flemmingBeitrageZurKenntniss1879} Flemming's observations did not differ much from his colleague's Wilhelm Waldeyer's description of "chromosomes"\sjcitep[.]{deichmannChromatinItsHistory2015} These early findings directly stemmed from the technological refinement of microscopic lenses, which had by now become accurate enough to permit detailed studies of developing embryos. This early area of cell biology produced a number of fundamental questions that would engage the minds of biologists to this day:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
	\item How can a single fertilised egg cell divide and self-organise into an entire organism?
	\item Where is the information that steers this process encoded, and how is it interpreted?
	\item How can this process go wrong, leading to disease, and can we manipulate it?
\end{enumerate}

At that time, the concept of physical store and transfer of genetic information was not yet widely accepted, and the function and identity of Flemming and Waldeyer's chromosomes was still unknown. In search of answers, marine embryologist Thomas Hunt Morgan began studying inheritance in \textit{Drosophila melanogaster} (fruit fly) in 1908. After years of breeding flies, a mutant with white eyes (aptly named \textit{white}) appeared. Through careful tracking, Morgan discovered that these pigmentless eyes followed a sex-dependent inheritance pattern which could be reliably predicted across generations\sjcitep[.]{morganSexLimitedInheritance1910} More than half a century earlier, Moravian friar Gregor Mendel had demonstrated the exact same principles in \textit{Pisum sativum} (sweet pea), but his work was initially ignored and later disputed by the scientific community upon its rediscovery in 1900. By 1909 however, Danish botanist Wilhelm Johannsen had experimentally repeated much of Mendel's work, and coined the term "gene" to describe the abstract "unit of heredity" that is used to pass on traits between generations\sjcitep[.]{johannsen1909erblichkeitslehre}

Morgan, together with his undergraduate student Alfred Sturtevan, discovered that co-inheritance of genes also followed reproducible patterns ("linkage analysis")\sjcitep[.]{sturtevant1913} This finding supported the idea that genes were located on a linear carrier. A second breakthrough came when Morgan demonstrated that the chromosomes observed by Flemming and Waldeyer were in fact the physical bodies responsible for the transfer of phenotypic traits\sjcitep[.]{morganRandomSegregationCoupling1911} Armed with experimental evidence gathered from thousands of generations of fruit flies, Morgan formally unified the theory of Mendelian inheritance with the physical transfer of genes on chromosomes in 1915\sjcitep[.]{morganMechanismMendelianHeredity1915}

Together, Morgan's findings inaugurated the 20\textsuperscript{th} century's era of genetics, and defined the context for many of the major discoveries that ensued. Hermann Muller, for example, discovered the mutagenic effects of irradiation in \textit{Drosophila}\sjcitep[,]{Muller1927} accelerating the study of mutations\sjcitep[.]{muller1930} In fact, Muller showed that relocation of genes, inferred through Morgan and Sturtevan's linkage analysis, could alter their "variegation" (expression), a very early understanding of cis gene regulation!

In parallel, the field of embryology began to consolidate. Early views such as "preformationism", the idea that all parts of the body were present in miniature form in the embryo at conception, were defeated when Hans Spemann began constricting dividing \textit{Triturus} (newt) embryos. Both halves of the embryo could develop into full, though smaller, animals \textit{if} part of the dorsal side of the embryo were present. Later, Spemann and his student Hilde Mangold, discovered that transplantation of the dorsal lip from one embryo to the ventral side of another embryo would induce the formation of a ventrally conjoined twin (\autoref{fig:spemannmangold})\sjcitep[.]{spemannUberInduktionEmbryonenanlagen1924} Importantly, the pair showed that the conjoined secondary embryo consisted of tissue from the \textit{host}, indicating that the transplant merely instructed its host surroundings to change developmental fate. Spemann and Mangold later named this steering tissue the "organizer field", and multiple labs attempted (unsuccessfully) to purify and characterise the compounds responsible for these processes in an "organizer gold rush"\sjcitep[.]{slackConradHalWaddington2002}

However, the link between embryonic development and the "new" insights in genetics was not readily made, even disputed by parts of the scientific community. This sentiment began to change after a young Conrad Waddington, who had previously been performing organizer grafting experiments in mammals and birds, visited Morgan's fly lab in 1935. Here, Waddington discovered that specific heritable genetic mutations could disrupt \textit{Drosophila} wing development at specific stages\sjcitep[.]{waddingtonGeneticControlWing1940} Based on these findings, Waddington wrote his seminal work \textit{Organisers and Genes} in 1940, and introduced the notion that embryonic development was a large decision tree under the control of genes, which he metaphorically described as a "landscape"\sjcitep[.]{waddingtonOrganisersGenes1940, waddingtonStrategyGenes1957} A ball, representing a cell's current state, metaphorically rolls down the furrows in the landscape, passing several bifurcations which ultimately determine its fate (\autoref{fig:waddington_landscape}a). Beneath the surface, a series of tension wires, symbolizing genes, forcefully pull the surface into shape (\autoref{fig:waddington_landscape}b). Disruptions in an organism's genetic context, for example by mutation, could alter the landscape and thus the cell's final state.

It must be noted that the genes Morgan and Waddington addressed were still somewhat vaguely-defined, as their exact physical nature beyond the chromosome was still unknown. Phoebus Levene, who had identified adenine, guanine, thymine, cytosine, deoxyribose-phosphate backbone as the basic components of DNA, suggested that DNA was ordered in tandem tetranucleotide repeats\sjcitep[.]{levene1919structure} In fact, the storage of genetic information was attributed to proteins at the time, as they made up a much larger portion of the chromosomal dry mass than nucleic acids (DNA, RNA), and were known to be more complex in composition. This notion was overturned in 1944, when Avery, MacLeod and McCarty demonstrated that it was transfer of acids, and not proteins, purified from a non-virulent strain of bacteria that could "transform" a virulent strain to the non-virulent strain\sjcitep[.]{griffithSignificancePneumococcalTypes1928,averyStudiesChemicalNature1944} Despite uncertainties about the physical nature of genetic information, awareness of the link between disease phenotypes, proteins and genes began to rise. For example sickle cell anemia, which was known to follow a Mendelian, heterozygous-recessive inheritence pattern, was shown to be caused by an aberrant form of hemoglobin in red blood cells. Since hemoglobin is a protein, and people with the trait had a 50:50 mix, the most logical explanation was that the gene must encode the structure of the protein\sjcitep[,]{pauling1949sickle} for the first time showing \textit{"a clear case of a change produced in a protein molecule by an allelic change in a single gene"}. A major breakthrough in the molecular understanding of genetic information came when Watson, Crick and Franklin resolved DNA's double-helix structure using X-ray crystallography\sjcitep[,]{watsonMolecularStructureNucleic1953} and for the first time suggested that \textit{"the precise sequence of the bases is the code which carries the genetical information"}\sjcitep[.]{,watsonGeneticalImplicationsStructure1953} It had thus become clear that life stores information in the nucleotide sequence of DNA.

Although Morgan and Waddington's visions of genes as decision makers for a cell's behaviour were largely theoretical, they were soon substantiated. Jacques Monod, also a former student of Thomas Hunt Morgan, observed that bacteria grown in a medium containing both glucose and lactose would preferentially feed on glucose, and would only begin feeding on lactose once the other had run out. Further research together with Fran\c{c}ois Jacob showed that the production of \textbeta-galactosidase, the enzyme responsible for this metabolic switch, was under the control of a "repressor", which was inactivated by the presence of lactose. Together, the pair published these findings in 1961\sjcitep[,]{jacobGeneticRegulatoryMechanisms1961} in an article which features the first diagram of a \textit{gene regulatory network} (\autoref{fig:lac_repressor}). The Jacob-Monod diagram also explicitly showed the repressor as a protein originating from a gene. Genes thus (indirectly) controlled other genes, and therefore the behaviour of the cell - precisely how Conrad Waddington had prophesised twenty years prior.

Earlier that month, Jacob, together with Sidney Brenner, had also proposed the existence of an unstable form of RNA which acted as a "messenger" to transfer information from DNA-based genes to proteins\sjcitep[.]{brennerUnstableIntermediateCarrying1961} This messenger RNA was already clearly depicted as the intermediate carrier of information between genes and proteins in Jacob \& Monod's gene regulatory network. The exact code through which the sequence of mRNA translates to proteins was deciphered soon after using clever "forward" experiments: Marshall Nirenberg fed synthetic RNA homopolymer repeats (e.g. UUU\textsubscript{n}, AAA\textsubscript{n}, ...) to cell-free protein synthesis systems in which one of the 20 possible amino-acids were radioactively labeled. The detection of a radioactive protein product then revealed the identity of the amino-acid for which the input homopolymer coded\sjcitep[.]{nirenbergRNACodewordsProtein1964} Har Gobind Khorana combined more complex RNA trinucleotide repeat polymers (e.g. ACA\textsubscript{n}, UAG\textsubscript{n}, ...) with chemical analysis of the resulting proteins to decipher the remaining possible 3-nucleotide codons, producing a finalised code cipher in 1966\sjcitep[.]{khoranaPolynucleotideSynthesisGenetic1966} These experiments together confirmed how genes encoded for proteins.

Precise extraction and purification of specific proteins or other macromolecules began to take a center stage. For example, in 1966 Gilbert and M{\"u}ller-Hill for the first time purified \textit{lac} repressor protein fractions, confirming Jacob and Monod's theoretical model of a proteins acting as repressors\sjcitep[.]{gilbert1966} A year later, in a letter titled \textit{The Lac Operator is DNA} the pair showed that the the \textit{lac} repressor specifically binds to DNA extracted from \textlambda{}80 phage carrying the \textit{lac} operon, and that this interaction could be disrupted by exposing the complex to IPTG\sjcitep[.]{gilbert1967}

While codons had been de-coded, a "reverse" experiment in which the sequence of a given DNA or RNA molecule could be sequenced had not been performed. Walter Fiers (Ghent University) was the first to do so in 1972 by reporting a complete sequence of the bacteriophage MS2 coat protein gene\sjcitep[.]{jouNucleotideSequenceGene1972} Fiers first digested the phage's \textsuperscript{32}P-labeled RNA genome using ribonuclease T\textsubscript{1}, which cuts RNA at G positions, and iteratively separated the resulting fragments based on size and charge through multiple rounds of two-dimensional fractionation. Jo\"{e}l Vandekerckhove and Marc Van Montagu (both also Ghent University) had simultaneously been working on determining the amino-acid sequence of the MS2 coat protein\sjcitep[.]{vandekerckhoveUseFluorescamineSequence1973} Under the guidance of Van Montagu's preliminary amino-acid map and the Nirenberg-Khorana codon dictionary, Fiers and colleagues were able to puzzle together their fragments into a near-complete base sequence of the MS2 coat protein gene.

Finally, in 1973, Maxam \& Gilbert sequenced the \textit{lac} operon, fully cementing Jacob \& Monod's early model of gene regulation\sjcitep[.]{gilbert1973nucleotide} Restriction enzymes with known cut sites were used to digest a purified RNA copy of the operon, and the fragments were separated on a two-dimensional PAGE developed by Fiers and De Wachter\sjcitep[.]{dewachter1972} Fragment lengths generated by different restriction enzymes could be used to infer the 27 basepair-long sequence: \textit{5'-TGG\textbf{AATTGT}G\textbf{A}G\textbf{C}G\textbf{G}A\textbf{T}A\textbf{ACAATT}-3'}. Gilbert and Maxam remarked that the palindromic symmetry in this short motif could imply that the sequence is bound by a dimer or tetramer of lac repressor proteins. Later, they published an improved DNA sequencing method which relied on comparing the 4 electrophoresis ladders generated by chemically cleaving the DNA of interest at G, C, G+A or C+T sites\sjcitep[.]{maxamGilbert1977} The first exhaustive DNA sequencing method was thus a direct consequence of gene regulation research. Soon after, Fredrick Sanger, who had developed the two-dimensional fractionation used by Fiers\sjcitep[,]{sangerTwodimensionalFractionationProcedure1965} and had already been awarded the Nobel prize for the amino-acid sequence of insulin in 1958, succeeded in deterministically sequencing the entire DNA genome of bacteriophage \textphi X174 in 1977\sjcitep[.]{sangerDNASequencingChainterminating1977} Earlier work had shown that dideoxy variants of the four common nucleotides (A, C, G \& T) would inhibit further polymerisation when included into the newly synthesised DNA strand due to the absence of a reactive phosphate. Sanger devised that a polymerisation reaction performed in a mixture of the four common nucleotides combined with a single dideoxy variant will produce a mixture of strands terminated at each position of the dideoxy variant. He further reasoned that performing this reaction for all four dideoxy variants in parallel should yield all the positions of each of the four bases (\autoref{fig:sangersequencing}). Indeed, when applied to fragments of \textphi X174 DNA, the entire phage's genome (\num{5386} basepairs) could be reconstructed. Initially, Maxam-Gilbert sequencing was more popular than Sanger's method because it could use purified DNA directly without cloning for template amplification, but the toxicity of hydrazine, dimethyl sulfate and radioactive \textsuperscript{32}P ultimately prevented commercialisation and adoption. Sanger sequencing was commercialised throughout the 80s, first as a service, and later packaged into benchtop devices.

\section{DNA-binding proteins}
While the function of chromatin's DNA component as the primary store of information had become clear, the relationship between its protein content and nucleic acids was still under dispute. Alfred and Ellen Stedman investigated different histone fractions between different species and tissues, and proposed that \textit{"the basic proteins of cell nuclei are gene inhibitors, each histone or protamin being capable of suppressing the activities of specific groups of genes"}\sjcitep[.]{stedman1951} This idea was confirmed experimentally when purified, protein-free DNA reconstituted with histone proteins was shown to be inactive in RNA synthesis, despite presence of RNA polymerase\sjcitep[.]{huang1962} Further study of this inhibitory capacity found that \textit{"relatively minor modifications of histone structure (acetylation), taking place on the intact protein molecule, offer a means of switching on or off RNA synthesis at different loci along the chromosome"}\sjcitep[.]{allfrey1964}

By the early 1970s, biochemists had chemically identified five different histone proteins (H1, H2A, H2B, H3, H4), present at ratios of 0.5:1:1:1:1. Histones H2A and H2B were found to be rich in lysine, and H3 in arginine, positively charged amino acids known to bind DNA through electrostatic attraction\sjcitep[.]{delange1972} X-ray diffraction research suggested a DNA "super-helix" structure stabilized by histone proteins\sjcitep[,]{pardon1972} while others found "thin fibrils" and "nodular elements"\sjcitep[.]{slayter1972} It was however known that staphylococcal nuclease would cleave rat liver DNA into fragments of multiples of 200 base pairs in length, heavily hinting that DNA and histone proteins were organised in discrete units\sjcitep[.]{hewish1973} This hypothesis was strengthened by early electron microscopy, which directly visualised chromatin fibers as linear arrays of particles \textasciitilde{\SI{70}{\angstrom}} in diameter resembling "beads on a string"\sjcitep[.]{olins1974spheroid}

Finally, in 1974, Kornberg and Thomas showed that histone proteins H2A and H2B form dimers, and H3 and H4 formed stable tetramers\sjcitep[.]{kornbergChromatinStructureOligomers1974} When mixed with DNA, these dimers and tetramers returned X-ray diffraction spectra identical to native chromatin. Later research with enhanced resolution X-ray diffraction clearly showed a repeating pattern with with a periodicity of \textasciitilde{\SI{100}{\angstrom}}\sjcitep[.]{kornbergchromatinstructurerepeating1974a} Kornberg remarked that \textit{"the occurrence of multiples rather than just 200 base pair pieces is presumably due to some cleavage sites being blocked by nonhistone proteins"} - a prescient vision of transcription factors. Histone protein H1 was later identified as a stabilizer, binding to 20-80 bp long DNA links between histone octamers, leading to supercoiling and further chromatin compaction\sjcitep[.]{finch1976} This new "nucleosome" model of chromatin compacts DNA \textasciitilde{7-fold} at the nucleosome level, and further supercoiling explains how mitotic chromosomes achieve a 10 000-fold compaction. Later, Weintraub and Groudine showed that actively expressed genes are preferentially digested by DNase I\sjcitep[.]{weintraub1976chromosomal} Moreover, digestion with staphylococcal nuclease did \textit{not} show preference for active genes, suggesting that active genes are still nucleosome-bound, although more loosely.

By the late 70s, several pieces of the gene regulatory puzzle had been discovered:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
	\item Genetic information is stored in the base sequence of a cell's DNA.
	\item This information is transferred to proteins according to a well-defined codon "dictionary" through messenger RNA.
	\item Some proteins, such as the lac repressor, are capable of influencing transcription and thus the behaviour of the cell.
\end{enumerate}

However, mechanistic and molecular insights in precisely \textit{how} genes might be regulated molecularly was still sparse. Several breakthroughs came from the lab of Robert Roeder, who had previously purified the polymerase enzymes that catalyze the transcription of messenger RNA from DNA in 1969\sjcitep[.]{roederMultipleFormsDNAdependent1969} His lab showed that purified RNA polymerases could only initiate the transcription of DNA templates in the presence of uncharacterised cellular protein extracts. This finding confirmed earlier ideas that additional, then-unknown factors were necessary to initiate the transcription reaction\sjcitep[.]{ngTranscriptionClonedXenopus1979} Several such initiation factors were purified and characterised within the next year\sjcitep[,]{matsuiMultipleFactorsAre1980} and it soon transpired that a large number of different (co-)factors are necessary for transcription to occur. Parallel research led to the discovery of a protein which induced expression of the \textit{5S} ribosomal RNA gene in \textit{Xenopus} (clawed frog) egg cells. This factor, later named Transcription Factor III (\textit{TFIIIa}), was the first protein shown to initiate transcription of a \textit{specific} gene, rather than genome wide, in eukaryotes. Roeder suggested that \textit{TFIIIa} might \textit{"recognize a unique region of DNA"}, a hypothesis which was explored and demonstrated soon after when \textit{TFIIIa} was shown to bind precisely on position 45 to 96 of the \textit{5S} gene\sjcitep[.]{engelkeSpecificInteractionPurified1980} \textit{5S} rRNA, the gene enhanced by Roeder's original transcription factor, \textit{TFIIIa}, is ubiquitously present in all cells across all known organisms. Transcription factors involved in more specialised, cell-type specific processes were identified and characterised soon after. Examples include the glucocorticoid receptor \textit{Gr}\sjcitep[,]{payvarPurifiedGlucocorticoidReceptors1981} \textit{Sp1}\sjcitep[,]{dynanIsolationTranscriptionFactors1983} and \textit{Gal4}\sjcitep[,]{bramSpecificProteinBinding1985} factors which are still under scrutiny to this day. It also became clear that some of these factors were extremely powerful: their ectopic expression could convert one cell type to another\sjcitep[,]{davisExpressionSingleTransfected1987} and later specific combinations were found to even revert fully differentiated skin cells back to stem cells\sjcitep[.]{takahashiInductionPluripotentStem2006} The molecules responsible for Spemann \& Mangold's organizer were hypothesized to be transcription factors, and several of them were identified molecularly shortly after\sjcitep[.]{choMolecularNatureSpemann1991, lambNeuralInductionSecreted1993}

In parallel, understanding of gene regulation was shifting from an exclusively promoter-proximal process (e.g. \textit{TFIIIa}, TATA-box) to also include promoter-distal elements. Reporter studies by Banerji and colleagues showed that a 72 bp tandem repeat sequence located 200 bp upstream of the SV40 early promoter, which was known to reduce nearby gene expression when mutated, could drive expression at large distances\sjcitep[.]{banerji1981expression} Constructs containing the 72 bp repeat, \textit{"for convenience referred to as the enhancer"}, showed a 200-fold increase in reporter (\textbeta-globulin) expression, and removal of this specific sequence ablated the effect. Moreover, inversion of the region relative to the reporter gene conserved expression, and this effect remained when inserted at distances of up to \SI{3.3}{\kilo\base}. Finally, co-transfection of separate plasmids - one with the SV40 72 bp enhancer, the other containing the \textbeta-globulin reporter gene - did not show expression, confirming that the SV40 enhancer works in cis only. Further research on the immunoglobulin (\textit{Ig}) enhancer, which specifically drives expression of \textit{recombined} V(D)J genes only, showed that this enhancer was active only in B-lymphocyte myeloma cells, not in HeLa, 3T6, or mink lung cells - cell-type specific enhancer activity!\sjcitep[]{banerji1983lymphocyte} The group also correctly proposed that the cell-type specific nature of the Ig enhancer owes its effect to cell-type dependent expression of its binding transcription factors, and that that chromosomal translocations could lead to oncogenic expression such as observed in Burkitt's lymphoma.

Together with the new discoveries of genomic cis-regulatory elements, an explosion in discovery of the physics behind protein-DNA-binding mechanisms occurred. Crystallography of {\textlambda{}} phage (\textit{cI}), \textit{Cro} and \textit{E. coli} \textit{cap}, three known DNA-binding proteins, showed the first generalized mechanism of binding: two {\textalpha{}}-helices joined by a tight turn (helix-turn-helix motif) generate a sharp nick that fits into the major groove of DNA, leading to sequence-specific - though degenerate - binding\sjcitep[.]{pabo1984protein} Simultaneous studies in \textit{D. melanogaster} led to the discovery of the homeodomain, a 60 amino acid DNA-binding domain highly conserved across metazoans, especially those with segmented body plans\sjcitep[.]{mcginnis1984homologous} Later, it was shown that the homeodomain also uses a helix-turn-helix structure, but is significantly longer and complexes DNA differently than the \textlambda{} HTH\sjcitep[.]{kissinger1990engrailed} Zinc-binding repeating Cys2His2 motifs were further identified in \textit{TFIIIa}, and these zinc-protein complexes were also shown to bind specific DNA motifs\sjcitep[.]{miller1985repetitive, pavletich1991zinc} Next, an amino acid motif conserved across transcription factors \textit{CEBP}, \textit{Myc}, \textit{Fos}, \textit{Jun} and the yeast TF \textit{GCN4} were shown to interdigitate through leucine residues every seventh position ("basic leucine zipper"). This interdigitation allows these factors to dimerize, and bind palindromic DNA motifs through adjacent DNA-recognizing residues\sjcitep[.]{landschulz1988leucine} Finally, basic helic-loop-helix motifs were shown to similarly dimarize, allowing both monomers and hetero/homodimers to recognize and bind the palindromic E-box motif (consensus \verb|CANNTG|, but ideally \verb|CACGTG|)\sjcitep[.]{murre1989interactions, ellenberger1994crystal} Structural studies also confirmed that the lac repressor is a tetramer protein capable of binding two operator sites simultaneously, forming DNA loops that repress expression\sjcitep[.]{lewis1996}

Throughout the entirety of the 80s, when mechanistic insights into gene regulation began to form, precise knowledge of gene sequences in human was still sparse. For example, cystic fibrosis, the most common lethal autosomal recessive disease among Caucasians, was \textit{suspected} to be monogenic based on its Mendelian inheritance pattern, but the precise gene responsible had not been identified. By 1985, researchers had narrowed down the location of the cystic fibrosis gene to a region \textit{"1 percent of the human genome, about 30 million base pairs"}\sjcitep[.]{tsui1985} This result was achieved using linkage analysis (invented by Morgan \& Sturtevant) with restriction fragment length polymorphisms\sjcitep[,]{botstein1980} requiring Southern blotting thousands of clone libraries. Sequencing the human CFTR gene using chromosome walking and Sanger's method took several more years\sjcitep[,]{riordan1989,rommens1989} revealing that the mutation {\textdelta}F508 was the most common cause. This marked the first time a disease's causal gene was identified in a purely genomics manner before its protein was known. HTT, the monogene responsible for Huntington's disease, was identified in a nearly identical manner\sjcitep[.]{gusella1983polymorphic,macdonaldNovelGeneContaining1993}

The decade-long efforts required to identify CFTR and HTT led to widespread consensus in the academic community: a complete reference sequence of the human genome was necessary. With a human genome available, researchers would be able to immediately compare any extracted DNA to a healthy reference and locate disease polymorphisms rapidly. Moreover, it was now clear that genomes contained information beyond genes, and that understanding the relationship between these different genomic elements and disease would lead to transformative medicine. Francis Collins, who had been heavily involved in the CFTR gene hunt, became a leading advocate for the Human Genome Project\sjcitep[,]{collins1999} which was officially kicked off in 1990. Originally a joint academic effort between the US National Institutes of Health, several international collaborators (United Kingdom, Germany, Japan, France) soon followed\sjcitep[.]{birney2021international} Interestingly, the US Department of Energy also played a major role, with Lawrence Livermore, Los Alamos and Lawrence Berkeley labs providing extensive support in computational analysis and data storage.

The project was first expected to take 15 years and cost 3 billion dollars. Early efforts focused on a hierarchical clone-by-clone approach: the genome was broken into fragments (\textasciitilde\SI{200}{\kilo\base}) and cloned into over \num{100000} bacterial artificial chromosomes (BACs). These BACs were then linked into large contigs using a variety of techniques (mostly sequence tagging sites PCR, which was somewhat novel at the time\sjcitep[,]{saiki1985,mullis1986} and restriction enzyme fingerprinting), which were in turn mapped to entire chromosomes. A minimum tile path map, dividing the genome into regions that were distinct enough to sequence by Sanger, was made. Individual BAC clones were then further fragmented into smaller pieces (\SI{1000}{\bp}) which could be sequenced individually. The first several years of the project were spent on the minimum-tiling BAC generation and archival. Repetitive DNA regions and coverage gaps complicated the assembly of contigs, and Sanger's dideoxy method was incredibly labour intensive. Luckily, automation soon followed. One company played a particularly important early role: ABI. First by releasing the ABI Prism 373 and 377, then the ABI Prism 310 capillary gel electrophoresis machine, and finally the ABI 3700 96-plex capillary gel electrophoresis analyzer, which became the workhorse of the human genome project. Ensuing throughput advances were staggering: whereas automated slab-gel instruments produced \num{1000} bp/day in 1985, the ABI 3700 capillary array reached \num{1000} bp/s by 1998, and in 1999, HGP centers were generating \textasciitilde90 Gbp per month.

Directly shearing the genome into sequencing-ready fragments, the "shotgun" strategy, had been used to manually map the kilobase-sized genomes of several bacteriophages. This method was deemed unsuitable for larger genomes due to the sheer number of reads required, an amount of data would not have been possible to align on early 90s computing infrastructure. However, in 1995, Craig Venter's group at the Institute for Genomic Research managed to sequence 1.8 MB \textit{H. influenzae} genome using the shotgun approach\sjcitep[.]{fleischmann1995whole} By cloning randomly sheared chromosomal fragments directly, and sequencing the resulting plasmids, the virus's entire genome was sequenced in just 14 months. \num{19687} clones generated \num{24304} reads ($6.3\times$ coverage), taking 30 hours to assemble on the 200 MHz SPARC supercomputer. The assembled genome revealed \textasciitilde\num{1750} genes, a third of which lacked any known function. It had become clear that improvements in computing infrastructure and sequencing hardware had made the shotgun viable, even for the human genome. In 1998, Venter launched Celera Genomics in partnership with Perkin-Elmer/ABI, and together they announced a whole-genome shotgun sequencing of the 3.2 Gb human genome using 300 ABI 3700 capillary sequencers and a 500-processor Compaq Alpha cluster\sjcitep[.]{venter1998shotgun} Celera further refined their strategy, and completed the \textit{D. melanogaster} genome - heavily leaning on approximately 29 Mb of the 120 Mb genome which had already been aligned using the clone-by-clone by international consortia\sjcitep[.]{adams2000genome} By this time, the HGP consortium also began applying shotgun sequencing to its continuing effort. Both the HGP and Celera assemblies of the human genome - each approximately 90\% complete - were released in 2001\sjcitep[.]{landerinitialsequencinganalysis2001,venter2001sequence} Alignment took several months of computing. Of course, the Human Genome Project had been releasing intermittent drafts all throughout its runtime, which Celera could freely download from GenBank: Celera's final assembly was composed of 27M shotgun reads plus 18M reads sourced from shredded public HGP data. In contrast, Celera's assembly was licensed - it could be viewed, but bulk downloads were restricted. While initially financially successful, the Celera assembly was soon surpassed by the HGP's free, and eventually more accurate, assembly. Demand for Celera's version collapsed, and it was placed in the public domain in 2005\sjcitep[.]{kling2005future}

The human genome race cemented WGS as the default strategy for large genomes, and began an era of comparative genomics and high-throughput "omics" driven by rapidly falling sequencing costs. Its economic return was later estimated at nearly \USD800 billion on a \USD3 billion public investment, largely through the birth of sequencing-based diagnostics, bioinformatics, and high-throughput sequencing assays\sjcitep[.]{tripp2011hgpimpact} The now completed assembly allowed researchers to shift focus from recording the genome to annotating it, and finally deciphering its logic. The new fields of transcriptomics, epigenomics, metagenomics, population genetics, ... would now require sequencing not just one genome, but thousands of genomes and other nucleic acid-derived samples across many species, individuals and tissues. This demand could not be met by the Sanger sequencing method, stimulating the development of a variety of new sequencing technologies.

Several NGS platforms were launched almost simultaneously. Roche/454's pyrosequencing\sjcitep[,]{margulies2005genome} ABI's SOLiD, Helicos's single-molecule chemistry, and Solexa's sequencing-by-synthesis (SBS)\sjcitep[]{bennett2005toward} each promised sequencing at a fraction of the Sanger method's cost. The competitive landscape narrowed after 2007, when Illumina acquired Solexa and began to scale SBS to industrial capacity. High signal-to-noise, coupled with better CMOS cameras and patterned flow-cells, allowed Illumina to double output roughly every 12 months while driving per-base cost down by four orders of magnitude\sjcitep[.]{metzkerSequencingTechnologiesNext2010, mardis2011decadesperspective} By 2012, benchtop instruments such as the MiSeq delivered data for \textasciitilde\USD0.10 per megabase\sjcitep[,]{shendureexpandingscopedna2012} compared with \textasciitilde\USD1000 per megabase in 2005\sjcitep[.]{shendureaccuratemultiplexpolony2005} Competing short-read platforms faded, and long-read entrants (PacBio SMRT, Oxford Nanopore) would not appear for another decade (\autoref{fig:sequencing_plaftorms}). A second wave of SBS providers (BGI/MGI, Ultima Genomics, Complete Genomics, Singular Genomics) has recently appeared, following expiry of key Illumina patents.

Sequencing by synthesis was first developed by Balasubramanian and Klenerman at Cambridge in 1998, and works by hybridizing DNA templates to an adapter oligo "lawn" on a glass flow cell\sjcitep[.]{furey1998use} Here, each individual template is amplified to generate a cluster of clones, which is then sequenced by sequentially adding fluorescently labeled nucleotides and recording where they are incorporated. Each incorporation cycle is imaged, generating imaging data of the oligo lawn. Image processing is then used to determine the position of oligo clusters present on the lawn, and these are stored in \verb|.locs| (locations) files. The basecalling algorithm will combine these \verb|.locs| files together with the imaging data to extract fluorescence intensities for all colours used per cluster. Dominant fluorescent signals then determine the base called for each cluster for every cycle. When no base can be called, \verb|N| is used. When a strand does not incorporate a base during a given cycle, or if it leads ahead by one base, it becomes out of phase with the other strands of that cluster for the rest of the sequencing run. This is called phasing/pre-phasing and is corrected by the basecalling algorithm. Theoretical phasing rates (approximately 0.1\% of strands in a given cluster per cycle) can be used to estimate the contribution of phasing noise and deconvolve true signal from phased noise. Each basecall is also assigned a "\verb|Phred|" quality score \verb|Q| based on signal-to-noise ratio, phasing, and separation of dense clusters: \( \verb|Q| = -10 \cdot \log_{10} (P) \) where \verb|P| is the probability of the basecall being correct. A \verb|Q| score of 30 corresponds to a probability of error of 1 in 1000. The result is a binary \verb|.bcl| file, which contains the basecalls for each cluster, and can then be converted to the \verb|ASCII| \verb|.fastq| format using \verb|bcl2fastq|.

Each sequenced strand is represented by four lines in the \verb|.fastq| file (\autoref{fig:fastq}). The first line is a header, which stores metadata such as sequencer and cluster coordinates. The second line is the sequence itself, followed by a separator (\verb|+|) on the third line, and the \verb|ASCII|-encoded quality score on the fourth line.
