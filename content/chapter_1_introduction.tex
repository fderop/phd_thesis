\chapter{\nohyphens{The Accessible Genome at Single-Cell Resolution}}
This introductory chapter will attempt to build the foundation for the explorations that follow by providing a historical overview of chromatin accessibility based on historical accounts\sjcitep[,]{slackConradHalWaddington2002, olinsChromatinHistoryOur2003, cobbWhoDiscoveredMessenger2015, deichmannChromatinItsHistory2015, deichmannEpigeneticsOriginsEvolution2016, lis50YearHistory2019, roeder50YearsEukaryotic2019} and frame it within the ongoing research on decoding gene regulation. Then, we illustrate the state of the art of single-cell chromatin sequencing technologies when I began this work, and delve into the capabilities and limitations of several breakthrough techniques. This context serves to highlight the importance of the subject matter, and to motivate our subsequent discussions and findings.

The word "cell" was first used in 1665 by Robert Hooke to describe the hexagonal walls he observed in a thin slice of cork through a self-made microscope\sjcitep[.]{hookeMicrographia1665} It took several more centuries of research until the concept of cells as the foundational building blocks of life began to take shape. Only in 1839, Theodor Schwann proposed that animals, just like plants, were composed of cells\sjcitep[,]{schwannMikroskopischeUntersuchungenUber1839} and Rudolf Virchow and Robert Remak later added that cells were the product of division of pre-existing cells\sjcitep[.]{rudolfCellularpathologieIhrerBegrundung1858} While studying this process of division in 1879, Walther Flemming first used the term "chromatin" to describe the filament-like threads between two dividing cells in 1879\sjcitep[.]{flemmingBeitrageZurKenntniss1879} Flemming's observations did not differ much from his colleague's Wilhelm Waldeyer's description of "chromosomes"\sjcitep[.]{deichmannChromatinItsHistory2015} These early findings directly stemmed from the refinement of microscopic lenses, which had by now become accurate enough to permit detailed studies of developing embryos. Notably, the work of Baltic-German polymath and explorer Karl Ernst von Baer, who between 1817 and 1826 made the first accounts of egg cells, development of the blastula, the notochord and the three germ layers, produced a number of fundamental questions that would engage the minds of biologists for the coming centuries:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
	\item How can a fertilised egg cell self-organise into an entire organism?
	\item Where is the information that steers this process encoded, and how is it interpreted?
	\item How can this process go wrong, leading to disease? \footnotetextwithoutmarker{\superfullcite{tagarelliHansSpemannOne2004}A. Tagarelli, et al. By the early 20th century, several groups were systematically studying these concepts. In 1924, Hans Spemann, together with his student Hilde Mangold, discovered that transplantation of the dorsal lip from one \textit{Triturus} (newt) embryo to the ventral side of another embryo would induce the formation of a ventrally conjoined twin (\autoref{fig:spemannmangold})\sjcitep[.]{spemannUberInduktionEmbryonenanlagen1924} Importantly, the pair showed that the conjoined secondary embryo primarily consisted of tissue from the \textit{host}, indicating that this transplanted tissue, which Spemann later named the "organizer field", merely instructed its surroundings to change developmental fate. Multiple labs raced to purify and characterise the compound responsible for these processes in an "organizer gold rush"\sjcitep[,]{slackConradHalWaddington2002} but without success. We now know that the compounds responsible for this process are small, secreted molecules termed "growth factors", and are capable of steering a cell's fate when present even at only nanogram quantities - far below the detection limit of that era's technologies.

Around that time, the concept of physical store and transfer of genetic information was not yet widely accepted, and the function and identity of Flemming and Waldeyer's chromosomes was unknown. In search of answers, Thomas Hunt Morgan began studying inheritance by irradiating \textit{Drosophila melanogaster} (fruit fly) and cross-breeding the resulting mutants in 1908. After some early years without results, Morgan discovered that the inheritance pattern of some mutant traits, such as the flies' eye colours, could be reliably predicted across generations\sjcitep[.]{morganSexLimitedInheritance1910} More than half a century earlier, Moravian friar Gregor Mendel had demonstrated the exact same principles in \textit{Pisum sativum} (sweet pea), but his work was lost and disputed by the scientific community upon its rediscovery. Using his own findings, Morgan verified Mendel's work and formally defined the theory of Mendelian inheritance in 1915\sjcitep[.]{morganMechanismMendelianHeredity1915} A second breakthrough came when Morgan demonstrated that the chromosomes observed by Flemming and Waldeyer were in fact the physical bodies responsible for the transfer of phenotypic traits\sjcitep[,]{morganRandomSegregationCoupling1911} confirming earlier ideas that chromosomes carried "genes" which encoded heritable traits.

Together, Morgan's findings inaugurated the 20\textsuperscript{th} century's era of genetics, and defined the context for many of the major discoveries that ensued. However, the link between embryonic development and Morgan's "new" insights in genetics was not readily made, and even dismissed by the broader scientific community. This changed when a young Conrad Waddington, who had previously been performing organizer grafting experiments in mammals and birds, visited Morgan's fly lab in 1935. Here, Waddington intensely studied \textit{Drosophila}'s embryonic wing development, and discovered that specific heritable mutations could disrupt this process at specific stages\sjcitep[.]{waddingtonGeneticControlWing1940} Based on these findings, Waddington wrote his seminal work \textit{Organisers and Genes} in 1940, and introduced the notion that embryonic development was a large decision tree under the control of genes, which he metaphorically described as a "landscape"\sjcitep[.]{waddingtonOrganisersGenes1940} In 1957, Waddington extensively elaborated on his concept of the "epigenetic landscape" in his eighth book, \textit{The Strategy of the Genes}\sjcitep[.]{waddingtonStrategyGenes1957} Here, he famously formulated this landscape as a multidimensional metabolic state space where a ball, representing a cell, metaphorically rolls downwards during development, passing several bifurcations which ultimately determine its fate (\autoref{fig:waddington_landscape}a). Beneath the surface, a series of tension ropes, symbolizing genes, forcefully pull the surface into shape (\autoref{fig:waddington_landscape}b).

In fact, the storage of genetic information was attributed to proteins at the time, as they made up a much larger portion of the chromosomal dry mass than nucleic acids, and were deemed far more complex in composition. Nucleic acids were thought to be only of structural importance to chromosomes, akin to the highly repetitive polymers found in cell walls and plant fibers. This notion was overturned in 1944, when Avery, MacLeod and McCarty demonstrated that it was DNA, and not proteins, purified from a non-virulent, rough (R) strain of bacteria that could "transform" a virulent, smooth (S) strain to the R strain\sjcitep[.]{griffithSignificancePneumococcalTypes1928,averyStudiesChemicalNature1944}

The Avery-MacLeod-McCarty experiment formally cemented DNA as the storage location of genetic information, but another decade would pass before the exact molecular mechanism through which DNA could contain information was discovered. This breakthrough came when Watson, Crick and Franklin resolved the double-helix structure of DNA using X-ray crystallography (\autoref{fig:watsoncrickfranklin})\sjcitep[,]{watsonMolecularStructureNucleic1953} and for the first time suggested that \textit{"the precise sequence of the bases is the code which carries the genetical information"}\sjcitep[.]{,watsonGeneticalImplicationsStructure1953} It had thus become clear that life stores information in the nucleotide sequence of DNA, and the scientific community began to prepare experiments that would leverage this concept.

\section{The early molecular era}
Although Conrad Waddington's visionary ideas were largely theoretical, they were soon substantiated. Jacques Monod, yet another pupil of Thomas Hunt Morgan, observed that bacteria grown in a medium containing both glucose and lactose would preferentially feed on glucose, and would only begin feeding on lactose once glucose had run out. Further research together with Monod's student François Jacob led to the understanding that the production of \textbeta-galactosidase, the enzyme responsible for lactose catabolism, was under the control of a "repressor", which was inactivated in the presence of lactose. Together, the pair published these findings in 1961\sjcitep[,]{jacobGeneticRegulatoryMechanisms1961} in a review which features the first diagram of a \textit{gene regulatory network} (\autoref{fig:lac_repressor}). The Jacob-Monod diagram also explicitly showed the repressor as a protein originating from a gene. Genes thus (indirectly) controlled other genes, and therefore the behaviour of the cell - exactly how Conrad Waddington had prophesised twenty years prior.

Earlier that month, Jacob, together with Sidney Brenner, had also proposed the existence of an unstable form of RNA which acted as a "messenger" to transfer information from DNA-based genes to proteins\sjcitep[.]{brennerUnstableIntermediateCarrying1961} This messenger RNA was already clearly depicted as the intermediate carrier of information between genes and proteins in Jacob \& Monod's gene regulatory network. The exact code through which the sequence of mRNA translates to proteins was deciphered soon after using clever "forward" experiments: Marshall Nirenberg, in the lab of Philip Leder, fed synthetic RNA homopolymer repeats (e.g. UUU\textsubscript{n}, AAA\textsubscript{n}, ...) to cell-free protein synthesis systems in which one of the 20 possible amino-acids were radioactively labeled. The detection of a radioactive protein product then revealed the identity of the amino-acid for which the input homopolymer coded\sjcitep[.]{nirenbergRNACodewordsProtein1964} Har Gobind Khorana combined more complex RNA trinucleotide repeat polymers (e.g. ACA\textsubscript{n}, UAG\textsubscript{n}, ...) with chemical analysis of the resulting proteins to decipher the remaining possible 3-nucleotide codons, producing a finalised code in 1966\sjcitep[.]{khoranaPolynucleotideSynthesisGenetic1966}

A "backward" experiment in which the sequence of a given DNA or RNA molecule could be sequenced remained elusive. The lab of Walter Fiers (Ghent University) was the first to do so in 1972 by reporting a complete sequence of the bacteriophage MS2 coat protein gene\sjcitep[.]{jouNucleotideSequenceGene1972} Fiers first digested the phage's \textsuperscript{32}P-labeled RNA genome using ribonuclease T\textsubscript{1}, which cuts RNA at G positions, and iteratively separated the resulting fragments based on size and charge through multiple rounds of two-dimensional fractionation. Joël Vandekerckhove and Marc Van Montagu (both also Ghent University) had simultaneously been working on determining the amino-acid sequence of the MS2 coat protein\sjcitep[.]{vandekerckhoveUseFluorescamineSequence1973} Under the guidance of Van Montagu's preliminary amino-acid map and the Nirenberg-Khorana codon dictionary, Fiers and colleagues were able to puzzle together their fragments into a near-complete base sequence of the MS2 coat protein gene.

Soon after, Fredrick Sanger, who had developed the two-dimensional fractionation used by Fiers\sjcitep[,]{sangerTwodimensionalFractionationProcedure1965} and had been awarded the Nobel prize for the amino-acid sequence of insulin in 1958, succeeded in deterministically sequencing the entire DNA genome of bacteriophage \textphi X174 in 1977\sjcitep[.]{sangerDNASequencingChainterminating1977} Earlier work had shown that dideoxy variants of the four common nucleotides (A, C, G \& T) would inhibit further polymerisation when included into the newly synthesised DNA strand due to the absence of a reactive phosphate. Sanger devised that a polymerisation reaction performed in a mixture of the four common nucleotides combined with a single dideoxy variant will produce a mixture of strands terminated at each position of the dideoxy variant. Sanger further reasoned that performing this reaction for all four dideoxy variants in parallel should yield all the positions of each of the four bases (\autoref{fig:sangersequencing}). Indeed, when applied to fragments of \textphi X174 DNA, the entire phage's genome (5386 basepairs) could be reconstructed. Sanger's method now allowed researchers to sequence DNA at a relatively "rapid" pace and with higher accuracy than previously possible, setting the stage for the coming decade's research. \section{Gene regulation unveiled}
In hindsight, several pieces of the gene regulatory puzzle had now been discovered:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
	\item Genetic information is stored in the base sequence of a cell's DNA, and DNA can be sequenced using Sanger's method.
	\item This information is transferred to proteins according to a well-defined codon "dictionary" through messenger RNA. Transcription of DNA to RNA is thus a crucial process of information transfer.
	\item The resulting proteins are capable of influencing transcription and thus the behaviour of the cell.
\end{enumerate}

However, mechanistic and molecular insights in \textit{how} genes might be regulated was still sparse. Several breakthroughs came from the lab of Robert Roeder. Roeder, who as a student had purified the polymerase enzymes that catalyze the transcription of messenger RNA from DNA in 1969\sjcitep[,]{roederMultipleFormsDNAdependent1969} showed that purified RNA polymerases could only initiate the transcription of DNA templates in the presence of uncharacterised cellular protein extracts. This finding confirmed earlier ideas that additional, then-unknown factors were necessary to initiate the transcription reaction\sjcitep[.]{ngTranscriptionClonedXenopus1979} Several such initiation factors were purified and characterised within the next year\sjcitep[,]{matsuiMultipleFactorsAre1980} and it soon transpired that a large number of different factors are necessary for transcription to occur.

Parallel research in Roeder's lab led to the discovery of a protein which induced expression of the 5S ribosomal RNA gene in \textit{Xenopus} (clawed frog) egg cells. This factor, later named Transcription Factor III (\textit{TFIIIa}), was the first protein shown to initiate transcription of a \textit{specific} gene in eukaryotes, and not just initiate transcription in general. Roeder and his student Takashi Matsui suggested that \textit{TFIIIa} might \textit{"recognize a unique region of DNA"}, a hypothesis which was explored and demonstrated soon after\sjcitep[.]{engelkeSpecificInteractionPurified1980} However, as part of the ribosomal body, 5S rRNA plays a role in cellular protein synthesis, and is ubiquitously present in all cells across all known organisms. Transcription factors involved in more specialised processes were identified and characterised relatively quickly: examples include the glucocorticoid receptor \textit{Gr}\sjcitep[,]{payvarPurifiedGlucocorticoidReceptors1981} \textit{Sp1}\sjcitep[,]{dynanIsolationTranscriptionFactors1983} and \textit{Gal4}\sjcitep[,]{bramSpecificProteinBinding1985} important transcription factors which are still under scrutiny to this day. In fact, it became clear that some of these factors were extremely powerful: their ectopic expression could convert one cell type to another\sjcitep[,]{davisExpressionSingleTransfected1987} and later specific combinations were found to even revert fully differentiated skin cells back to stem cells\sjcitep[.]{takahashiInductionPluripotentStem2006} By this time, the scientific realised that the factors responsible for the activity of Spemann \& Mangold's organizer were transcription factors, and several of these factors were identified shortly after\sjcitep[.]{choMolecularNatureSpemann1991, lambNeuralInductionSecreted1993}

A brief timeline:
The human genome project started in 1985 as a joint academic effort between the US National Institutes of Health. Surprisingly, the US Department of Energy also played a major role, with Lawrence Livermore, Los Alamos and Lawrence Berkeley labs providing extensive support in computational analysis and data storage. The ties between DNA, cancer, radiation damage and the immune system were also known already. The project was first expected to take 15 years and cost ~\$3 billion dollars. Early efforts focused on a hierarchical approach: the genome was broken into fragments (~200kbp) and cloned into bacterial artificial chromosomes (BACs). These BACs were then linked into large contigs using a variety of techniques (mainly sequence tagging sites PCR and restriction enzyme fingerprinting), which were in turn mapped to entire chromosomes. Individual BAC clones were then further fragmeted into smaller pieces (1000bp) which could be sequenced via the Sanger method. A number of problems made this approach a slow process: repetitive DNA regions and coverage gaps and made the assembly of contigs difficult, and Sanger's dideoxy method was incredibly labour intensive. Luckily, automation soon followed. One company played a particularly important role: ABI, first with the ABI Prism 373 and 377 automated slab gel electrophoresis analyzers, then with the ABI Prism 310 capilarry gel electrophoresis, and finally the ABI 3700 96-plex capillary gel electrophoresis analyzer (1998), which became the workhorse of the human genome project. This machine could sequence \textit{thousands} of base pairs per day (this is particularly funny to write in 2025)!

In 1995, craig venter founded celera. at celera, 300 ABI 3700s were working 24/7 with the explicit intent to sequence the human genome faster than the human genome project. Their trick was to bypass the contig assembly altogether: instead, they used a whole genome shotgun approach, fragmenting the genome directly into fragments that could be run on the ABI 3700. This approach was first considered infeasible to resolve computationally, but development of better computers and algorithms during the 90s, and using the human genomoe projects' intermittently released data allowed Celera to publish a draft in 2001.

\section{Next generation sequencing}
[maybe link this part better with the previous section] The completion of the human genome project in 2003 allowed researchers to shift focus from recording the genome sequence to deciphering its code. The new fields of transcriptomics, epigenomics, metagenomics, population genetics, ... now required sequencing not just one genome, but thousands of genomes and other nucleic acid samples across many species, individuals and tissues. This demand could not be met by the Sanger sequencing method, spurring the development of a variety of new sequencing technologies. Sequencing by Synthesis (SBS) was developed around 1998 by chemist Shankar Balasubramanian and biophysicist David Klenerman, both professors at the university of Cambridge. The technology worked by hybridizing millions of DNA templates to an oligo "lawn" on a flow cell, amplifying each individual molecule to generate a cluster of clones, and sequencing each cluster by sequentially adding fluorescently labeled nucleotides.

Next Generation Sequencing \sjcitep[.]{metzkerSequencingTechnologiesNext2010}

How does basecalling work?

Each incorporation cycle is imaged, generating imaging data of the oligo lawn. Image processing is used to determine the position of oligo clusters present on the lawn, and these are stored in \verb|.locs| (locations) files. The basecalling algorithm will combine these \verb|.locs| files together with the imaging data to extract fluorescence intensities for all colours used per cluster. Dominant fluorescent signals then determine the base called for each cluster for every cycle.  When no base can be called, \verb|N| is used.


When a strand does not incorporate a base during a given cycle, or if it leads ahead by one base, it becomes out of phase with the other strands of that cluster for the rest of the sequencing run. This is called phasing/pre-phasing and is corrected by the basecalling algorithm. Theoretical phasing rates (approximately 0.1\% of strands in a given cluster per cycle) can be used to estimate the contribution of phasing noise and deconvolve true signal from phased noise.

Each basecall is also assigned a "Phred" quality score \verb|Q| based on signal-to-noise ratio, phasing, and separation of dense clusters:

\begin{equation}
	Q = -10 \cdot \log_{10} (P)
\end{equation}

A Q score of 30 corresponds to a probability of error of 1 in 1000. 

where \verb|P| is the probability of the basecall being correct.

The result is a binary \verb|.bcl| file, which contains the basecalls for each cluster. The binary \verb|.bcl| file is then converted to the ASCII \verb|.fastq| format using \verb|bcl2fastq|.

Each sequenced strand is represented by four lines in the \verb|.fastq| file. The first line is a header, which stores metadata such as sequencer and cluster coordinates. The second line is the sequence itself, followed by a separator (\verb|+|) on the third line, and the ASCII-encodedquality score on the fourth line.

Once data is sequenced, and raw \verb|.fastq| data is available, the first step in data analysis is alignment to a reference genome. A number of algorithms exist for this task, and the optimal choice depends on several factors. Short-read aligners such as \verb|bowtie2| are optimized for reads < \SI{200}{\bp}, while long-read aligners like \verb|minimap2| are better at handling reads > \SI{1000}{\bp}. The type of sequencing technology used also plays a role: sequencing by synthesis (Illumina) generates more indels due to incorporation errors, which are explicitly corrected for by \verb|bwa-mem|. \verb|minimap2| is better suited for handling the higher single-base error rates found in Nanopore/PacBio reads. When sequencing transcriptomic data, RNA-seq specific aligners like \verb|STAR| and \verb|HISAT2| are used to handle spliced reads. Like many classes of algorithms, a trade-off between memory and compute can also be made, for example \verb|STAR| requires large amounts of memory due to its use of an uncompressed genome index, but is faster, while \verb|HISAT2| is more memory-efficient, but takes longer.

The diversity in sequencing technologies and alignment algorithms led to the use of several independent alignment formats, each tied to specific sequencing technologies or alignment algorithms. Analyses on different datasets (e.g. variant calling, genotype analysis) required cross parsing or conversion. \section{Sequencing assays}
Before the availability of next-generation sequencing technology, studying the genome and gene expression at scale relied heavily on microarrays. Here, nucleic acid samples are hybridized to pre-designed DNA probes arrayed on a glass slide, and fluorescence intensity measures response levels of each probe. This technique was, and still is, used to quantify RNA transcripts\sjcitep[,]{schenaQuantitativeMonitoringGene1995} genotyping using SNP arrays\sjcitep[,]{wang1998large} and even DNA-protein interactions using ChIP-on-chip\sjcitep[.]{ren2000genome} While microarrays allow for up to ~\num{50000} probes to be interrogated in a single experiment, limited dynamic range biases absolute quantification. The probe set also requires intelligent design and thus cannot discover novel sequences. Probeset size is particularly troubling for ChIP-on-Chip, since binning limits genomic resolution to around 500-1000 bp - too low for novel TF motif discovery.

By the mid 2000s, the availability of the human genome as a public resource and the falling cost of NGS allowed researchers to test hypotheses in a much more systematic manner than before. As a result, focus shifted from cataloging the genome towards determining how it functioned and self-organized. One of the first evolutions was the transition from microarrays to sequencing-based quantitative assays, beginning with histone methylation ChIP-seq in 2007\sjcitep[.]{barskiHighresolutionProfilingHistone2007} By sequencing immunoprecipitated DNA directly, methylation could be assessed genome-wide and with fewer quantitative biases. Specifically, a spectrum of 20 different lys/arg histone methylation ChIP-seq variants was performed on CD4\textsuperscript{+} T cells, and correlated to GNF SymAtlas gene expression microarray data. This approach allowed genome-wide classification and discovery of functional elements (active/inactive/poised promoters, enhancers, gene bodies, CTCF insulators) in the genome based on their methylation signatures. The distribution of these methylation signatures could then be systematically correlated to disease-associated regions, for example enrichment of H3K4Me3 enrichment near canonical sites of chromosome breakpoints in T cell cancers. In parallel, ChIP-seq was being applied to transcription factors, improving transcription factor footprinting resolution to 50 base pairs. Mechanistic understanding of how transcription factors bind was being refined rapidly. "True" single-cell sequencing was first demonstrated in 2009 by Tang and Surani at the Gurdon Institute\sjcitep[.]{tangMRNASeqWholeTranscriptome2009} By modifying existing mRNA amplification methods (notably increasing reverse transcription and PCR extension time), the group was able to generate sequencing-ready cDNA libraries from individual mouse blastomeres. This sequencing-based approach provided a number of immediate advantages over microarray analysis: comparison of single-blastomere mRNA-seq data with pooled (320 cells) Affymetrix microarray data showed that \SI{94.3}{\percent} of microarray-detected genes had at least 5 transcripts in the mRNA-seq data, but mRNA-seq detected \num{5270} more genes in total. Sensitivity and dynamic range (\SIrange{0.2}{12000}{RPKM}) were decidedly higher for mRNA-seq than for analog microarrays. Since whole transcripts were sequenced, the group also discovered \num{1753} novel splice junctions, and demonstrated for the first time that different isoforms of the same gene can occur in the same single cell. 

Tang et al. used the ABI SOLiD sequencer, which had become commercially available only two years prior. Given that the sample extraction and amplification protocol itself was only slightly different from existing microarray protocols, it can be posed that development of this method was a direct result of sequencing hardware improvements. However, the new sequencing-based approach also came with a new set of challenges. Most importantly, the cost of sequencing was incredibly high - Tang et al. spent around 24 to 85 million reads per \textit{single cell}, equivalent to \USD{10000} to \USD{50000} in SOLiD sequencing costs per cell alone. At the time, one Affymetric microarray sample cost in the order of \USD{800}. Despite longer extension times, the protocol was also unable to capture transcripts longer than \SI{3}{kb}, and was limited to poly(A)\textsuperscript{+} transcripts since it was based on oligo(dT) capture. The barcode-rank plot (also referred to as "knee or elbow plot") visualises the distribution of reads across all sequenced barcodes in the sequencing dataset. It is obtained by counting the number of reads per barcode, and ranking each barcode in a log-log figure - similarly in concept to a Zipf or Lorenz curve. If the dataset is sequenced to near-saturation, the total number of barcodes will exceed the whitelist, as sequencing/PCR/synthesis errors introduce non-whitelisted species. In the "ideal signal" case, all reads are evenly distributed across a small number of barcodes that matches the number of input cells (e.g. \num{10000} in the figure). This means that there are no quality differences between real cells, and there are no barcodes with low read counts. The opposite scenario ("ideal noise") has an even distribution of all reads across \textit{all} barcodes, and we cannot distinguish cells from noise. Real datasets fall between these two extremes. A high quality dataset exhibits a clear cut-off, where the number of reads per barcode sharply drops off in the transition between real cells and background noise. A dataset of lower quality will exhibit a more gradual transition between real cells and background noise. This can be caused by a variety of mechanisms, notably ambient RNA or non-pure cell barcodes on the beads.

Conventional bulk RNA-seq analysis tools modeled gene counts using Poissonian (edgeR\sjcitep[]{robinsonEdgeRBioconductorPackage2010}) or Negative Binomial (DESeq\sjcitep[]{andersDifferentialExpressionAnalysis2010}) distributions. Compared to bulk RNA-seq and microarray data, single-cell sequencing data is highly zero-inflated: when sampling transcripts in a single cell, the probability of a given gene not being detected is high, leading to large amounts of "dropouts" (false negative gene expression), necessitating more complex approaches. These dropout probabilities are then used as priors to model gene expression levels in individual cells. Dropouts are weighted based on their prior probability, and likely relative (fold-change) gene expression levels are inferred.
