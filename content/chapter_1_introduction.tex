The word "cell" was first used in 1665 by Robert Hooke to describe the hexagonal walls he observed in a thin slice of cork through a self-made microscope\sjcitep[.]{hookeMicrographia1665} It took several more centuries of research until the concept of cells as the foundational building blocks of life began to take shape. Only in 1839, Theodor Schwann proposed that animals, just like plants, were composed of cells\sjcitep[,]{schwannMikroskopischeUntersuchungenUber1839} and Rudolf Virchow and Robert Remak later added that cells were the product of division of pre-existing cells\sjcitep[.]{rudolfCellularpathologieIhrerBegrundung1858} While studying this process of division in 1879, Walther Flemming first used the term "chromatin" to describe the filament-like threads between two dividing cells in 1879\sjcitep[.]{flemmingBeitrageZurKenntniss1879} Flemming's observations did not differ much from his colleague's Wilhelm Waldeyer's description of "chromosomes"\sjcitep[.]{deichmannChromatinItsHistory2015} These early findings directly stemmed from the refinement of microscopic lenses, which had by now become accurate enough to permit detailed studies of developing embryos. Notably, the work of Baltic-German polymath and explorer Karl Ernst von Baer, who between 1817 and 1826 made the first accounts of egg cells, development of the blastula, the notochord and the three germ layers, produced a number of fundamental questions that would engage the minds of biologists for the coming centuries:

By the early 20th century, several groups were systematically studying these concepts. In 1924, Hans Spemann, together with his student Hilde Mangold, discovered that transplantation of the dorsal lip from one \textit{Triturus} (newt) embryo to the ventral side of another embryo would induce the formation of a ventrally conjoined twin (\autoref{fig:spemannmangold})\sjcitep[.]{spemannUberInduktionEmbryonenanlagen1924} Importantly, the pair showed that the conjoined secondary embryo primarily consisted of tissue from the \textit{host}, indicating that this transplanted tissue, which Spemann later named the "organizer field", merely instructed its surroundings to change developmental fate. Multiple labs raced to purify and characterise the compound responsible for these processes in an "organizer gold rush"\sjcitep[,]{slackConradHalWaddington2002} but without success. We now know that the compounds responsible for this process are small, secreted molecules termed "growth factors", and are capable of steering a cell's fate when present even at only nanogram quantities - far below the detection limit of that era's technologies.

Around that time, the concept of physical store and transfer of genetic information was not yet widely accepted, and the function and identity of Flemming and Waldeyer's chromosomes was unknown. In search of answers, Thomas Hunt Morgan began studying inheritance by irradiating \textit{Drosophila melanogaster} (fruit fly) and cross-breeding the resulting mutants in 1908. After some early years without results, Morgan discovered that the inheritance pattern of some mutant traits, such as the flies' eye colours, could be reliably predicted across generations\sjcitep[.]{morganSexLimitedInheritance1910} More than half a century earlier, Moravian friar Gregor Mendel had demonstrated the exact same principles in \textit{Pisum sativum} (sweet pea), but his work was lost and disputed by the scientific community upon its rediscovery. Using his own findings, Morgan verified Mendel's work and formally defined the theory of Mendelian inheritance in 1915\sjcitep[.]{morganMechanismMendelianHeredity1915} A second breakthrough came when Morgan demonstrated that the chromosomes observed by Flemming and Waldeyer were in fact the physical bodies responsible for the transfer of phenotypic traits\sjcitep[,]{morganRandomSegregationCoupling1911} confirming earlier ideas that chromosomes carried "genes" which encoded heritable traits.

Together, Morgan's findings inaugurated the 20\textsuperscript{th} century's era of genetics, and defined the context for many of the major discoveries that ensued. However, the link between embryonic development and Morgan's "new" insights in genetics was not readily made, and even dismissed by the broader scientific community. This changed when a young Conrad Waddington, who had previously been performing organizer grafting experiments in mammals and birds, visited Morgan's fly lab in 1935. Here, Waddington intensely studied \textit{Drosophila}'s embryonic wing development, and discovered that specific heritable mutations could disrupt this process at specific stages\sjcitep[.]{waddingtonGeneticControlWing1940} Based on these findings, Waddington wrote his seminal work \textit{Organisers and Genes} in 1940, and introduced the notion that embryonic development was a large decision tree under the control of genes, which he metaphorically described as a "landscape"\sjcitep[.]{waddingtonOrganisersGenes1940} In 1957, Waddington extensively elaborated on his concept of the "epigenetic landscape" in his eighth book, \textit{The Strategy of the Genes}\sjcitep[.]{waddingtonStrategyGenes1957} Here, he famously formulated this landscape as a multidimensional metabolic state space where a ball, representing a cell, metaphorically rolls downwards during development, passing several bifurcations which ultimately determine its fate (\autoref{fig:waddington_landscape}a). Beneath the surface, a series of tension ropes, symbolizing genes, forcefully pull the surface into shape (\autoref{fig:waddington_landscape}b).

In fact, the storage of genetic information was attributed to proteins at the time, as they made up a much larger portion of the chromosomal dry mass than nucleic acids, and were deemed far more complex in composition. Nucleic acids were thought to be only of structural importance to chromosomes, akin to the highly repetitive polymers found in cell walls and plant fibers. This notion was overturned in 1944, when Avery, MacLeod and McCarty demonstrated that it was DNA, and not proteins, purified from a non-virulent, rough (R) strain of bacteria that could "transform" a virulent, smooth (S) strain to the R strain\sjcitep[.]{griffithSignificancePneumococcalTypes1928,averyStudiesChemicalNature1944}

The Avery-MacLeod-McCarty experiment formally cemented DNA as the storage location of genetic information, but another decade would pass before the exact molecular mechanism through which DNA could contain information was discovered. This breakthrough came when Watson, Crick and Franklin resolved the double-helix structure of DNA using X-ray crystallography (\autoref{fig:watsoncrickfranklin})\sjcitep[,]{watsonMolecularStructureNucleic1953} and for the first time suggested that \textit{"the precise sequence of the bases is the code which carries the genetical information"}\sjcitep[.]{,watsonGeneticalImplicationsStructure1953} It had thus become clear that life stores information in the nucleotide sequence of DNA, and the scientific community began to prepare experiments that would leverage this concept.

\section{The early molecular era}
Although Conrad Waddington's visionary ideas were largely theoretical, they were soon substantiated. Jacques Monod, also a former pupil of Thomas Hunt Morgan, observed that bacteria grown in a medium containing both glucose and lactose would preferentially feed on glucose, and would only begin feeding on lactose once glucose had run out. Further research together with Monod's student François Jacob led to the understanding that the production of \textbeta-galactosidase, the enzyme responsible for lactose catabolism, was under the control of a "repressor", which was inactivated in the presence of lactose. Together, the pair published these findings in 1961\sjcitep[,]{jacobGeneticRegulatoryMechanisms1961} in a review which features the first diagram of a \textit{gene regulatory network} (\autoref{fig:lac_repressor}). The Jacob-Monod diagram also explicitly showed the repressor as a protein originating from a gene. Genes thus (indirectly) controlled other genes, and therefore the behaviour of the cell - exactly how Conrad Waddington had prophesised twenty years prior.

Earlier that month, Jacob, together with Sidney Brenner, had also proposed the existence of an unstable form of RNA which acted as a "messenger" to transfer information from DNA-based genes to proteins\sjcitep[.]{brennerUnstableIntermediateCarrying1961} This messenger RNA was already clearly depicted as the intermediate carrier of information between genes and proteins in Jacob \& Monod's gene regulatory network. The exact code through which the sequence of mRNA translates to proteins was deciphered soon after using clever "forward" experiments: Marshall Nirenberg, in the lab of Philip Leder, fed synthetic RNA homopolymer repeats (e.g. UUU\textsubscript{n}, AAA\textsubscript{n}, ...) to cell-free protein synthesis systems in which one of the 20 possible amino-acids were radioactively labeled. The detection of a radioactive protein product then revealed the identity of the amino-acid for which the input homopolymer coded\sjcitep[.]{nirenbergRNACodewordsProtein1964} Har Gobind Khorana combined more complex RNA trinucleotide repeat polymers (e.g. ACA\textsubscript{n}, UAG\textsubscript{n}, ...) with chemical analysis of the resulting proteins to decipher the remaining possible 3-nucleotide codons, producing a finalised code in 1966\sjcitep[.]{khoranaPolynucleotideSynthesisGenetic1966}

Biology was shifting from mutant models to molecular methods. Precise extraction and purification of specific proteins or other macromolecules began to take a center stage. [[later on: quantification]] For example, in 1966 Gilbert and M{\"u}ller-Hill for the first time purified lac repressor protein fractions, confirming Jacob and Monod's theoretical model of a proteins acting as repressors[.]{gilbert1966} A year later, in a letter aptly titled \textit{The Lac Operator is DNA}, the pair showed that the the Lac repressor specifically binds to DNA extracted from \textlambda80 phage carrying the lac operon, and that this interaction could be disrupted by exposing the complex to IPTG. 

While codons had been de-coded, a "reverse" experiment in which the sequence of a given DNA or RNA molecule could be sequenced remained elusive. The lab of Walter Fiers (Ghent University) was the first to do so in 1972 by reporting a complete sequence of the bacteriophage MS2 coat protein gene\sjcitep[.]{jouNucleotideSequenceGene1972} Fiers first digested the phage's \textsuperscript{32}P-labeled RNA genome using ribonuclease T\textsubscript{1}, which cuts RNA at G positions, and iteratively separated the resulting fragments based on size and charge through multiple rounds of two-dimensional fractionation. Joël Vandekerckhove and Marc Van Montagu (both also Ghent University) had simultaneously been working on determining the amino-acid sequence of the MS2 coat protein\sjcitep[.]{vandekerckhoveUseFluorescamineSequence1973} Under the guidance of Van Montagu's preliminary amino-acid map and the Nirenberg-Khorana codon dictionary, Fiers and colleagues were able to puzzle together their fragments into a near-complete base sequence of the MS2 coat protein gene.

Finally, in 1973,  Gilbert \& Maxam sequenced the lac operon, confirming Jacob \& Monod's model of gene regulation\sjcitep[.]{gilbert1973nucleotide} Restriction enzymes with known cut sites were used to digest a purified RNA copy of the operon, and the fragments were separated on a two-dimensional PAGE developed by none other than Fiers and De Wachter\sjcitep{dewachter1972}. Fragment lengths generated by different restriction enzymes could be used to infer the 27 basepair-long sequence: \textit{5'--TGG\textbf{AATTGT}G\textbf{A}G\textbf{C}G\textbf{G}A\textbf{T}A\textbf{ACAATT} 3-3'}. Gilbert and Maxam remarked that the palindromic symmetry in this short motif could imply that the sequence is bound by a dimer or tetramer of lac repressor proteins.

In 1977, Maxam and Gilbert published a DNA sequencing method \sjcitep[,]{maxamGilbert1977} which relied on comparing the 4 electrophoresis ladders generated by chemically cleaving the DNA of interest at G, C, G+A or C+T sites.  Soon after, Fredrick Sanger, who had developed the two-dimensional fractionation used by Fiers\sjcitep[,]{sangerTwodimensionalFractionationProcedure1965} and had been awarded the Nobel prize for the amino-acid sequence of insulin in 1958, succeeded in deterministically sequencing the entire DNA genome of bacteriophage \textphi X174 in 1977\sjcitep[.]{sangerDNASequencingChainterminating1977} Earlier work had shown that dideoxy variants of the four common nucleotides (A, C, G \& T) would inhibit further polymerisation when included into the newly synthesised DNA strand due to the absence of a reactive phosphate. Sanger devised that a polymerisation reaction performed in a mixture of the four common nucleotides combined with a single dideoxy variant will produce a mixture of strands terminated at each position of the dideoxy variant. Sanger further reasoned that performing this reaction for all four dideoxy variants in parallel should yield all the positions of each of the four bases (\autoref{fig:sangersequencing}). Indeed, when applied to fragments of \textphi X174 DNA, the entire phage's genome (5386 basepairs) could be reconstructed. \section{Further lifting the veil}
In hindsight, several pieces of the gene regulatory puzzle had now been discovered:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
	\item Genetic information is stored in the base sequence of a cell's DNA, and DNA can be sequenced using Sanger's method.
	\item This information is transferred to proteins according to a well-defined codon "dictionary" through messenger RNA. Transcription of DNA to RNA is thus a crucial process of information transfer.
	\item The resulting proteins are capable of influencing transcription and thus the behaviour of the cell.
\end{enumerate}

However, mechanistic and molecular insights in \textit{how} genes might be regulated was still sparse. Several breakthroughs came from the lab of Robert Roeder. Roeder, who as a student had purified the polymerase enzymes that catalyze the transcription of messenger RNA from DNA in 1969\sjcitep[,]{roederMultipleFormsDNAdependent1969} showed that purified RNA polymerases could only initiate the transcription of DNA templates in the presence of uncharacterised cellular protein extracts. This finding confirmed earlier ideas that additional, then-unknown factors were necessary to initiate the transcription reaction\sjcitep[.]{ngTranscriptionClonedXenopus1979} Several such initiation factors were purified and characterised within the next year\sjcitep[,]{matsuiMultipleFactorsAre1980} and it soon transpired that a large number of different factors are necessary for transcription to occur.

Parallel research in Roeder's lab led to the discovery of a protein which induced expression of the 5S ribosomal RNA gene in \textit{Xenopus} (clawed frog) egg cells. This factor, later named Transcription Factor III (\textit{TFIIIa}), was the first protein shown to initiate transcription of a \textit{specific} gene in eukaryotes, and not just initiate transcription in general. Roeder and his student Takashi Matsui suggested that \textit{TFIIIa} might \textit{"recognize a unique region of DNA"}, a hypothesis which was explored and demonstrated soon after\sjcitep[.]{engelkeSpecificInteractionPurified1980} However, as part of the ribosomal body, 5S rRNA plays a role in cellular protein synthesis, and is ubiquitously present in all cells across all known organisms. Transcription factors involved in more specialised processes were identified and characterised relatively quickly: examples include the glucocorticoid receptor \textit{Gr}\sjcitep[,]{payvarPurifiedGlucocorticoidReceptors1981} \textit{Sp1}\sjcitep[,]{dynanIsolationTranscriptionFactors1983} and \textit{Gal4}\sjcitep[,]{bramSpecificProteinBinding1985} important transcription factors which are still under scrutiny to this day. In fact, it became clear that some of these factors were extremely powerful: their ectopic expression could convert one cell type to another\sjcitep[,]{davisExpressionSingleTransfected1987} and later specific combinations were found to even revert fully differentiated skin cells back to stem cells\sjcitep[.]{takahashiInductionPluripotentStem2006} By this time, the scientific realised that the factors responsible for the activity of Spemann \& Mangold's organizer were transcription factors, and several of these factors were identified shortly after\sjcitep[.]{choMolecularNatureSpemann1991, lambNeuralInductionSecreted1993}

The human genome project started in 1985 as a joint academic effort between the US National Institutes of Health. Surprisingly, the US Department of Energy also played a major role, with Lawrence Livermore, Los Alamos and Lawrence Berkeley labs providing extensive support in computational analysis and data storage. The ties between DNA, cancer, radiation damage and the immune system were also known already. The project was first expected to take 15 years and cost ~\$3 billion dollars. Early efforts focused on a hierarchical approach: the genome was broken into fragments (~200kbp) and cloned into bacterial artificial chromosomes (BACs). These BACs were then linked into large contigs using a variety of techniques (mainly sequence tagging sites PCR and restriction enzyme fingerprinting), which were in turn mapped to entire chromosomes. Individual BAC clones were then further fragmeted into smaller pieces (1000bp) which could be sequenced via the Sanger method. A number of problems made this approach a slow process: repetitive DNA regions and coverage gaps and made the assembly of contigs difficult, and Sanger's dideoxy method was incredibly labour intensive. Luckily, automation soon followed. One company played a particularly important role: ABI, first with the ABI Prism 373 and 377 automated slab gel electrophoresis analyzers, then with the ABI Prism 310 capilarry gel electrophoresis, and finally the ABI 3700 96-plex capillary gel electrophoresis analyzer (1998), which became the workhorse of the human genome project. \section{Next generation sequencing}

Sequencing by synthesis (SBS) was developed by Balasubramanian and Klenerman at Cambridge in 1998. The technology works by hybridizing millions of DNA templates to an oligo "lawn" on a glass flow cell, where each individual template is amplified to generate a cluster of clones. Each cluster is then sequenced by sequentially adding fluorescently labeled nucleotides and recording where they are incorporated.

Each incorporation cycle is imaged, generating imaging data of the oligo lawn. Image processing is used to determine the position of oligo clusters present on the lawn, and these are stored in \verb|.locs| (locations) files. The basecalling algorithm will combine these \verb|.locs| files together with the imaging data to extract fluorescence intensities for all colours used per cluster. Dominant fluorescent signals then determine the base called for each cluster for every cycle.  When no base can be called, \verb|N| is used. When a strand does not incorporate a base during a given cycle, or if it leads ahead by one base, it becomes out of phase with the other strands of that cluster for the rest of the sequencing run. This is called phasing/pre-phasing and is corrected by the basecalling algorithm. Theoretical phasing rates (approximately 0.1\% of strands in a given cluster per cycle) can be used to estimate the contribution of phasing noise and deconvolve true signal from phased noise. Each basecall is also assigned a "Phred" quality score \verb|Q| based on signal-to-noise ratio, phasing, and separation of dense clusters: \( Q = -10 \cdot \log_{10} (P) \) where \verb|P| is the probability of the basecall being correct. A Q score of 30 corresponds to a probability of error of 1 in 1000.  The result is a binary \verb|.bcl| file, which contains the basecalls for each cluster. The binary \verb|.bcl| file is then converted to the ASCII \verb|.fastq| format using \verb|bcl2fastq|.

Each sequenced strand is represented by four lines in the \verb|.fastq| file. The first line is a header, which stores metadata such as sequencer and cluster coordinates. The second line is the sequence itself, followed by a separator (\verb|+|) on the third line, and the ASCII-encoded quality score on the fourth line.

Once data is sequenced, and raw \verb|.fastq| data is available, the first step in data analysis is alignment to a reference genome. A number of algorithms exist for this task, and the optimal choice depends on several factors. Short-read aligners such as \verb|bowtie2| are optimized for reads < \SI{200}{\bp}, while long-read aligners like \verb|minimap2| are better at handling reads > \SI{1000}{\bp}. The type of sequencing technology used also plays a role: sequencing by synthesis (Illumina) generates more indels due to incorporation errors, which are explicitly corrected for by \verb|bwa-mem|. \verb|minimap2| is better suited for handling the higher single-base error rates found in Nanopore/PacBio reads. When sequencing transcriptomic data, RNA-seq specific aligners like \verb|STAR| and \verb|HISAT2| are used to handle spliced reads. Like many classes of algorithms, a trade-off between memory and compute can also be made, for example \verb|STAR| requires large amounts of memory due to its use of an uncompressed genome index, but is faster, while \verb|HISAT2| is more memory-efficient, but takes longer.

The diversity in sequencing technologies and alignment algorithms led to the use of several independent alignment formats, each tied to specific sequencing technologies or alignment algorithms. Analyses on different datasets (e.g. variant calling, genotype analysis) required cross parsing or conversion. The introduction of the Sequence Alignment Map (\verb|SAM|) format as part of the 1000 Genomes Project in 2009\sjcitep[,]{liSequenceAlignmentMapFormat2009} provided a unified standard for storing aligned sequencing data which has since become the de facto standard. Each read is stored in a single line containing 11 mandatory fields including: read name, reference name (chromosome or contig), position, mapping quality, the actual sequence, several other alignment quality parameters as well as optional tags.

Before the availability of next-generation sequencing technology, studying the genome and gene expression at scale relied heavily on microarrays. Here, nucleic acid samples are hybridized to pre-designed DNA probes arrayed on a glass slide, and fluorescence intensity measures response levels of each probe. Microarrays were, and still are, used to quantify RNA transcripts\sjcitep[,]{schenaQuantitativeMonitoringGene1995} genotype samples using SNP arrays\sjcitep[,]{wang1998large} and even measure DNA-protein interactions using ChIP-on-chip\sjcitep[.]{ren2000genome} While microarrays allow for up to ~\num{50000} probes to be interrogated in a single experiment, limited dynamic range, high false-positive hybridisation and heterogeneous probe hybridisation capacities skew absolute quantification. The probe set also requires intelligent design and thus cannot discover novel sequences. Probeset size is particularly troubling for ChIP-on-Chip, since genome binning limits resolution to around 500-1000 bp - too low for novel TF motif discovery.

Large scale, distributed application of microarray technologies by 35 individual research groups culminated in a first draft of the Encyclopedia of DNA Elements (ENCODE)in 2007\sjcitep[,]{ENCODE_Project_Consortium2007-zd} a careful annotation of 30 MBs - around 1\% - of the human genome. The release of this draft was the last project of such scale that did not use next generation sequencing. Indeed, by the mid 2000s, the availability of the human genome as a public resource and the falling cost of NGS significantly accelerated cataloging efforts. One of the first steps was the transition from microarrays to sequencing-based quantitative assays, beginning with histone methylation ChIP-seq in 2007. By sequencing immunoprecipitated DNA directly, methylation could now be assessed genome-wide and with fewer quantitative biases. Performing this technique for a spectrum of 20 different lys/arg histone methylation ChIP-seq variants on CD4\textsuperscript{+} T cells, and correlating the annotations with GNF SymAtlas gene expression microarray data allowed genome-wide classification and discovery of functional elements (active/inactive/poised promoters, enhancers, gene bodies, CTCF insulators)\sjcitep[.]{barskiHighresolutionProfilingHistone2007} The distribution of these methylation signatures were then systematically correlated to disease-associated regions, for example enrichment of H3K4Me3 enrichment near canonical sites of chromosome breakpoints in T cell cancers. A comparison of histone methylation in mouse embryonic stem cells, neural progenitor cells and embryonic fibroblasts revealed transitory methylation changes in the differentiation from ES to NPCs\sjcitep[.]{mikkelsen2007genome} In parallel, ChIP-seq was being applied to study individual transcription factors at an improved resolution of around 50 base pairs. An early application of ChIP-seq on neuron-restrictive silencer factor (NRSF) in Jurkat T-cells showed that this factor can also bind "weakly" to altered, non-canonical motifs dependent on local chromatin state (methylation, accessibility) and co-factor motif context\sjcitep[.]{johnsongenomewidemappingvivo2007} These findings contradicted earlier ideas of stringent binding to only canonical motifs, confirmed by SELEX and microarray studies, a rejection made possible only through the broadness of NGS. NGS also improved dynamic range compared to microarrays, showing for example that STAT1 motif enrichment was strongly correlated to absolute ChIP-seq peak height\sjcitep[.]{robertsonGenomewideProfilesSTAT12007}

While systematic application of ChIP-seq refined models of gene regulation, the technique still required a strong antibody and prior knowledge of the protein or histone modification of interest. In contrast, DNase I hypersensitivity assays work by exposing chromatin to DNase extracting exposed regions of chromatin. DHS had already been applied for genome-wide accessibility studies, first by southern blot\sjcitep[,]{wu1980heat} then capillary Sanger sequencing\sjcitep[,]{crawford2004identifying} and later microarrays\sjcitep[.]{crawford2006dnase}. The first DHS maps obtained by sequencing (DNase-seq) emerged in 2009, identifying 6.4M cleavage sites and \num{4384} high-confidence DHSs in \textit{S. cerevisiae}\sjcitep[.]{hesselberth2009global}. A study involving coupled DNase-seq and ChIP-seq of the glucocorticoid receptor (GR) in murine mammary adenocarcinoma revealed that out of \num{8236} GR binding sites, 95\% were accessible before dexamethasone treatment\sjcitep[.]{john2011chromatin} These findings rejected prior notions that GR might act as a pioneer factor, remodeling inaccessible chromatin and exposing its preferred motif after hormone binding. Moreover, pituitary gland cells shared only 11.4\% of these binding sites, further reinforcing that prior chromatin state drives selection of GR binding sites, explaining how differential chromatin context underlies differential responses to the same hormone. NGS-driven DNase-seq studies offered a genomic resolution of up to 1 base pair. Unsurprisingly, the ENCODE's second draft included DNAse-seq data from 125 tissues, along with H3K4me3 and TF ChIP-seq and bisulfite sequencing\sjcitep[.]{thurman2012accessible,encode2012} 2.9 million DHSs were identified and annotated genome-wide, covering almost all previously known regulatory element candidates. 94.4\% of TF binding sites known from ENCODE ChIP-seq data were found to fall within these sites, and only \num{3692} such sites were found in all cell types assessed, suggesting that chromatin accessibility and downstream gene regulation are robust and highly cell-type specific processes. Moreover, combination of these new data types with GWAS confirmed that many disease-related SNPs are located in regulatory regions of the genome.

iRegulon\sjcitep[]{janky2014iregulon} provides a computational alternative for identifying master regulators when experimental TF binding data is unavailable. First, a genome-wide assessment of motif enrichment near TSSs is generated by scoring occurrence of \num{10000} motif PWMs aggregated from various databases using ClusterBuster\sjcitep[]{frith2003cluster}, generating for each PWM a ranking of the genes it may regulate. Applying this approach to validated gene sets from 115 ENCODE ChIP-seq datasets returned 94 correct TF hits, and was able to identify 307 direct p53 target genes using ENCODE ChIP-seq data.

The barcode-rank plot (also referred to as "knee or elbow plot") visualises the distribution of reads across all sequenced barcodes in the sequencing dataset. It is obtained by counting the number of reads per barcode, and ranking each barcode in a log-log figure - similarly in concept to a Zipf or Lorenz curve. If the dataset is sequenced to near-saturation, the total number of barcodes will exceed the whitelist, as sequencing/PCR/synthesis errors introduce non-whitelisted species. In the "ideal signal" case, all reads are evenly distributed across a small number of barcodes that matches the number of input cells (e.g. \num{10000} in the figure). This means that there are no quality differences between real cells, and there are no barcodes with low read counts. The opposite scenario ("ideal noise") has an even distribution of all reads across \textit{all} barcodes, and we cannot distinguish cells from noise. Real datasets fall between these two extremes. A high quality dataset exhibits a clear cut-off, where the number of reads per barcode sharply drops off in the transition between real cells and background noise. A dataset of lower quality will exhibit a more gradual transition between real cells and background noise. This can be caused by a variety of mechanisms, notably ambient RNA or non-pure cell barcodes on the beads.

cisTopic also introduces a region score which rewards regions that are more specific to a topic, and penalises regions that are ubiquitous across topics:

\[
\text{Region score} = \beta_{r,t} \left( \log \beta_{r,t} - \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log \beta_{r,t} \right)
\]

Consider Shannon entropy $H(X) = -\sum_{i} p(x_i) \log p(x_i)$, then the term \(\beta_{r,t} \log \beta_{r,t}\) can be considered the local information content of the region in topic context, while the term \(\frac{\beta_{r,t}}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log \beta_{r,t}\) is the average log-probability of the region acrosss all topics [double check this, is beta a probability?]. The region score is thus an average-normalized measure of a given region's information content, indicating how specific it is to each topic. Topics can then be scored for TF motif enrichment by counting TF motif occurrence in the score-ranked regions and calculating the area under this rank curve (AUCell\sjcitep[]{aibarSCENICSingleCell2017,aibarSCENICSingleCell2017}).

cisTopic's LDA approach was benchmarked against other popular tools such as chromVAR, scABC, BROCKMAN, SCRAT, Cicero, and LSI, using both simulated single-cell ATAC-seq datasets and real data from FAC-sorted hematopoietic cells. cisTopic typically yielded higher adjusted Rand index (ARI) scores for cell-type clustering, especially in low-coverage scenarios, confirming that the method can more robustly capture biologically meaningful patterns in sparse single-cell ATAC-seq data.

Hubel and Wiesel showed that specific neurons in a cat's visual cortex respond to common spatial patterns (e.g. edges, corners, orientations) in specific locations within the cat's visual field. These patterns can overlap when layers of neurons pass information onwards, forming broader structures which ultimately allow the cat to recognise complex objects. This idea was borrowed by Fukishima and later LeCun to implement convolutional neural networks (CNNs) for handwritten digit recognition. Rasterized (vectorized) images are scanned by a "filter", converting the original data into a new representation. This new representation is then passed on to the next layer, where it is again scanned by a new filter, and so on until the final layer. CNNs are thus well suited for detecting spatial patterns, and integrating these patterns into larger, more global structures. The same logic is applied in [ref], where a CNN is trained to detect local patterns in DNA (cell-type specific peaks). In order to train a CNN on DNA sequences (peaks), DNA sequences were one-hot encoded (each position is encoded by a binary vector of length 4, and the sequence is represented as a matrix of these vectors of size $N \times L \times 4$). where $N$ is the number of sequences, $L$ is the sequence length (500 bp), and the nucleotides are one-hot encoded as binary vectors.

The convolution operation for filter $k$ is:
\[
z_{i,k} = f \left( \sum_{j=1}^{h} \sum_{m=1}^{4} X_{i+j-1, m} W_{j, m}^{(k)} + b_k \right)

After pooling, the flattened feature map $\mathbf{P}$ is mapped to outputs as:
\[
\mathbf{\hat{Y}} = \sigma \left( \mathbf{P} \mathbf{V} + \mathbf{b} \right)

\]
with the total loss:

\mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{N \cdot C} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{j=1}^{C} \mathcal{L}_{ij}
