\chapter{\nohyphens{Molecular Tools}}
This introductory chapter will attempt to build the foundation for the explorations that follow by providing a historical overview of chromatin accessibility based on historical accounts\sjcitep[,]{slackConradHalWaddington2002, olinsChromatinHistoryOur2003, cobbWhoDiscoveredMessenger2015, deichmannChromatinItsHistory2015, deichmannEpigeneticsOriginsEvolution2016, lis50YearHistory2019, roeder50YearsEukaryotic2019} and frame it within the ongoing research on decoding gene regulation. Then, we illustrate the state of the art of single-cell chromatin sequencing technologies when I began this work, and delve into the capabilities and limitations of several breakthrough techniques. This context serves to highlight the importance of the subject matter, and to motivate our subsequent discussions and findings.

\section{Early research}
The word "cell" was first used in 1665 by Robert Hooke to describe the hexagonal walls he observed in a thin slice of cork through a self-made microscope\sjcitep[.]{hookeMicrographia1665} It took several more centuries of research until the concept of cells as the foundational building blocks of life began to take shape. Between 1817 and 1826, Baltic-German polymath and explorer Karl Ernst von Baer, made the first accounts of egg cells, development of the blastula, the notochord and the three germ layers. Only in 1839, Theodor Schwann proposed that animals, just like plants, were composed of cells\sjcitep[,]{schwannMikroskopischeUntersuchungenUber1839} and Rudolf Virchow and Robert Remak later added that cells were the product of division of pre-existing cells\sjcitep[.]{rudolfCellularpathologieIhrerBegrundung1858} While studying this process of division in 1879, Walther Flemming first used the term "chromatin" to describe the filament-like threads between two dividing cells in 1879\sjcitep[.]{flemmingBeitrageZurKenntniss1879} Flemming's observations did not differ much from his colleague's Wilhelm Waldeyer's description of "chromosomes"\sjcitep[.]{deichmannChromatinItsHistory2015} These early findings directly stemmed from the technological refinement of microscopic lenses, which had by now become accurate enough to permit detailed studies of developing embryos. This early area of cell biology produced a number of fundamental questions that would engage the minds of biologists to this day:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
	\item How can a single fertilised egg cell divide and self-organise into an entire organism?
	\item Where is the information that steers this process encoded, and how is it interpreted?
	\item How can this process go wrong, leading to disease, and can we manipulate it? \footnotetextwithoutmarker{\sjcitep[]{tagarelliHansSpemannOne2004}Tagarelli, \textit{Anat, Hist, Embr}, 2004}\hspace{-2pt} % this stupid footnote is here because this reference is first cited in the X ray crystallography wrapfigure environment. At that time, the concept of physical store and transfer of genetic information was not yet widely accepted, and the function and identity of Flemming and Waldeyer's chromosomes was still unknown. In search of answers, marine embryologist Thomas Hunt Morgan began studying inheritance in \textit{Drosophila melanogaster} (fruit fly) in 1908. After years of breeding flies, a mutant with white eyes (aptly named \textit{white}) appeared. Through careful tracking, Morgan discovered that these pigmentless eyes followed a sex-dependent inheritance pattern which could be reliably predicted across generations\sjcitep[.]{morganSexLimitedInheritance1910} More than half a century earlier, Moravian friar Gregor Mendel had demonstrated the exact same principles in \textit{Pisum sativum} (sweet pea), but his work was initially ignored and later disputed by the scientific community upon its rediscovery in 1900. By 1909 however, Danish botanist Wilhelm Johannsen had experimentally repeated much of Mendel's work, and coined the term "gene" to describe the abstract "unit of heredity" that is used to pass on traits between generations\sjcitep[.]{johannsen1909erblichkeitslehre}

Morgan, together with his undergraduate student Alfred Sturtevan, discovered that co-inheritance of genes also followed reproducible patterns ("linkage analysis")\sjcitep[.]{sturtevant1913} This finding supported the idea that genes were located on a linear carrier. A second breakthrough came when Morgan demonstrated that the chromosomes observed by Flemming and Waldeyer were in fact the physical bodies responsible for the transfer of phenotypic traits\sjcitep[.]{morganRandomSegregationCoupling1911} Armed with experimental evidence gathered from thousands of generations of fruit flies, Morgan formally unified the theory of Mendelian inheritance with the physical transfer of genes on chromosomes in 1915\sjcitep[.]{morganMechanismMendelianHeredity1915}

Together, Morgan's findings inaugurated the 20\textsuperscript{th} century's era of genetics, and defined the context for many of the major discoveries that ensued. Hermann Muller, for example, discovered the mutagenic effects of irradiation in \textit{Drosophila}\sjcitep[,]{Muller1927} accelerating the study of mutations\sjcitep[.]{muller1930} In fact, Muller showed that relocation of genes, inferred through Morgan and Sturtevan's linkage analysis, could alter their "variegation" (expression), a very early understanding of cis gene regulation!

In parallel, the field of embryology began to consolidate. Early views such as "preformationism", the idea that all parts of the body were present in miniature form in the embryo at conception, were defeated when Hans Spemann began constricting dividing \textit{Triturus} (newt) embryos. Both halves of the embryo could develop into full, though smaller, animals \textit{if} part of the dorsal part were present. Later, Spemann and his student Hilde Mangold, discovered that transplantation of the dorsal lip from one embryo to the ventral side of another embryo would induce the formation of a ventrally conjoined twin (\autoref{fig:spemannmangold})\sjcitep[.]{spemannUberInduktionEmbryonenanlagen1924} Importantly, the pair showed that the conjoined secondary embryo consisted of tissue from the \textit{host}, indicating that the transplant merely instructed its host surroundings to change developmental fate. Spemann and Mangold later named this steering tissue the "organizer field", and multiple labs attempted (unsuccessfully) to purify and characterise the compounds responsible for these processes in an "organizer gold rush"\sjcitep[.]{slackConradHalWaddington2002}

However, the link between embryonic development and the "new" insights in genetics was not readily made, even disputed by parts of the scientific community. This sentiment began to change after a young Conrad Waddington, who had previously been performing organizer grafting experiments in mammals and birds, visited Morgan's fly lab in 1935. Here, Waddington discovered that specific heritable genetic mutations could disrupt \textit{Drosophila} wing development at specific stages\sjcitep[.]{waddingtonGeneticControlWing1940} Based on these findings, Waddington wrote his seminal work \textit{Organisers and Genes} in 1940, and introduced the notion that embryonic development was a large decision tree under the control of genes, which he metaphorically described as a "landscape"\sjcitep[.]{waddingtonOrganisersGenes1940, waddingtonStrategyGenes1957} A ball, representing a cell's current state, metaphorically rolls down the furrows in the landscape, passing several bifurcations which ultimately determine its fate (\autoref{fig:waddington_landscape}a). Beneath the surface, a series of tension wires, symbolizing genes, forcefully pull the surface into shape (\autoref{fig:waddington_landscape}b). Disruptions in an organism's genetic context, for example by mutation, could alter the landscape and thus the cell's final state.

In fact, the storage of genetic information was attributed to proteins at the time, as they made up a much larger portion of the chromosomal dry mass than nucleic acids (DNA, RNA), and were known to be more complex in composition. Phoebus Levene, who had identified adenine, guanine, thymine, cytosine, deoxyribose-phosphate backbone as the basic components of DNA\sjcitep[.]{levene1919structure} Nucleic acids were thought to be only of structural importance to chromosomes, akin to the highly repetitive polymers found in cell walls and plant fibers. This notion was overturned in 1944, when Avery, MacLeod and McCarty demonstrated that it was transfer of acids, and not proteins, purified from a non-virulent strain of bacteria that could "transform" a virulent  strain to the non-virulent strain\sjcitep[.]{griffithSignificancePneumococcalTypes1928,averyStudiesChemicalNature1944} Despite uncertainties about the physical nature of genetic information, awareness of the link between disease phenotypes, proteins and genes began to rise. For example sickle cell anemia, which was known to follow a Mendelian, heterozygous-recessive inheritence pattern, was shown to be caused by an aberrant form of hemoglobin in red blood cells. Since hemoglobin is a protein, and people with the trait had a 50:50 mix, the most logical explanation was that the gene must encode the structure of the protein\sjcitep[,]{pauling1949sickle} for the first time showing \textit{"a clear case of a change produced in a protein molecule by an allelic change in a single gene"}.

A major breakthrough came when Watson, Crick and Franklin resolved DNA's double-helix structure using X-ray crystallography\sjcitep[,]{watsonMolecularStructureNucleic1953} and for the first time suggested that \textit{"the precise sequence of the bases is the code which carries the genetical information"}\sjcitep[.]{,watsonGeneticalImplicationsStructure1953} It had thus become clear that life stores information in the nucleotide sequence of DNA, and the scientific community began to prepare experiments that would leverage this concept.

\section{The early molecular era}
Although Morgan and Waddington's visions of genes as decision makers for a cell's behaviour were largely theoretical, they were soon substantiated. Jacques Monod, also a former student of Thomas Hunt Morgan, observed that bacteria grown in a medium containing both glucose and lactose would preferentially feed on glucose, and would only begin feeding on lactose once the other had run out. Further research together with Fran\c{c}ois Jacob showed that the production of \textbeta-galactosidase, the enzyme responsible for this metabolic switch, was under the control of a "repressor", which was inactivated by the presence of lactose. Together, the pair published these findings in 1961\sjcitep[,]{jacobGeneticRegulatoryMechanisms1961} in an article which features the first diagram of a \textit{gene regulatory network} (\autoref{fig:lac_repressor}). The Jacob-Monod diagram also explicitly showed the repressor as a protein originating from a gene. Genes thus (indirectly) controlled other genes, and therefore the behaviour of the cell - exactly how Conrad Waddington had prophesised twenty years prior.

Earlier that month, Jacob, together with Sidney Brenner, had also proposed the existence of an unstable form of RNA which acted as a "messenger" to transfer information from DNA-based genes to proteins\sjcitep[.]{brennerUnstableIntermediateCarrying1961} This messenger RNA was already clearly depicted as the intermediate carrier of information between genes and proteins in Jacob \& Monod's gene regulatory network. The exact code through which the sequence of mRNA translates to proteins was deciphered soon after using clever "forward" experiments: Marshall Nirenberg fed synthetic RNA homopolymer repeats (e.g. UUU\textsubscript{n}, AAA\textsubscript{n}, ...) to cell-free protein synthesis systems in which one of the 20 possible amino-acids were radioactively labeled. The detection of a radioactive protein product then revealed the identity of the amino-acid for which the input homopolymer coded\sjcitep[.]{nirenbergRNACodewordsProtein1964} Har Gobind Khorana combined more complex RNA trinucleotide repeat polymers (e.g. ACA\textsubscript{n}, UAG\textsubscript{n}, ...) with chemical analysis of the resulting proteins to decipher the remaining possible 3-nucleotide codons, producing a finalised code cipher in 1966\sjcitep[.]{khoranaPolynucleotideSynthesisGenetic1966} These experiments together confirmed how genes encoded for proteins.

Biology was shifting from mutant models to molecular methods. Precise extraction and purification of specific proteins or other macromolecules began to take a center stage. For example, in 1966 Gilbert and M{\"u}ller-Hill for the first time purified \textit{lac} repressor protein fractions, confirming Jacob and Monod's theoretical model of a proteins acting as repressors[.]{gilbert1966} A year later, in a letter aptly titled \textit{The Lac Operator is DNA}\sjcitep[]{gilbert1967} the pair showed that the the \textit{lac} repressor specifically binds to DNA extracted from \textlambda80 phage carrying the \textit{lac} operon, and that this interaction could be disrupted by exposing the complex to IPTG.

While codons had been de-coded, a "reverse" experiment in which the sequence of a given DNA or RNA molecule could be sequenced remained elusive. Walter Fiers (Ghent University) was the first to do so in 1972 by reporting a complete sequence of the bacteriophage MS2 coat protein gene\sjcitep[.]{jouNucleotideSequenceGene1972} Fiers first digested the phage's \textsuperscript{32}P-labeled RNA genome using ribonuclease T\textsubscript{1}, which cuts RNA at G positions, and iteratively separated the resulting fragments based on size and charge through multiple rounds of two-dimensional fractionation. Jo\"{e}l Vandekerckhove and Marc Van Montagu (both also Ghent University) had simultaneously been working on determining the amino-acid sequence of the MS2 coat protein\sjcitep[.]{vandekerckhoveUseFluorescamineSequence1973} Under the guidance of Van Montagu's preliminary amino-acid map and the Nirenberg-Khorana codon dictionary, Fiers and colleagues were able to puzzle together their fragments into a near-complete base sequence of the MS2 coat protein gene.

Finally, in 1973,  Maxam \& Gilbert sequenced the \textit{lac} operon, fully cementing Jacob \& Monod's early model of gene regulation\sjcitep[.]{gilbert1973nucleotide} Restriction enzymes with known cut sites were used to digest a purified RNA copy of the operon, and the fragments were separated on a two-dimensional PAGE developed by Fiers and De Wachter\sjcitep[.]{dewachter1972} Fragment lengths generated by different restriction enzymes could be used to infer the 27 basepair-long sequence: \textit{5'--TGG\textbf{AATTGT}G\textbf{A}G\textbf{C}G\textbf{G}A\textbf{T}A\textbf{ACAATT} 3-3'}. Gilbert and Maxam remarked that the palindromic symmetry in this short motif could imply that the sequence is bound by a dimer or tetramer of lac repressor proteins. Later, Maxam and Gilbert published a DNA sequencing method \sjcitep[,]{maxamGilbert1977} which relied on comparing the 4 electrophoresis ladders generated by chemically cleaving the DNA of interest at G, C, G+A or C+T sites. The first exhaustive DNA sequencing method was thus a direct consequence of gene regulation research. Soon after, Fredrick Sanger, who had developed the two-dimensional fractionation used by Fiers\sjcitep[,]{sangerTwodimensionalFractionationProcedure1965} and had already been awarded the Nobel prize for the amino-acid sequence of insulin in 1958, succeeded in deterministically sequencing the entire DNA genome of bacteriophage \textphi X174 in 1977\sjcitep[.]{sangerDNASequencingChainterminating1977} Earlier work had shown that dideoxy variants of the four common nucleotides (A, C, G \& T) would inhibit further polymerisation when included into the newly synthesised DNA strand due to the absence of a reactive phosphate. Sanger devised that a polymerisation reaction performed in a mixture of the four common nucleotides combined with a single dideoxy variant will produce a mixture of strands terminated at each position of the dideoxy variant. Sanger further reasoned that performing this reaction for all four dideoxy variants in parallel should yield all the positions of each of the four bases (\autoref{fig:sangersequencing}). Indeed, when applied to fragments of \textphi X174 DNA, the entire phage's genome (5386 basepairs) could be reconstructed. \section{Chromatin}
While the function of chromatin's DNA component as the primary store of information had become clear, the relationship between its protein content and nucleic acids was still under dispute. Alfred and Ellen Stedman investigated different histone fractions between different species and tissues, and proposed that \textit{"the basic proteins of cell nuclei are gene inhibitors, each histone or protamin being capable of suppressing the activities of specific groups of genes"}\sjcitep[.]{stedman1951} This idea was confirmed experimentally when purified protein-free DNA reconstituted with chromatin-purified histone proteins was shown to be inactive in RNA synthesis despite presence of RNA polymerase\sjcitep[.]{huang1962} Further study of this inhibitory capacity found that "that relatively minor modifications of histone structure [acetylation], taking place on the intact protein molecule, offer a means of switching on or off RNA synthesis at different loci along the chromosome" \sjcitep[.]{allfrey1964}

By the early 1970s, biochemists had chemically identified five different histone proteins (H1, H2A, H2B, H3, H4), present at ratios of 0.5:1:1:1:1. Histones H2A and H2B were found to be rich in lysine, and H3 in arginine, positively charged amino acids known to bind DNA through electrostatic attraction\sjcitep[.]{delange1972} X-ray diffraction research suggested a DNA "super-helix" structure stabilized by histone proteins\sjcitep[,]{pardon1972} while others found "thin fibrils" and "nodular elements"\sjcitep[.]{slayter1972} It was however known that staphylococcal nuclease would cleave rat liver DNA into fragments of multiples of 200 base pairs in length, heavily hinting that DNA and histone proteins were organised in discrete units\sjcitep[.]{hewish1973} This hypothesis was strengthened by early electron microscopy, which directly visualised chromatin fibers as linear arrays of particles \textasciitilde{\SI{70}{angstrom}} in diameter resembling "beads on a string"\sjcitep[.]{olins1974spheroid}

Finally, in 1974, Kornberg and Thomas showed that histone proteins H2A and H2B form dimers, and H3 and H4 formed stable tetramers\sjcitep[.]{kornbergChromatinStructureOligomers1974} When mixed with DNA, these dimers and tetramers returned X-ray diffraction spectra identical to native chromatin. Later research with enhanced resolution X-ray diffraction clearly showed a repeating pattern with with a periodicity of \textasciitilde{\SI{100}{angstrom}}\sjcitep[.]{kornbergchromatinstructurerepeating1974a} Kornberg remarked that \textit{"the occurrence of multiples rather than just 200 base pair pieces is presumably due to some cleavage sites being blocked by nonhistone proteins"} - a prescient vision of transcription factors. Histone protein H1 was later identified as a stabilizer, binding to 20-80 bp long DNA links between histone octamers, leading to supercoiling and further chromatin compaction\sjcitep[.]{finch1976} This new "nucleosome" model of chromatin compacts DNA \textasciitilde{7-fold} at the nucleosome level, and further supercoiling explains how mitotic chromosomes achieve a 10 000-fold compaction.

\section{DNA binding proteins}
In hindsight, several pieces of the gene regulatory puzzle had now been discovered:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
	\item Genetic information is stored in the base sequence of a cell's DNA.
	\item This information is transferred to proteins according to a well-defined codon "dictionary" through messenger RNA.
	\item Some proteins, such as the lac repressor, are capable of influencing transcription and thus the behaviour of the cell.
\end{enumerate}

However, mechanistic and molecular insights in \textit{how} genes might be regulated was still sparse. Several breakthroughs came from the lab of Robert Roeder, who had previously purified the polymerase enzymes that catalyze the transcription of messenger RNA from DNA in 1969\sjcitep[.]{roederMultipleFormsDNAdependent1969} His lab showed that purified RNA polymerases could only initiate the transcription of DNA templates in the presence of uncharacterised cellular protein extracts. This finding confirmed earlier ideas that additional, then-unknown factors were necessary to initiate the transcription reaction\sjcitep[.]{ngTranscriptionClonedXenopus1979} Several such initiation factors were purified and characterised within the next year\sjcitep[,]{matsuiMultipleFactorsAre1980} and it soon transpired that a large number of different factors are necessary for transcription to occur. Parallel research in Roeder's lab led to the discovery of a protein which induced expression of the 5S ribosomal RNA gene in \textit{Xenopus} (clawed frog) egg cells. This factor, later named Transcription Factor III (\textit{TFIIIa}), was the first protein shown to initiate transcription of a \textit{specific} gene, rather than genome wide, in eukaryotes.

Roeder suggested that \textit{TFIIIa} might \textit{"recognize a unique region of DNA"}, a hypothesis which was explored and demonstrated soon after when TFIIIa was shown to bind precisely on position 45 to 96 of the 5S gene\sjcitep[.]{engelkeSpecificInteractionPurified1980}

In parallel, understanding of gene regulation was shifting from an exclusively promoter-proximal process (e.g. TFIIIa, TATA-box) to also include promoter-distal elements. Reporter studies by Banerji et al. showed that a 72 bp tandem repeat sequence located 200 bp upstream of the SV40 early promoter, which was known to reduce nearby gene expression when mutated, could drive expression at large distances\sjcitep[.]{banerji1981expression} Constructs containing the 72 bp repeat, \textit{"for convenience referred to as the 'enhancer'"}, showed a 200-fold increase in reporter (\textbeta-globulin) expression, and removal of this specific sequence ablated the effect. Moreover, inversion of the region relative to the reporter gene conserved expression, and this effect remained when inserted at distances of up to \SI{3.3}{\kilo\base}. Finally, co-transfection of separate plasmids - one with the SV40 72 bp enhancer, the other containing the \textbeta-globulin reporter gene - did not show expression, confirming that the SV40 enhancer works in cis only. Further research on the Ig enhancer, which specifically drives expression of \textit{recombined} V(D)J genes only, showed that this enhancer was active only in B-lymphocyte myeloma cells, not in HeLa, 3T6, or mink lung cells - cell-type specific enhancer activity!\sjcitep[]{banerji1983lymphocyte} The group also correctly proposed that the cell-type specific nature of the Ig enhancer owes its effect to cell-type dependent expression of its binding transcription factors, and that that chromosomal translocations could lead to oncogenic expression such as observed in Burkitt's lymphoma.

In parallel with the new discoveries of genomic cis-regulatory elements, an explosion in discovery of protein-DNA-binding mechanisms occurred. Crystallography of {\textlambda} phage (\textit{cI}), \textit{Cro} and \textit{E. coli} \textit{cap}, three known DNA-binding proteins showed the first generalized mechanism of binding: two {\textalpha}-helices joined by a tight turn (HTH motif) generate a sharp nick that fits into the major groove of DNA, leading to sequence-specific - though degenerate and context-specific - binding\sjcitep[.]{pabo1984protein} Simultaneous studies in \textit{D. melanogaster} led to the discovery of the homeodomain, a 60 amino acid DNA-binding domain highly conserved across metazoans, especially those with segmented body plans\sjcitep[.]{mcginnis1984homologous} Later, it was shown that the homeodomain also uses a helix-turn-helix structure, but is significantly longer and complexes DNA differently than the \textlambda HTH\sjcitep[.]{kissinger1990engrailed} Zinc-binding repeating Cys2His2 motifs were further identified in \textit{TFIIIa}, and these zinc-protein complexes were also shown to bind specific DNA motifs\sjcitep[.]{miller1985repetitive, pavletich1991zinc} Next, an amino acid motif conserved across transcription factors \textit{CEBP}, \textit{Myc}, \textit{Fos}, \textit{Jun} and the yeast TF \textit{GCN4} were shown to interdigitate through leucine residues every seventh position ("basic leucine zipper"). This interdigitation allows these factors to dimerize, and bind palindromic DNA motifs through adjacent DNA-recognizing residues\sjcitep[.]{landschulz1988leucine} Finally, basic helic-loop-helix motifs were shown to similarly dimarize, allowing both monomers and hetero/homodimers to recognize and bind the palindromic E-box motif (consensus CANNTG, but ideally CACGTG)\sjcitep[.]{murre1989interactions, ellenberger1994crystal} Structural studies also confirmed that the lac repressor is a tetramer protein capable of binding two operator sites simultaneously, forming DNA loops that repress expression\sjcitep[.]{lewis1996}

\textit{TFIIIa}'s enhanced gene, 5S rRNA, is ubiquitously present in all cells across all known organisms. Transcription factors involved in more specialised, cell-type specific processes were identified and characterised soon after. Examples include the glucocorticoid receptor \textit{Gr}\sjcitep[,]{payvarPurifiedGlucocorticoidReceptors1981} \textit{Sp1}\sjcitep[,]{dynanIsolationTranscriptionFactors1983} and \textit{Gal4}\sjcitep[,]{bramSpecificProteinBinding1985} factors which are still under scrutiny to this day. The human genome project started in 1985 as a joint academic effort between the US National Institutes of Health. Surprisingly, the US Department of Energy also played a major role, with Lawrence Livermore, Los Alamos and Lawrence Berkeley labs providing extensive support in computational analysis and data storage. The ties between DNA, cancer, radiation damage and the immune system were also known already. The project was first expected to take 15 years and cost ~\$3 billion dollars. Early efforts focused on a hierarchical approach: the genome was broken into fragments (~200kbp) and cloned into bacterial artificial chromosomes (BACs). These BACs were then linked into large contigs using a variety of techniques (mainly sequence tagging sites PCR and restriction enzyme fingerprinting), which were in turn mapped to entire chromosomes. Individual BAC clones were then further fragmeted into smaller pieces (1000bp) which could be sequenced via the Sanger method. A number of problems made this approach a slow process: repetitive DNA regions and coverage gaps and made the assembly of contigs difficult, and Sanger's dideoxy method was incredibly labour intensive. Luckily, automation soon followed. One company played a particularly important role: ABI, first with the ABI Prism 373 and 377 automated slab gel electrophoresis analyzers, then with the ABI Prism 310 capillary gel electrophoresis, and finally the ABI 3700 96-plex capillary gel electrophoresis analyzer (1998), which became the workhorse of the human genome project. This machine could sequence \textit{thousands} of base pairs per day.

\section{From human genome to sequencing assays}
Before the availability of next-generation sequencing technology, studying the genome and gene expression at scale relied heavily on microarrays. Microarrays were, and still are, used to quantify RNA transcripts\sjcitep[,]{schenaQuantitativeMonitoringGene1995} genotype samples using SNP arrays\sjcitep[,]{wang1998large} and even measure DNA-protein interactions using ChIP-on-chip\sjcitep[.]{ren2000genome} While microarrays allow for up to ~\num{50000} probes to be interrogated in a single experiment, limited dynamic range, high false-positive hybridisation and heterogeneous probe hybridisation capacities skew absolute quantification. The probe set also requires intelligent design and thus cannot discover novel sequences. Probeset size is particularly troubling for ChIP-on-Chip, since genome binning limits resolution to around 500-1000 bp - too low for novel TF motif discovery. Nonetheless, large scale, distributed application of microarray technologies culminated in a first draft of the Encyclopedia of DNA Elements (ENCODE)in 2007\sjcitep[,]{ENCODE_Project_Consortium2007-zd} a careful annotation of 30 MBs - around 1\% - of the human genome. The release of this draft was the last project of such scale that did not use next generation sequencing.

By the mid 2000s, the availability of the human genome as a public resource and the falling cost of NGS led to the development of a new class of quantitative assays: specific fractions of nucleic acids extracted from tissues could now be sequenced, aligned to the reference genome, and counted. One of the first such assays was histone methylation ChIP-seq, first published in 2007\sjcitep[.]{barskiHighresolutionProfilingHistone2007} By sequencing immunoprecipitated DNA directly rather than using microarrays, methylation could now be assessed genome-wide and with fewer quantitative biases. Performing this technique for a spectrum of 20 different lys/arg histone methylation ChIP-seq variants on CD4\textsuperscript{+} T cells, and correlating the annotations with GNF SymAtlas gene expression microarray data allowed genome-wide classification and discovery of various classes of functional elements. The distribution of methylation signatures could now be systematically correlated to phenotypes, helping understand disease and development. For example, a comparison of histone methylation in mouse embryonic stem cells and embryonic fibroblasts revealed transitory methylation changes in the de-differentiation of ES\sjcitep[.]{mikkelsen2007genome}

In parallel research, ChIP-seq was also being applied to individual transcription factors at a genomic resolution of around 50 base pairs, a welcome improvement compared to microarrays. For example, application of ChIP-seq on neuron-restrictive silencer factor (NRSF) in Jurkat T-cells showed that this factor can also bind "weakly" to altered, non-canonical motifs dependent on local chromatin state (methylation, accessibility) and co-factor motif context\sjcitep[.]{johnsongenomewidemappingvivo2007} These findings contradicted earlier ideas of stringent binding to only canonical motifs, confirmed by SELEX and microarray studies, a rejection made possible only through the broadness of NGS. NGS also improved dynamic range compared to microarrays, showing for example that STAT1 motif enrichment was strongly correlated to absolute ChIP-seq peak height\sjcitep[.]{robertsonGenomewideProfilesSTAT12007}

The increased genomic resolution of ChIP-seq was rapidly refining models of gene regulation, but the technique still required a strong antibody and prior knowledge of the protein or histone modification of interest. In contrast, DNase I hypersensitivity (DHS) assays could survey chromatin accessibility genome-wide. The technique had already been applied for genome-wide accessibility studies, first by southern blot\sjcitep[,]{wu1980heat} then capillary Sanger sequencing\sjcitep[,]{crawford2004identifying} and later microarrays\sjcitep[.]{crawford2006dnase}. Like ChIP, DNase-seq was soon translated to NGS\sjcitep[,]{hesselberth2009global}, rapidly contributing to even higher resolution (1 basepair) understanding of gene gene regulation. For example, matched DNase-seq and glucocorticoid receptor (GR) ChIP-seq revealed that out of \num{8236} active GR binding sites in murine mammary adenocarcinoma, 95\% were already accessible \textit{prior to} receptor activation\sjcitep[.]{john2011chromatin} These findings rejected prior ideas that GR might act as a pioneer factor, remodeling inaccessible chromatin and exposing its preferred motif after receptor activation. Moreover, pituitary gland cells shared only 11.4\% of the binding sites accessible in adenocarcinoma, suggesting that differential chromatin context underlies differential responses to the same transcription factor. Unsurprisingly, ENCODE's second draft included DNAse-seq data from 125 tissues, along with H3K4me3 and TF ChIP-seq and bisulfite sequencing\sjcitep[.]{thurman2012accessible,encode2012} In total, 2.9 million DNase I hypersensitivity sites were identified and annotated genome-wide, covering almost all previously known regulatory element candidates: 94.4\% of previously known TF ChIP-seq regions were found to overlap with these DNase I sites, and only \num{3692} such sites were found in all cell types assessed, confirming that chromatin accessibility and downstream gene regulation are highly tissue and cell-type specific processes. Combination of these new regulatory readouts with GWAS further confirmed that many disease-related SNPs are located in regulatory regions, accelerating therapeutic target discovery.

By 2008 development of NGS-driven RNA-seq, and subsequent displacement of microarrays in transcriptomics began in 2008, first in yeast\sjcitep[,]{nagalakshmi2008transcriptional} and later in mouse\sjcitep[,]{mortazavi2008mapping} and human\sjcitep[.]{wang2008alternative}. In the Mortazavi article, which included a detailed RNA-seq protocol, poly(A)\textsuperscript{+} mRNA was extracted and purified from brain, liver, and skeletal muscle from C57BL/6 mice extracted en enriched for poly(A) mRNA, hydrolised and reverse-transcribed to cDNA using random hexamer priming. Illumina/Solexa was used to sequence the resulting libraries. This particular study delivered a groundbreaking number of new observations through the first quantitative whole transcriptome in mouse:

In 1950, Barbara McClintock discovered how \textit{"mutable loci arise when chromatin material is inserted adjacent to genes"} in maize\sjcitep[,]{mcclintock1950,ravindran2012} the first observation of position-effect variegation and cis-regulatory chromatin influence outside of \textit{Drosophila}\sjcitep[.]{muller1930} Some 20 years later, Shapiro and colleagues observed how some disruptive mutations in \textit{E. coli}'s \textit{gal} operon are caused by insertion of foreign DNA\sjcitep[,]{shapiro1969} marking the first experimental evidence of transposable element insertion in bacteria. Later, Berg and colleagues found how entire resistance plasmids carried by \textit{E. coli} can spontaneously integrate into the genome of bacteriophage \textlambda, and how 1.5 kb long inverted sequence repeats flank the inserted kanamycin resistance gene\sjcitep[.]{berg1975} The transposable element studied by Berg and colleagues was later classified as Tn5\sjcitep[,]{bukhari1977} and by 1982, its two inverted repeats (IS50-L and IS50-R) were been fully sequenced\sjcitep[.]{reznikoff1981,reznikoff1982} This sequence map revealed a number of exciting regulatory novelties within the transposable element. First, the transposase protein itself (461 amino acids) is located on IS50-R. A truncated version of the protein (421 amino acids) is also expressed due to the presence of a second, downstream transcription start site. This truncated protein was found to inhibit the complete transposase by competitive binding, repressing total transposition activity. A conserved stop codon point mutation found in the nearly identical IS50-L repeat disrupts complete expression of the transposase there, but interestingly introduces a promotor sequence for the embedded antibiotic resistance genes! The Tn5 transposon is thus capable of transfering antibiotic resistance genes independently of the host's endogenous transfer mechanisms, and self-regulates both its transposon and resistance activities. Soon, Tn5's potential as a molecular tool was recognised. For example, in-vitro Tn5 insertions could be used to disrupt genes in bacteria, with antibiotic resistance as a positive selection marker\sjcitep[.]{ruvkun1982} A first full crystallography of Tn5 protein in complex with DNA showed that it shares mechanistic features with HIV-1 integrase and RAG1/2 V(D)J recombinases\sjcitep[.]{davies2000threedimensional}

Owing to its use as a lab tool, Tn5 protein mutants and their molecular mechanisms were quickly studied\sjcitep[.]{wiegand1992characterization, weinreich1994evidence} A triple mutant, E54K (enhances OE binding) - M56A (disrupts inhibitor production) L372P (increases activity by altering dimerization), was engineered to increase catalytic activity\sjcitep[,]{goryshintn5vitrotransposition1998} ("hyperactive Tn5") and is the major variant in use to date. The transposase can bind to two sites on the ends of each IS50 sequence: the inside or outside end (IE, OE). A synthetic "mosaic end" (ME), engineered by combining elements of the IE and the OE showed enhanced binding capacity, increasing efficiency\sjcitep[.]{zhou1998molecular} through increasingly efficient dimerization and complexation of Tnp with DNA\sjcitep[.]{steinigerwhiteEvidenceUnseenTransposase2002} The transposase's exact catalytic mechanism was unknown until Bhasin and colleagues showed how the Tn5 transposase nicks at the transposon ends, forms a hairpin, releases the donor dna, and opens the hairpin and integrates at the acceptor site\sjcitep[.]{bhasin1999hairpin} A staggered cut is made at the acceptor site, and after insertion of the blunt-end transposon, the missing 9 base pairs are repaired, leading to a duplication. A classic DDE motif, known from RNAseH enzymes, in Tn5 transposase's active site coordinates magnesium ions critical for catalysis by stabilizing the DNA-protein complexs\sjcitep[.]{lovell2002twometal,reznikoffTn5ModelUnderstanding2003}

By the mid 2000s, Tn5 was in use in various ways: to tag cloned DNA with selection markers, primers, or even regulatory elements\sjcitep[.]{reznikoffTn5MolecularGenetics2004} This could be achieved without in vivo expression of the transposon by loading transposase protein onto pre-cleaved DNA, and introducing the complex into electroporated bacteria. Tn5 transposase was first used by Adey and Shendure to construct shotgun sequencing libraries in 2010\sjcitep[.]{adeyRapidLowInputLowbias2010} Previously, researchers needed to fragment DNA using sonication or restriction enzymes, followed by end repair and ligation, a process that spanned 6 to 10 hours. With Tn5 transposase "tagmentation", these pre-PCR steps were reduced to only 15 to 20 minutes. Moreover, the Adey protocol could use inputs as low as \SI{10}{\pico\gram} as opposed to the \SI{1}{\micro\gram} required by the ligation methods, a sensitivity increase that would later allow library extraction from single-cells.
