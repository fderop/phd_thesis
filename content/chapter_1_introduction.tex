\chapter{\nohyphens{The Accessible Genome at Single-Cell Resolution}}
This introductory chapter will attempt to build the foundation for the explorations that follow by providing a historical overview of chromatin accessibility based on historical accounts\sjcitep[,]{slackConradHalWaddington2002, olinsChromatinHistoryOur2003, cobbWhoDiscoveredMessenger2015, deichmannChromatinItsHistory2015, deichmannEpigeneticsOriginsEvolution2016, lis50YearHistory2019, roeder50YearsEukaryotic2019} and frame it within the ongoing research on decoding gene regulation. Then, we illustrate the state of the art of single-cell chromatin sequencing technologies when I began this work, and delve into the capabilities and limitations of several breakthrough techniques. This context serves to highlight the importance of the subject matter, and to motivate our subsequent discussions and findings.

The word "cell" was first used in 1665 by Robert Hooke to describe the hexagonal walls he observed in a thin slice of cork through a self-made microscope\sjcitep[.]{hookeMicrographia1665} It took several more centuries of research until the concept of cells as the foundational building blocks of life began to take shape. Only in 1839, Theodor Schwann proposed that animals, just like plants, were composed of cells\sjcitep[,]{schwannMikroskopischeUntersuchungenUber1839} and Rudolf Virchow and Robert Remak later added that cells were the product of division of pre-existing cells\sjcitep[.]{rudolfCellularpathologieIhrerBegrundung1858} While studying this process of division in 1879, Walther Flemming first used the term "chromatin" to describe the filament-like threads between two dividing cells in 1879\sjcitep[.]{flemmingBeitrageZurKenntniss1879} Flemming's observations did not differ much from his colleague's Wilhelm Waldeyer's description of "chromosomes"\sjcitep[.]{deichmannChromatinItsHistory2015} These early findings directly stemmed from the refinement of microscopic lenses, which had by now become accurate enough to permit detailed studies of developing embryos. Notably, the work of Baltic-German polymath and explorer Karl Ernst von Baer, who between 1817 and 1826 made the first accounts of egg cells, development of the blastula, the notochord and the three germ layers, produced a number of fundamental questions that would engage the minds of biologists for the coming centuries:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
	\item How can a fertilised egg cell self-organise into an entire organism?
	\item Where is the information that steers this process encoded, and how is it interpreted?
	\item How can this process go wrong, leading to disease? \footnotetextwithoutmarker{\superfullcite{tagarelliHansSpemannOne2004}A. Tagarelli, et al. By the early 20th century, several groups were systematically studying these concepts. In 1924, Hans Spemann, together with his student Hilde Mangold, discovered that transplantation of the dorsal lip from one \textit{Triturus} (newt) embryo to the ventral side of another embryo would induce the formation of a ventrally conjoined twin (\autoref{fig:spemannmangold})\sjcitep[.]{spemannUberInduktionEmbryonenanlagen1924} Importantly, the pair showed that the conjoined secondary embryo primarily consisted of tissue from the \textit{host}, indicating that this transplanted tissue, which Spemann later named the "organizer field", merely instructed its surroundings to change developmental fate. Multiple labs raced to purify and characterise the compound responsible for these processes in an "organizer gold rush"\sjcitep[,]{slackConradHalWaddington2002} but without success. We now know that the compounds responsible for this process are small, secreted molecules termed "growth factors", and are capable of steering a cell's fate when present even at only nanogram quantities - far below the detection limit of that era's technologies.

Around that time, the concept of physical store and transfer of genetic information was not yet widely accepted, and the function and identity of Flemming and Waldeyer's chromosomes was unknown. In search of answers, Thomas Hunt Morgan began studying inheritance by irradiating \textit{Drosophila melanogaster} (fruit fly) and cross-breeding the resulting mutants in 1908. After several years without results, Morgan realised that the inheritance pattern of some mutant traits, such as the flies' eye colours, could be reliably predicted across generations\sjcitep[.]{morganSexLimitedInheritance1910} More than half a century earlier, Moravian friar Gregor Mendel had demonstrated the exact same principles in \textit{Pisum sativum} (sweet pea), but his work was lost and disputed by the scientific community upon its rediscovery. Using his own findings, Morgan verified Mendel's work and formally defined the theory of Mendelian inheritance in 1915\sjcitep[.]{morganMechanismMendelianHeredity1915} A second breakthrough came when Morgan demonstrated that chromosomes, first observed by Flemming and Waldeyer, were in fact the physical bodies responsible for the transfer of phenotypic traits\sjcitep[,]{morganRandomSegregationCoupling1911} confirming earlier ideas that chromosomes carried "genes" which encoded heritable traits.

Together, Morgan's findings inaugurated the 20\textsuperscript{th} century's era of genetics, and defined the context for many of the major discoveries that ensued. However, the link between embryonic development and Morgan's "new" insights in genetics was not readily made, and even dismissed by the broader scientific community. This changed when a young Conrad Waddington, who had previously been performing organizer grafting experiments in mammals and birds, visited Morgan's fly lab in 1935. Here, Waddington intensely studied \textit{Drosophila}'s embryonic wing development, and discovered that specific heritable mutations could disrupt this process at specific stages\sjcitep[.]{waddingtonGeneticControlWing1940} Based on these findings, Waddington wrote his seminal work \textit{Organisers and Genes} in 1940, and introduced the notion that embryonic development was a large decision tree under the control of genes, which he metaphorically described as a "landscape"\sjcitep[.]{waddingtonOrganisersGenes1940} In 1957, Waddington extensively elaborated on his concept of the "epigenetic landscape" in his eighth book, \textit{The Strategy of the Genes}\sjcitep[.]{waddingtonStrategyGenes1957} Here, he famously formulated this landscape as a multidimensional metabolic state space where a ball, representing a cell, metaphorically rolls downwards during development, passing several bifurcations which ultimately determine its fate (\autoref{fig:waddington_landscape}a). Beneath the surface, a series of tension ropes, symbolizing genes, forcefully pull the surface into shape (\autoref{fig:waddington_landscape}b).

In fact, the storage of genetic information was attributed to proteins at the time, as they made up a much larger portion of the chromosomal dry mass than nucleic acids, and were deemed far more complex in composition. Nucleic acids were thought to be only of structural importance to chromosomes, akin to the highly repetitive polymers found in cell walls and plant fibers. This notion was overturned in 1944, when Avery, MacLeod and McCarty demonstrated that it was DNA, and not proteins, purified from a non-virulent, rough (R) strain of bacteria that could "transform" a virulent, smooth (S) strain to the R strain\sjcitep[.]{griffithSignificancePneumococcalTypes1928,averyStudiesChemicalNature1944}

The Avery-MacLeod-McCarty experiment formally cemented DNA as the storage location of genetic information, but another decade would pass before the exact molecular mechanism through which DNA could contain information was discovered. This breakthrough came when Watson, Crick and Franklin resolved the double-helix structure of DNA using X-ray crystallography (\autoref{fig:watsoncrickfranklin})\sjcitep[,]{watsonMolecularStructureNucleic1953} and for the first time suggested that \textit{"the precise sequence of the bases is the code which carries the genetical information"}\sjcitep[.]{,watsonGeneticalImplicationsStructure1953} It had thus become clear that life stores information in the nucleotide sequence of DNA, and the scientific community began to prepare experiments that would leverage this concept.

\section{The early molecular era}
Although Conrad Waddington's visionary ideas were largely theoretical, they were soon substantiated. Jacques Monod, yet another pupil of Thomas Hunt Morgan, observed that bacteria grown in a medium containing both glucose and lactose would preferentially feed on glucose, and would only begin feeding on lactose once glucose had run out. Further research together with Monod's student François Jacob led to the understanding that the production of \textbeta-galactosidase, the enzyme responsible for lactose catabolism, was under the control of a "repressor", which was inactivated in the presence of lactose. Together, the pair published these findings in 1961\sjcitep[,]{jacobGeneticRegulatoryMechanisms1961} in a review which features the first diagram of a \textit{gene regulatory network} (\autoref{fig:lac_repressor}). The Jacob-Monod diagram also explicitly showed the repressor as a protein originating from a gene. Genes thus (indirectly) controlled other genes, and therefore the behaviour of the cell - exactly how Conrad Waddington had prophesised twenty years prior.

Earlier that month, Jacob, together with Sidney Brenner, had also proposed the existence of an unstable form of RNA which acted as a "messenger" to transfer information from DNA-based genes to proteins\sjcitep[.]{brennerUnstableIntermediateCarrying1961} This messenger RNA was already clearly depicted as the intermediate carrier of information between genes and proteins in Jacob \& Monod's gene regulatory network. The exact code through which the sequence of mRNA translates to proteins was deciphered soon after using clever "forward" experiments: Marshall Nirenberg, in the lab of Philip Leder, fed synthetic RNA homopolymer repeats (e.g. UUU\textsubscript{n}, AAA\textsubscript{n}, ...) to cell-free protein synthesis systems in which one of the 20 possible amino-acids were radioactively labeled. The detection of a radioactive protein product then revealed the identity of the amino-acid for which the input homopolymer coded\sjcitep[.]{nirenbergRNACodewordsProtein1964} Har Gobind Khorana combined more complex RNA trinucleotide repeat polymers (e.g. ACA\textsubscript{n}, UAG\textsubscript{n}, ...) with chemical analysis of the resulting proteins to decipher the remaining possible 3-nucleotide codons, producing a finalised code in 1966\sjcitep[.]{khoranaPolynucleotideSynthesisGenetic1966}

A "backward" experiment in which the sequence of a given DNA or RNA molecule could be sequenced remained elusive. The lab of Walter Fiers (Ghent University) was the first to do so in 1972 by reporting a complete sequence of the bacteriophage MS2 coat protein gene\sjcitep[.]{jouNucleotideSequenceGene1972} Fiers first digested the phage's \textsuperscript{32}P-labeled RNA genome using ribonuclease T\textsubscript{1}, which cuts RNA at G positions, and iteratively separated the resulting fragments based on size and charge through multiple rounds of two-dimensional fractionation. Joël Vandekerckhove and Marc Van Montagu (both also Ghent University) had simultaneously been working on determining the amino-acid sequence of the MS2 coat protein\sjcitep[.]{vandekerckhoveUseFluorescamineSequence1973} Under the guidance of Van Montagu's preliminary amino-acid map and the Nirenberg-Khorana codon dictionary, Fiers and colleagues were able to puzzle together their fragments into a near-complete base sequence of the MS2 coat protein gene.

Soon after, Fredrick Sanger, who had developed the two-dimensional fractionation used by Fiers\sjcitep[,]{sangerTwodimensionalFractionationProcedure1965} and had been awarded the Nobel prize for the amino-acid sequence of insulin in 1958, succeeded in deterministically sequencing the entire DNA genome of bacteriophage \textphi X174 in 1977\sjcitep[.]{sangerDNASequencingChainterminating1977} Earlier work had shown that dideoxy variants of the four common nucleotides (A, C, G \& T) would inhibit further polymerisation when included into the newly synthesised DNA strand due to the absence of a reactive phosphate. Sanger devised that a polymerisation reaction performed in a mixture of the four common nucleotides combined with a single dideoxy variant will produce a mixture of strands terminated at each position of the dideoxy variant.Sanger further reasoned that performing this reaction for all four dideoxy variants in parallel should yield all the positions of each of the four bases (\autoref{fig:sangersequencing}). Indeed, when applied to fragments of \textphi X174 DNA, the entire phage's genome could be reconstructed. Sanger's method now allowed researchers to sequence DNA at a relatively "rapid" pace and with higher accuracy than previously possible, setting the stage for the coming decade's research.

\section{Gene regulation unveiled}
In hindsight, several pieces of the gene regulatory puzzle had now been discovered:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
	\item Genetic information is stored in the base sequence of a cell's DNA, and DNA can be sequenced using Sanger's method.
	\item This information is transferred to proteins according to a well-defined codon "dictionary" through messenger RNA. Transcription of DNA to RNA is thus a crucial process of information transfer.
	\item The resulting proteins are capable of influencing transcription and thus the behaviour of the cell.
\end{enumerate}

However, mechanistic and molecular insights in \textit{how} genes might be regulated was still sparse. Several breakthroughs came from the lab of Robert Roeder. Roeder, who as a student had purified the polymerase enzymes that catalyze the transcription of messenger RNA from DNA in 1969\sjcitep[,]{roederMultipleFormsDNAdependent1969} showed that purified RNA polymerases could only initiate the transcription of DNA templates in the presence of uncharacterised cellular protein extracts. This finding confirmed earlier ideas that additional, then-unknown factors were necessary to initiate the transcription reaction\sjcitep[.]{ngTranscriptionClonedXenopus1979} Several such initiation factors were purified and characterised within the next year\sjcitep[,]{matsuiMultipleFactorsAre1980} and it soon transpired that a large number of different factors are necessary for transcription to occur.

Parallel research in Roeder's lab led to the discovery of a protein which induced expression of the 5S ribosomal RNA gene in \textit{Xenopus} (clawed frog) egg cells. This factor, later named Transcription Factor III (\textit{TFIIIa}), was the first protein shown to initiate transcription of a \textit{specific} gene in eukaryotes, and not just initiate transcription in general. Roeder and his student Takashi Matsui suggested that \textit{TFIIIa} might \textit{"recognize a unique region of DNA"}, a hypothesis which was explored and demonstrated soon after\sjcitep[.]{engelkeSpecificInteractionPurified1980} However, as part of the ribosomal body, 5S rRNA plays a role in cellular protein synthesis, and is ubiquitously present in all cells across all known organisms. Transcription factors involved in more specialised processes were identified and characterised relatively quickly: examples include the glucocorticoid receptor \textit{Gr}\sjcitep[,]{payvarPurifiedGlucocorticoidReceptors1981} \textit{Sp1}\sjcitep[,]{dynanIsolationTranscriptionFactors1983} and \textit{Gal4}\sjcitep[,]{bramSpecificProteinBinding1985} important transcription factors which are still under scrutiny to this day. In fact, it became clear that some of these factors were extremely powerful: their ectopic expression could convert one cell type to another\sjcitep[,]{davisExpressionSingleTransfected1987} and later specific combinations were found to even revert fully differentiated skin cells back to stem cells\sjcitep[.]{takahashiInductionPluripotentStem2006} By this time, the scientific realised that the factors responsible for the activity of Spemann \& Mangold's organizer were transcription factors, and several of these factors were identified shortly after\sjcitep[.]{choMolecularNatureSpemann1991, lambNeuralInductionSecreted1993}
