\chapter{A Discussion on Scaling Omics}
The work described here encompasses two parts. First, we developed an open-source droplet microfluidic platform for scATAC-seq and scRNA-seq, and second, we benchmarked this assay together with other open-source and commercial protocols. Together, these efforts touched on all low-level aspects of omics data generation: we optimized reaction chemistries, engineered hardware, wrote software, managed large data structures, and coordinated high-power compute.

Overall, the work feeds into a broader, cross-disciplinary trend of generating large datasets explicitly for model training. This strategy first began to show results in games like go and chess\sjcitep[,]{sutton2019bitterlesson} but has recently manifested in more tangible applications such as self-driving cars\sjcitep[]{waymo2025scaling} and language models\sjcitep[.]{brown2020languagemodelsfewshotlearners} Exploring the parameter sensitivity ("scaling laws") of transformer models has shown that performance continues to scale with model size, dataset size, and training length - although model size was the largest contributor\sjcitep[.]{kaplan2020scalinglawsneurallanguage}

Google's BERT was trained on just \SI{16}{\giga\byte} of uncompressed text\sjcitep[,]{devlin2019bertpretrainingdeepbidirectional} whereas OpenAI's GPT-3 was trained on a total of \textasciitilde{\SI{700}{\giga\byte}} of compressed data sourced from Common Crawl (\SI{340}{\giga\byte}), Wikipedia (\SI{15}{\giga\byte}), and nearly all written books (\SI{90}{\giga\byte}) just a year later\sjcitep[.]{brown2020languagemodelsfewshotlearners} Compute was scaled orders of magnitude more, roughly \SI{1.2e17}{\flops} versus \SI{3.14e23}{\flops}. The resulting performance increase coupled with post-training has allowed GPT-3 to penetrate into consumer markets, eventually becoming the most rapidly adopted technology of all time. While improvements were later made with architectural novelties like mixture of experts (MoE)\sjcitep[]{fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion} and reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF)\sjcitep[,]{ouyang2022traininglanguagemodelsfollow} it can be posed that the first jump in performance was caused by a disproportionally large commitment of data and compute.

A question that is much debated today is whether or not the data + compute scaling paradigm will hold for biological problems as well. Some efforts, outside of the genomics/sequencing space, seem to suggest so. One  example is Meta's Evolutionary Scale Modeling (ESM), which applies large language model architecture (specifically, BERT) to protein language to predict masked tokens in amino acid sequences. The log-likelihood of mutant sequences serves as a proxy for how disruptive the mutation is. Between 2021 and 2025, ESM grew from 650M\sjcitep[]{rives2021biological} to 15B\sjcitep[]{lin2023evolutionary} and recently 98B\sjcitep[]{hayes2025simulating} parameters. ESM-3 was trained on 711B tokens across 2.78B proteins, amended with synthetic data generated by AlphaFold, and trained over \SI{1.07e24}{\flops} of compute. The model introduced multimodal masked token modeling: joint tokenized amino-acid sequence, atomic structure, and region annotations can be passed partially masked, and the model will jointly reconstruct missing tokens. This led to the generation of esmGFP, a completely novel fluorescent protein that shares a sequence similarity of only 58\% with the nearest natural fluorescent protein. ESM-3 also performs better over low-homology sequences than its predecessors, further suggesting that amending data scarcity continues to increase performance.

Since ESM is de facto a domain-specific LLM trained on protein language, it is not surprising that transformer scaling laws apply to it. The case of Google DeepMind's AlphaFold is a bit more nuanced. AlphaFold 1 was a CNN trained on protein structures (PDB) and multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) to predict residue-residue distances\sjcitep[.]{senior2020improved} AlphaFold 2 switched architecture, passing MSAs through Evoformer, a model with transformer components, to predict spatial contacts and ultimately complete 3D protein structure\sjcitep[.]{jumper2021highly} These changes led it to achieve a median backbone accuracy of \SI{0.96}{\angstrom} in CASP14 - surpassing the next best method by \textasciitilde\SI{2}{\angstrom}. AlphaFold 3 included a generative diffusion model to handle atomic coordinates directly rather than relying on MSAs. As a result, the model is capable of predicting binding interactions across protein, DNA/RNA, and small molecules, and performs better than AlphaFold 2 on other tasks. DeepMind has not released the precise training data used for AlphaFold1-3, but it is generally thought that the size increases are not as large as for GPT and ESM: AlphaFold 1 was already trained on a large fraction of available PDB and MSA data, and AlphaFold 3's weights are only \SI{1}{\giga\byte} in size. When data constrained, model architecture innovations such as end-to-end modeling, introduction of attention and diffusion, are thus still viable paths to raw performance improvement. However, recent benchmarks have also shown that protein/ligand binding models, including AlphaFold 3, suffer performance drops when re-trained without train-test leakage\sjcitep[,]{plinder2024proteinligand} and still perform poorly on ligands that are sparsely represented in the training data, suggesting that \textit{"data scarcity remains a major challenge for these methods"}\sjcitep[.]{skrinar2025proteinligand} Optimistically, these findings suggests that generation of additional or higher-quality data will yield further performance improvements for protein modeling.

The availability of in-silico predictive tools helps advance science in a number of ways. First and foremost, accurate models save costs by narrowing down the number of hypotheses. Synthetic enhancer candidates, for example, can be evaluated in-silico, filtering the most promising ones to evaluate in the reporter cohort. These reductions in cost also reduce the barrier to entry, distributing work more efficiently, and improving iteration speed\sjcitep[.]{lakhani2007principles}
