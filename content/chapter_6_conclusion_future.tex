\chapter{A Discussion on Scaling Omics}
Biology has evolved through a series of overlapping technological and conceptual shifts. In the early 1900s, microscopic observations at the cellular level sparked systematic study of biology. By the mid-20th century, scientists began uncovering the biochemical foundations of life: how information is stored in DNA physically, and how this information is retrieved. Mechanistic understanding of gene regulation began to form in the 70s and 80s, notably with the cataloguing of genomic organisation and DNA-binding proteins. The 90s up to the early 2000s were marked by the sequencing and cataloguing of entire genomes. Shortly after, commodification of sequencing enabled high-throughput assays to quantify RNA, chromatin state, and other DNA-derived species. Today, data-driven computational models have matured, enabling low-level predictions of various molecular systems.

The work described here encompasses two parts. First, we developed an open-source droplet microfluidic platform for scATAC-seq and scRNA-seq, and second, we benchmarked this assay together with other open-source and commercial protocols. Together, these efforts touched on all low-level aspects of omics data generation: we optimized reaction chemistries, engineered hardware, wrote software, managed large data structures, and coordinated high-power compute. The end result is (a) open-source droplet-microfluidic single-cell assay that is competitive with commercial solutions and (b) a benchmarking framework for high-throughput single-cell ATAC-seq datasets.

Overall, the work feeds into a broader, cross-disciplinary trend of generating large data explicitly for model training. This strategy first began to show results in games like go and chess\sjcitep[,]{sutton2019bitterlesson} but has recently manifested in more tangible applications such as self-driving cars\sjcitep[]{waymo2025scaling} and language models\sjcitep[.]{brown2020languagemodelsfewshotlearners} Exploring the parameter sensitivity ("scaling laws") of transformer models has shown that performance continues to scale with model size, dataset size, and training length - although model size was the largest contributor\sjcitep[.]{kaplan2020scalinglawsneurallanguage} For example, Google's BERT was trained on just \SI{16}{\giga\byte} of uncompressed text\sjcitep[,]{devlin2019bertpretrainingdeepbidirectional} whereas OpenAI's GPT-3 was trained on a total of \textasciitilde{\SI{700}{\giga\byte}} of compressed data sourced from Common Crawl (\SI{570}{\giga\byte}), Wikipedia, WebText2 and nearly all written books (\SI{120}{\giga\byte}) just a year later\sjcitep[.]{brown2020languagemodelsfewshotlearners} Compute was scaled orders of magnitude more, roughly \SI{7.8e20}{\flops} versus \SI{3.14e23}{\flops}. The resulting performance increase coupled with post-training into an instructable chat application allowed GPT-3 to penetrate into consumer markets, eventually becoming the most rapidly adopted technology of all time. While improvements were later made with architectural novelties like mixture of experts (MoE)\sjcitep[]{fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion} and reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF)\sjcitep[,]{ouyang2022traininglanguagemodelsfollow} it can be posed that the initial jump in performance was caused by a disproportionally large commitment of data and compute.

A question that is much debated today is \textbf{whether or not the data + compute scaling paradigm will generalize to jumps in biological model performance as well}. Some current efforts, outside of the genomics/sequencing space, seem to suggest so. One example is Meta's Evolutionary Scale Modeling (ESM), which applies large language model architecture (specifically, BERT) to protein language to predict masked tokens in amino acid sequences. The log-likelihood of proposed sequences serves as an estimate for how disruptive given mutations will be, allowing researchers to triage synthetic designs. Between 2021 and 2025, ESM grew from 650M\sjcitep[]{rives2021biological} to 15B\sjcitep[]{lin2023evolutionary} and recently 98B\sjcitep[]{hayes2025simulating} parameters. ESM-3 was trained on 771B tokens across 2.78B proteins, amended with synthetic data generated by AlphaFold, and trained over \SI{1.07e24}{\flops} of compute. ESM-3 also introduced multimodal masked token modeling: joint tokenized amino-acid sequence, atomic structure, and region annotations can be passed partially masked, and the model will jointly reconstruct missing tokens. This led to the generation of esmGFP, a completely synthetically designed fluorescent protein.

Since ESM is de facto a domain-specific LLM trained on protein language, it is not surprising that transformer scaling laws apply to it. The case of Google DeepMind's AlphaFold is a bit more nuanced. AlphaFold 1 was a CNN trained on protein structures (PDB) and multiple sequence alignments (MSAs) to predict residue-residue distances\sjcitep[.]{senior2020improved} AlphaFold 2 switched architecture, passing MSAs through Evoformer, a model with transformer-based self-attention, to predict spatial contacts and ultimately complete 3D protein structure\sjcitep[.]{jumper2021highly} These changes led it to achieve a median backbone accuracy of \SI{0.96}{\angstrom} in CASP14 - surpassing the next best method by \textasciitilde\SI{2}{\angstrom}. AlphaFold 3 included a generative diffusion model to handle atomic coordinates directly rather than relying on MSAs. As a result, the model is capable of predicting binding interactions across protein, DNA/RNA, and small molecules, and performs better than AlphaFold 2 on other tasks. DeepMind has not released the precise training data used for AlphaFold 1-3, but it is generally thought that the jumps in size were not as large as for GPT and ESM: AlphaFold 1 was already trained on a large fraction of available PDB and MSA data, and AlphaFold 3's weights are only \SI{1}{\giga\byte} in size. When data constrained, model architecture innovations such as end-to-end modeling, introduction of attention and diffusion, are thus still viable paths to raw performance improvement. However, recent benchmarks have also shown that protein/ligand binding models, including AlphaFold 3, suffer performance drops when re-trained without train-test leakage\sjcitep[.]{plinder2024proteinligand} Ligands that are sparsely represented in the training data were especially problematic, suggesting that \textit{"data scarcity remains a major challenge for these methods"}\sjcitep[.]{skrinar2025proteinligand} Optimistically, these findings suggests that generation of additional or higher-quality data will yield further performance improvements in the space.

In a similar vein, several foundation models have been developed in the field of genomics. The original DNABERT was an 86M parameter transformer trained on the 3.2 Gbp human reference genome to classify promoters, TF-binding sites, and other regulatory features\sjcitep[.]{ji2021dnabert} The model was competitive with ClinVar for SNV scoring, but had a relatively small context window of 512 bp, so could not compete in long range tasks such as enhancer-promoter pairing. Nucleotide Transformer (NT) extended the BERT approach to multi-species data: adding both genomic and evolutionary variety by using \num{3300} individual human genomes and 850 other organisms, totaling 262B tokens. NT consistently outperformed BERT and DeepSEA on promoter, enhancer, chromatin, and histone mark prediction, and enabled zero-shot scoring of SNPs and indels - achieving better accuracy than previous models on ClinVar benchmarks\sjcitep[.]{dallatorre2025nucleotide} Interestingly, several training sizes and regimes were investigated. Training on many species improved performance on human benchmarks more than increasing parameters from 500M to 2.5B, suggesting that diversity matters more than raw volume. Compute also partially offset size: smaller NT models trained for longer approach the accuracy of larger ones. Recently, Evolutionary Scale Modeling for Genomes (Evo 2) pushed this scaling further with a 40B-parameter model trained on 9.3T tokens spanning all domains of life\sjcitep[.]{brixi2025genome} The model scores variant effects across taxa, and supports up to 1 million tokens of context, enabling the generation of entire synthetic mitochondrial or bacterial genomes. Loss decreased smoothly with more data and parameters, with no saturation observed so far. However, a 7B checkpoint already matched the 40B model on most clinical-genomics benchmarks, suggesting diminishing returns beyond this size for short-range tasks. Instead, data engineering, such as biasing the training distribution toward gene-proximal regions and de-emphasizing repetitive DNA, improved AUROC by up to 0.1, even at constant parameter count. Larger models mainly unlocked generative capabilities: only the 40B Evo 2 model could synthesize coherent genome-scale DNA.

The amount of pure genomic data available is large, and we can assume that the hundreds of thousands of genomes used in Evo 2 sample a significant fraction of evolutionary space. The training datasets used in these fields are thus large relative to the spaces queried. GenBank contains \SI{25}{\tera\bp} from 3.7 billion annotated sequences over \num{581000} species\sjcitep[.]{sayers2024genbank} In contrast, GEO currently contains about \num{250000} datasets across 7.5 million samples, of which around \num{380000} are (sc)RNA-seq, \num{44000} are (sc)ATAC-seq, and [n] are [chip, dnase, ...]. [ENCODE] Despite fewer data available, a number of foundation models have appeared.

Several track-predicting models came from the work of David R. Kelley. In 2018 with Basenji2, a CNN trained on \num{4229} human epigenomic (DNase-seq, histone-ChIP) and transcriptional (CAGE) tracks across > 500 cell and tissue types, with a large receptive field of 131 kb\sjcitep[]{kelley2018sequential} The model could predict 128-bp binned coverage across the genome: users feed any 131-kb reference or mutant sequence to the pretrained model (or fine-tune on new tracks) and obtain coverage predictions for each of the \num{4229} tissue-specific tracks it was trained on. In 2021, Enformer replaced dilated convolutions with global self-attention and pushed the receptive field to 100 kb with a 200 kb input window\sjcitep[.]{avsec2021effective} Trained now on \num{5313} human and \num{1643} mouse tracks (TF and histone-mark ChIP, DNaseI, ATAC and CAGE), it predicts these tracks at 128 bp resolution, including long-range interactions. Borzoi is another CNN-attention hybrid, trained on thousands of CAGE, DNase, ATAC, and ChIP-seq datasets similarly to Basenji2 and Enformer, but also adding 866 human and 279 mouse RNA-seq tracks\sjcitep[.]{linder2025predicting} The model predicts these tracks now at 32 bp-resolution. Kundaje lab introduced ChromBPNet, a CNN trained on ENCODE ATAC-seq and DNase-seq tracks for the five tier 1 ENCODE cell lines (GM12878, HepG2, K562, IMR90, H1-hESC)\sjcitep[.]{pampari2025} A smaller sub-net learned each enzyme's sequence biases based on non-peak regions signal, allowing ChromBPNet to isolate each motif's true footprint independent from assay used. This allows the model to predict, at single base resolution, the accessibility read profile (shape + total counts) that either ATAC-seq or DNase-seq would produce.

Geneformer applies the transformer playbook to a collection of 30M human scRNA-seq cells across various tissues, with cells tokenized as a ranked list of their top 2048 genes sorted by expression (no count information)\sjcitep[.]{theodoris2023transferlearning} A transformer architecture is then trained to fill in masked gene positions, implicitly learning patterns of co-expression and regulation across tissues and conditions present in the data. The model is designed to fine-tuned for specific tasks such as dosage-sensitivity classification, regulatory range prediction, and chromatin-state inference. scFoundation, another masked-prediction transformer, was trained on 100M single cells\sjcitep[.]{hao2024large} Varying size from 3M to 100M parameters showed that its loss curves followed the same token vs FLOPs power laws seen in language models.

Large datasets, most often sourced by scraping public repositories, have thus produced tools which perform well on generalized tasks, and can be finetuned with transfer learning on local datasets for specialised tasks. In short, scaling paradigms in general seem to hold up for most modalities of -omics model training assessed here. However, some observations warn against naive, premature data scaling:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
	\item In almost all the cases discussed above, hindsight optimisations in model architecture, data/compute allocation, data formats or variety and normalization, have shown that similar performance can often be achieved with fewer resources. Recently, this has also been shown for LLMs\sjcitep[.]{deepseekai2025deepseekr1incentivizingreasoningcapability}
	\item In many cases, smaller models trained on well-constrained systems such as specific tissues or disease models, perform better at precise tasks. Intelligent problem isolation can thus prevent waste of resources.
	\item Data depth and width seem to scale performance multiplicatively, rather than additively (e.g. number of cells versus individual cell sequencing depth).
\end{enumerate}

When predictive performance saturates, additional data can be used to increase the breadth or precision of the predicted elements (e.g. genomic window, or resolution).

One modality is palpably absent from the analysis above: perturbation effect predictions - either chemical or genetic. Of course, many of the DNA foundation models discussed predict variant effects, but they are trained on observational, evolutionary data. Few large, high-quality perturbation datasets exist so far - especially when comparing to the total space of possible perturbations, which is also extremely combinatorial. In order to predict all downstream effects of a given mutation, true perturbation data will be needed at scale. Extreme throughput arrayed perturbation remains an open technical challenge.

It is likely that the process of data scaling will self-reinforce. For one, better understanding of proteins and gene regulation will lead to better molecular tools (e.g. synthetic enzymes, optimal expression systems and vectors, better perturbation delivery methods).

Several of the datasets discussed here have been produced by privately funded research institutions (Google DeepMind, Meta, CZI, Calico, Arc, Tahoe), signaling that -omics technology may have been sufficiently derisked by academia for industry capital to be deployed. There is thus mounting market evidence that confidence in data scaling strategies is growing. While individual companies may fail locally, it seems more likely than not that the trend will continue, potentially at paces comparable to the scaling of transistors or next-generation sequencing technology. Overall, it is an incredibly exciting time for the field of systems biology, with space for technological progress and optimisation as far as the eye can see.
