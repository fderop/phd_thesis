\chapter{Scaling quantitative omics}
The study of biology has evolved through a series of overlapping technological and conceptual shifts. In the early 1900s, microscopic observations at the cellular level sparked interest in life as a mechanistic, physical system. By the mid-20th century, scientists began uncovering the biochemical mechanisms that underpinned this system: how information is stored in DNA physically, and how this information is retrieved through interactions between DNA, RNA, and proteins (\autoref{ch:early}). The 90s and early 2000s were marked by the complete sequencing of several large genomes, including of humans, subsequently allowing systematic cataloguing of its code at base-pair resolution. Shortly after, commodification of sequencing technology led to the development of high-throughput assays, allowing us to quantify RNA, chromatin state, and other DNA-derived species up to the single-cell level (\autoref{ch:quantitative}). Today, data-driven computational models have matured, enabling low-level predictions of various molecular systems (\autoref{ch:computational}).

The work described here encompasses two parts. First, we developed an open-source droplet microfluidic platform for scATAC-seq and scRNA-seq, and second, we benchmarked this assay together with other open-source and commercial protocols. Together, these efforts touched on all low-level aspects of omics data generation: we optimized reaction chemistries, engineered hardware, wrote software, managed large data structures, and coordinated high-power compute. The end result is (a) HyDrop, an open-source droplet-microfluidic single-cell assay that is competitive with commercial solutions (\autoref{ch:design_development}), and (b) \verb|PUMATAC|, a benchmarking framework for high-throughput single-cell ATAC-seq datasets (\autoref{ch:benchmarking}).

Overall, the work feeds into a broader, cross-disciplinary trend of generating large data explicitly for model training. This strategy first began to show results in games like go and chess\sjcitep[,]{sutton2019bitterlesson} but has recently manifested in more tangible applications such as self-driving cars\sjcitep[]{waymo2025scaling} and language models\sjcitep[.]{brown2020languagemodelsfewshotlearners} Exploring the parameter sensitivity ("scaling laws") of transformer models has shown that their performance continues to scale with model dimensions, dataset size, and training length - though model dimension was the largest contributor\sjcitep[.]{kaplan2020scalinglawsneurallanguage} Exploitation of these scaling laws has led to impressive results. For example, whereas Google's BERT was trained on just \SI{16}{\giga\byte} of uncompressed text in 2019\sjcitep[,]{devlin2019bertpretrainingdeepbidirectional} OpenAI's GPT-3 was trained on a total of \textasciitilde{\SI{700}{\giga\byte}} of compressed data sourced from Common Crawl (\SI{570}{\giga\byte}), Wikipedia, WebText2 and nearly all written books (\SI{120}{\giga\byte}) just a year later\sjcitep[.]{brown2020languagemodelsfewshotlearners} Compute was scaled orders of magnitude more, roughly \SI{7.8e20}{\flops} versus \SI{3.14e23}{\flops}. The resulting performance increase coupled with post-training into an instructable chat application has allowed GPT-3 to penetrate into consumer markets, eventually becoming the most rapidly adopted technology of all time\sjcitep[.]{parveen2024unraveling} While improvements were later made with architectural novelties like mixture of experts (MoE)\sjcitep[]{fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion} and reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF)\sjcitep[,]{ouyang2022traininglanguagemodelsfollow} it can be posed that the initial jump in performance was primarily caused by a disproportionally large commitment of data and compute.

A question that is much debated today is \textbf{whether or not the data + compute scaling paradigm has or will generalize to biological models} and lead to discontinuous performance increases there. The case studies in \autoref{ch:computational} seem to suggest so: brute-force scaling of data generally improves performance. However, deeper scrutiny reveals nuance:

First, protein-language models such as Meta's ESM family obey the same power-law curves that govern GPT-style LLMs: between 2021 and 2025, ESM grew from 650M\sjcitep[]{rives2021biological} to 15B\sjcitep[]{lin2023evolutionary} and recently 98B\sjcitep[]{hayes2025simulating} parameters. ESM-3 was trained on 771B tokens across 2.78B proteins, amended with synthetic data generated by AlphaFold, and trained over \SI{1.07e24}{\flops} of compute. Since ESM is de facto a domain-specific LLM trained on protein language, it is not surprising that transformer scaling laws apply to it. Google DeepMind's AlphaFold relied more on architectural innovation, such as introduction of attention with Evoformer blocks, end-to-end diffusion, than on raw parameter or data scaling. DeepMind has not released the precise training data used for AlphaFold 1-3, but it is generally thought that the jumps in size were not as large as for GPT and ESM: AlphaFold 1 was already trained on a large fraction of available PDB and MSA data, and AlphaFold 3's weights are only \SI{1}{\giga\byte} in size. When data constrained, model architecture innovations such as end-to-end modeling, introduction of attention and diffusion, are thus still viable paths to raw performance improvement. However, recent benchmarks have also shown that protein/ligand binding models, including AlphaFold 3, suffer performance drops when re-trained without train-test leakage\sjcitep[.]{plinder2024proteinligand} Ligands that are sparsely represented in the training data were especially problematic, suggesting that \textit{"data scarcity remains a major challenge for these methods"}\sjcitep[.]{skrinar2025proteinligand} Optimistically, these findings suggests that generation of additional or higher-quality data will yield further performance improvements in the space.

Second, smarter data engineering can rival or exceed sheer volume. Nucleotide Transformer gained more on human tasks by adding 850 non-human genomes than increasing model size from 500M to 2.5B parameters. Evo 2, another transformer model, showed that a 7B checkpoint trained on carefully re-weighted, gene-proximal sequence matches a 40B model on most clinical benchmarks while requiring only a tenth of the training compute. Larger models mainly unlocked generative capabilities: only the 40B Evo 2 model could synthesize coherent genome-scale DNA sequences.

Third, smaller, application-specific models may outperform native large foundation models. Enformer, ChromBPNet generalise after pre-training on thousands of multi-modal, tissue-specific genomic tracks. However, bespoke, domain-focused networks trained on single-cell data such as DeepMEL for melanoma, DeepFlyBrain for \textit{Drosophila}, and DeepZebrafish for embryogenesis, reach higher precision on enhancer logic with only a few million parameters, suggesting they internalize these systems more efficiently.

One modality is palpably absent these analyses: perturbation effect predictions - either chemical or genetic. The amount of pure genomic data available is large, and we can assume that the hundreds of thousands of genomes used in Evo 2 sample a significant fraction of evolutionary space. The training datasets used for these models are thus large relative to the spaces queried: genBank contains \SI{25}{\tera\bp} from 3.7 billion annotated sequences over \num{581000} species\sjcitep[.]{sayers2024genbank} Comparatively, the quantitative omics space is more sparse: GEO currently contains about \num{250000} datasets across 7.5 million samples, of which around \num{380000} are (sc)RNA-seq, and \num{44000} are (sc)ATAC-seq. Few large, high-quality perturbation datasets exist so far - especially when compared to the total space, which is also extremely combinatorial for both genetic and chemical perturbations. Of course, almost all models discussed predict variant effects, but they are trained on observational data. In order to predict all downstream effects of a given mutation, true perturbation data will be needed at scale. Recent work begins to close this gap: Tahoe delivers 100 million sci-derived single-cell transcriptomes of 50 diverse cancer lines perturbed with 1 to 100 small-molecule conditions (\textasciitilde\num{17800} unique line-drug pairs)\sjcitep[.]{zhang2025tahoe} \verb|State|, an accompanying transformer model, contrasts this perturbation atlas with 167 M observational cells sourced from public data to predict the effect of perturbations, exceeding mean-baselines for zero-shot ranking of drug effect sizes in unseen cell types by > 15\%\sjcitep[.]{youngblut2025scbasecount, adduri2025predicting}

Given the high labor and financial cost associated with (single-cell) sequencing assays, naively launching extreme scaling operations has not been entirely derisked, and, given the observations above, is likely not to be the only path to improvement. However, we are almost certaily at an inflection point. The cost of both sequencing assays and sequencing itself has been falling rapidly at predictable rates\sjcitep[.]{wetterstrand2023} What was at the start of this work considered a large atlas of \num{100000} cells can now be routinely generated in a few days, and 10 to 100 million cell datasets are being released. It is also likely that the process of data scaling will self-reinforce. For one, better understanding of proteins and gene regulation will lead to development of better molecular tools such as synthetic enzymes, optimal expression systems and vectors, better perturbation delivery methods. Improved models will also help increase the speed of validation and thus data generation, and may even generate synthetic data for finetuning. Advances in lab automation coupled with automated protocol generation through LLM support (PyLabRobot, Cursor) will further accelerate this process\sjcitep[.]{wierenga2023pylabrobot} Finally, several of the models and datasets discussed here have already been produced by privately funded research institutions (Google DeepMind, Meta, CZI, Calico, Arc, Tahoe). There is thus mounting market evidence that industry confidence in omics data scaling strategies is growing. As predictive performance increases, medicine and consumer products will further spur capital allocation.

While individual efforts may fail locally, it seems more likely than not that the data and model scaling trend in biology will continue, potentially at paces comparable to the scaling of transistors or even next-generation sequencing technology. Overall, it is an incredibly exciting time for the field of systems biology, with space for further technological progress and optimisation as far as the eye can see.
