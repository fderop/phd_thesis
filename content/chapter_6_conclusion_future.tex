\chapter{Scaling quantitative omics}
The study of biology has evolved through a series of overlapping technological and conceptual shifts. In the early 1900s, microscopic observations at the cellular level sparked interest in life as a mechanistic, physical system. By the mid-20th century, scientists began uncovering the biochemical mechanisms that underpinned this system: how information is stored in DNA physically, and how this information is retrieved through interactions between DNA, RNA, and proteins (\autoref{ch:early}). The 90s and early 2000s were marked by the complete sequencing of several large genomes, including of humans, subsequently allowing systematic cataloguing of its code at base-pair resolution. Shortly after, commodification of sequencing technology led to the development of high-throughput assays, allowing us to quantify RNA, chromatin state, and other DNA-derived species up to the single-cell level (\autoref{ch:quantitative}). Today, data-driven computational models have matured, enabling low-level predictions of various molecular systems (\autoref{ch:computational}).

The work described here encompasses two parts. First, we developed an open-source droplet microfluidic platform for scATAC-seq and scRNA-seq, and second, we benchmarked this assay together with other open-source and commercial protocols. Together, these efforts touched on all low-level aspects of omics data generation: we optimized reaction chemistries, engineered hardware, wrote software, managed large data structures, and coordinated high-power compute. The end result is (a) HyDrop, an open-source droplet-microfluidic single-cell assay that is competitive with commercial solutions (\autoref{ch:design_development}), and (b) \verb|PUMATAC|, a benchmarking framework for high-throughput single-cell ATAC-seq datasets (\autoref{ch:benchmarking}).

Overall, the work feeds into a broader, cross-disciplinary trend of generating large data explicitly for model training. This strategy first began to show results in games like go and chess\sjcitep[,]{sutton2019bitterlesson} but has recently manifested in more tangible applications such as self-driving cars\sjcitep[]{waymo2025scaling} and language models\sjcitep[.]{brown2020languagemodelsfewshotlearners} Exploring the parameter sensitivity ("scaling laws") of transformer models has shown that their performance continues to scale with model size, dataset size, and training length - although model size was the largest contributor\sjcitep[.]{kaplan2020scalinglawsneurallanguage}

Exploitation of these scaling laws has led to impressive results. For example, whereas Google's BERT was trained on just \SI{16}{\giga\byte} of uncompressed text in 2019\sjcitep[,]{devlin2019bertpretrainingdeepbidirectional} OpenAI's GPT-3 was trained on a total of \textasciitilde{\SI{700}{\giga\byte}} of compressed data sourced from Common Crawl (\SI{570}{\giga\byte}), Wikipedia, WebText2 and nearly all written books (\SI{120}{\giga\byte}) just a year later\sjcitep[.]{brown2020languagemodelsfewshotlearners} Compute was scaled orders of magnitude more, roughly \SI{7.8e20}{\flops} versus \SI{3.14e23}{\flops}. The resulting performance increase coupled with post-training into an instructable chat application has allowed GPT-3 to penetrate into consumer markets, eventually becoming the most rapidly adopted technology of all time\sjcitep[.]{parveen2024unraveling} While improvements were later made with architectural novelties like mixture of experts (MoE)\sjcitep[]{fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion} and reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF)\sjcitep[,]{ouyang2022traininglanguagemodelsfollow} it can be posed that the initial jump in performance was primarily caused by a disproportionally large commitment of data and compute.

Overall, transformer scaling laws in general seem to hold up for most modalities of -omics model training assessed here, especially for explicit transformer-like architectures. When predictive performance saturates, additional data can be used to increase the breadth or precision of the predicted elements (e.g. genomic context and resolution). However, some observations warn against premature data scaling:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
	\item In almost all the cases discussed above, hindsight optimisations in model architecture, data/compute allocation, data formats or variety and normalization, have shown that similar performance can often be achieved with fewer resources. Recently, this has also been shown for LLMs\sjcitep[.]{deepseekai2025deepseekr1incentivizingreasoningcapability}
	\item In many cases, smaller models trained on well-constrained systems such as specific tissues or disease models, perform better at precise tasks. Intelligent problem isolation can thus prevent waste of resources.
	\item Data depth and width seem to scale performance multiplicatively, rather than additively (e.g. number of cells versus individual cell sequencing depth).
\end{enumerate}

Given the high labor and financial cost associated with (single-cell) sequencing assays, for both library generation and sequencing, naively launching very large scaling operations can thus still backfire. But, we are almost certaily at an inflection point on this front. First, the cost of both sequencing assays and sequencing itself has been falling rapidly at predictable rates\sjcitep[.]{wetterstrand2023} What was at the start of this work considered a large atlas of \num{100000} cells can now be routinely generated in a few days, and 10 to 100 million cell datasets have been released recently. Second, it is likely that the process of data scaling will self-reinforce. For one, better understanding of proteins and gene regulation will lead to development of better molecular tools such as synthetic enzymes, optimal expression systems and vectors, better perturbation delivery methods. Improved models will also help increase the speed of validation and thus data generation, and may even generate synthetic data for finetuning. Advances in lab automation coupled with automated protocol generation with democratized LLM support through Cursor will accelerate this process\sjcitep[.]{wierenga2023pylabrobot} Third, several of the models and datasets discussed here have already been produced by privately funded research institutions (Google DeepMind, Meta, CZI, Calico, Arc, Tahoe). There is thus mounting market evidence that industry confidence in omics data scaling strategies is growing. As predictive performance grows, medicine and consumer products will further spur capital allocation.

While individual efforts may fail locally, it seems more likely than not that the data and model scaling trend in biology will continue, potentially at paces comparable to the scaling of transistors or even next-generation sequencing technology. Overall, it is an incredibly exciting time for the field of systems biology, with space for further technological progress and optimisation as far as the eye can see.
