\chapter*{Preface}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{\protect\numberline{}Preface}

Three events profoundly changed the course of my life.

First and foremost, around two weeks after I was conceived, while still a mostly spherical, round ball of cells, an "organizing center" spontaneously formed on my future back (or "dorsal") side. In this seemingly random location, a section of outer cells invaginated inwards, forming a dimple and then a pore, and ultimately the first cavity in my embryonic body. Further cell migrations and rounds of differentiation gave rise to all of my organs, including my brain and heart. Lewis Wolpert, one of \nth{20} century developmental biology's greatest, once joked that \textit{"it is not birth, marriage, or death, but gastrulation which is truly the most important time in your life."}\sjcitep[]{hopwoodNotBirthMarriage2022} In fact, gastrulation is considered as the moral boundary for embryonic research under the "14-day rule"\sjcitep[.]{applebyShould14Day2018} It speaks volumes to the importance of the other two events that they can compete with the process that practically sets me apart from a sponge.

The second key event of my life occurred around the age of twelve months, when I was adopted by my parents. This sudden (and quite unforeseen) change of decor allowed me to put my newly-gained consciousness to good use and escape the fate of growing up parentless in a third world country. While I cannot imagine life without gastrulation, I \textit{can} imagine what it would be like to grow up without all the opportunities and care in the world. As a result, I feel immensely grateful, and I have to give a shoutout to my parents by name. To you, they would be "Dominique \& Johan", but to me they will always be "Moeke \& Vake".

While I had absolutely no say in the first two, I most definitely had a hand in the third event: deciding to write my master's thesis, and later my PhD, at the lab of Stein Aerts. This period of five years was transformative for me on several levels. As a scientist, it was here that I was made, but also as a person it was a time of rapid growth. I came in as a snot-nosed young man with no noteworthy practical or even theoretical skills, and emerged as someone with clear life goals and the capabilities to reach them. Whenever I'm asked whether a PhD is a good time investment, I always respond "Don't do it. Unless you can do it at a really great lab, like Stein's." To maintain and craft a lab of that level from the ground up requires vision, unwavering dedication, and, above all, kindness. Stein, you set an example not only for what it means to be a scientist, but also a leader and a friend.

Suresh and Jasper, the three of you taught me almost everything I know, and I look up to each of you.  I feel privileged to have been a part of this collection of excellent people.

As you can tell, most of what I have achieved is the result of overwhelming luck, and only a little bit of hard work. It would have been a waste to do otherwise.
