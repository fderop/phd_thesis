\chapter{Discussion}
The work described in this thesis encompasses two parts. First, we developed an open-source droplet microfluidic platform for scATAC-seq and scRNA-seq, and second, we performed a systematic benchmark of scATAC-seq methods. Together, these efforts touched on all low-level aspects of omics data generation: we optimized reaction chemistries, engineered hardware, wrote software, managed large data structures, and coordinated high-power compute. The end result is (a) HyDrop, an open-source droplet-microfluidic single-cell assay that is competitive with commercial solutions (\autoref{ch:design_development}), and (b) \verb|PUMATAC|, a benchmarking framework for high-throughput single-cell ATAC-seq datasets (\autoref{ch:benchmarking}).

% The study of biology has evolved through a series of overlapping technological and conceptual shifts. In the early 1900s, microscopic observations at the cellular level sparked interest in life as a mechanistic, physical system. By the mid-20th century, scientists began uncovering the biochemical mechanisms that underpinned this system: how information is stored in DNA physically, and how this information is retrieved through interactions between DNA, RNA, and proteins (\autoref{sect:early}). The 90s and early 2000s were marked by the complete sequencing of several large genomes, including of humans, subsequently allowing systematic cataloguing of its code at base-pair resolution. Shortly after, commodification of sequencing technology led to the development of high-throughput assays, allowing us to quantify RNA, chromatin state, and other DNA-derived species up to the single-cell level (\autoref{sect:sequencing_assays}). Today, data-driven computational models have matured, enabling low-level predictions of various molecular systems (\autoref{sect:computational}).

We showed that HyDrop is capable of profiling \num{8000} cells per individual run, and has a high cell capture rate (75\%) even for very dilute samples. Single-cell sensitivity and specificity metrics comfortably outperformed other open-source droplet methods, and data integrated closely with 10x references datasets. The cost of reagents was less than \$0.03 per cell, which was significantly lower than commercial protocols like 10x Genomics. We open-sourced all designs and methods, opening up possibilities for further improvement and new assay development by the community. The technology has since been reproduced in other labs, and derivative products are available commercially\sjcitep[.]{ranbiotech_gelbeads_nochipdrop}

Awareness of the sensitivity and specificity biases described here is especially important in scATAC-seq studies compared to scRNA-seq studies, since peak region sets are sample-dependent rather than fixed gene sets. Therefore, high-specificity methods should be prioritised when generating reference peak region sets if public references (e.g. ENCODE ChIP-seq or DNase-seq) are not available a priori. Cost-efficient but lower sensitive/specificity open-source methods can be used for data generation at scale. Sequencing efficiency of these open-source methods can be improved by sequencing less deeply, since fewer reads will be lost to duplicates, and available resources can be allocated to sampling more cells rather than sequencing more. This strategy will lead to lower per-cell sensitivity, but increasing the total number of cells may be able to partially compensate. For example, both cell type annotation quality (\verb|seurat| label transfer scores) and DAR strength scaled with higher cell counts (fig. 3a). However, it is likely that scaling data horizontally (by cells only) will generate diminishing returns dependent on minimal baseline per-cell sensitivity required\sjcitep[.]{chiousinglecellmultiomic2023} Global optima for the trade-offs between cell counts, per-cell depth, and total experimental cost with respect to maximum predictive value remain an open problem, but this benchmark provides a strong indication of current performances, and a standardized evaluation framework for future datasets.

% here the part about critique on droplet methods

Microfluidic droplet-based assays require expertise to implement. Automating flow focusing droplet microfluidics is generally challenging, especially with standard automation hard- and software. The 10x Chromium Connect, an OEM Hamilton Nimbus liquid handler paired with an on-deck Chromium microfluidic controller, offers fully automated droplet generation and library prep, but is serial in nature: one batch of up to 8 samples is processed at a time. This approach does thus does not increase sample throughput without human intervention, and is best-suited for increased reproducibility and reduced hands-on time at small experimental scales.

Droplet generation itself is also a confounding process that affects the entire sample. If the process fails by the hand of dust, cell or DNA aggregates, air bubbles, chip imperfections, pump controller or gasket failures, etc., the entire sample is affected or lost completely. Manual droplet generation on an Onyx or Chromium followed by partial automation of the library prep is also impractical for many (96+) individual samples due to staggered delivery of samples and general sensitivity to delays. Plate-based single-cell methods can deal with sample multiplexing natively, for example by pre-sorting cells into plates using FACS, freezing the plates, and thawing the plates for processing on demand - a workflow which can be automated end to end. Moreover, the most recent SMART-seq assays have scaled down reagent cost through acoustic nanoliter dispensing and \num{1536} well-plate formats, and combinatorial indexing methods have come closer to 10x Chromium in terms of sensitivity.

Does this mean that droplet-based assays have no practical application for large scale data generation? Of course not. Since the release of the 10x scATAC-seq v2 kits, which can now reach up to \num{100000} unique fragments per cell, it has become clear that the sensitivity ceiling for droplet-based methods has not been attained by open-source droplet or plate-based scATAC-seq methods. For scRNA-seq, Smart-seq remains the sensitivity gold standard, but has yet to be applied to the same scales as even modest droplet-generated atlases - likely also owing to the high sequencing burden associated. Given only a small slowdown in 10x Genomics' annual kit sales\sjcitep[]{10xgenomics2025_10k} relative to the general capital slump in biotech and research post-2021, it appears that end-users are still willing to make the sensitivity/convenience/cost trade-off in favour of commercial droplet-microfluidics. Thus, how should we further develop the technology? Let us examine outstanding problems:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
	\item \textbf{Sample throughput is limited, even on parallel droplet generation chips.} To address this issue, antibody, lipid or oligonucleotide hashes or pre-indexing have been shown to scale throughput by at least 15x\sjcitep[,]{datlingerultrahighthroughput2021,mylkacomparativeanalysisantibody2022} and can scale both samples and absolute number of cells. These multiplexing methods are generally directly compatible with HyDrop's oligonucleotide sequence designs, but none of them are currently compatible with multiomics assays.
	\item \textbf{Emulsion-making is difficult to automate.} PIP-seq, a recent physical sample partitioning development, could offer a more generalizable sample throughput solution\sjcitep[.]{clarkMicrofluidicsFreeSingleCell2023} Here, droplet partitioning is achieved by vortexing aqueous (cells, enzyme mix, bead mix) and lipid phases until a stable emulsion is formed. This process can be performed in 96-well plates, and can thus be automated end to end. The original PIP-seq article only shows scRNA-seq through this method, and implementing scATAC-seq would pose some challenges: droplet PCR does not pair well with solid or hydrogel beads - the beads must dissolve to prevent emulsion coalescence. HyDrop and 10x achieve this by disulfide bonds which are reduced by contact with the cell and enzyme mix. PIP-seq requires cell and reagent mix to be pre-mixed and vortexed together, so the disulfide approach would not work. However, inDrop has previously used o-nitrobenzyl-mediated UV cleavage to release primers, an idea which could potentially be translated to PIP-ATAC-seq: UV-cleavable beads could be vortexed together with single-cells and enzymes, and the resulting emulsion could then be exposed to UV post-hoc. While this is impractical for manual lab processing, it should not pose a problem for automated workflows. Fluent BioSciences, which owns the IP to PIP-seq, and commercialized the assay, was recently acquired by Illumina - promising further development\sjcitep[.]{illumina2024fluent}
	\item \textbf{In-house bead production requires microfluidics and expertise.} After the HyDrop Elife paper was published, we were contacted by several labs who wanted to produce in-house beads, and correspondence revealed that bead production was the largest hurdle to implementation. One common issue was difficulty producing stable emulsions for bead generation, which may possibly be addressed by using PIP-seq-style emulsion generation for bead production. Besides emulsion making, the barcoding protocol is fully automatable on existing hardware.
\end{enumerate}

The issues presented above are related mostly to distribution, convenience and adoption rather than physical limits of droplet technology. Sensitivity differences between the open-source assay compared to the commercial alternative also a play a major role in adoption, and it would thus pay off to tip the balance closer towards the open-source alternative. Since the original publication of HyDrop, an improved version has been developed and open-sourced\sjcitep[,]{dickmanken2025hydrop} offering vastly improved sensitivity as well as more robust bead barcoding protocols. In this updated protocol, bead production was optimized for purity, strongly imprpoving sequencing efficiency. Additional optimisation of extraction and amplification procedures are likely to further push the sensitivity needle in favor of open-source protocols.

Overall, the work feeds into a broader, cross-disciplinary trend of generating large data explicitly for model training. This strategy first began to show results in games like go and chess\sjcitep[,]{sutton2019bitterlesson} but has recently manifested in more tangible applications such as self-driving cars\sjcitep[]{waymo2025scaling} and language models\sjcitep[.]{brown2020languagemodelsfewshotlearners} Exploring the parameter sensitivity ("scaling laws") of transformer models has shown that their performance continues to scale with model dimensions, dataset size, and training length - though model dimension was the largest contributor\sjcitep[.]{kaplan2020scalinglawsneurallanguage} Exploitation of these scaling laws has led to impressive results. For example, whereas Google's BERT was trained on just \SI{16}{\giga\byte} of uncompressed text in 2019\sjcitep[,]{devlin2019bertpretrainingdeepbidirectional} OpenAI's GPT-3 was trained on a total of \textasciitilde{\SI{700}{\giga\byte}} of compressed data sourced from Common Crawl (\SI{570}{\giga\byte}), Wikipedia, WebText2 and nearly all written books (\SI{120}{\giga\byte}) just a year later\sjcitep[.]{brown2020languagemodelsfewshotlearners} Compute was scaled orders of magnitude more, roughly \SI{7.8e20}{\flops} versus \SI{3.14e23}{\flops}. The resulting performance increase coupled with post-training into an instructable chat application has allowed GPT-3 to penetrate into consumer markets, eventually becoming the most rapidly adopted technology of all time\sjcitep[.]{parveen2024unraveling} While improvements were later made with architectural novelties like mixture of experts (MoE)\sjcitep[]{fedus2022switchtransformersscalingtrillion} and reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF)\sjcitep[,]{ouyang2022traininglanguagemodelsfollow} it can be posed that the initial jump in performance was primarily caused by a disproportionally large commitment of data and compute.

A question that is much debated today is \textbf{whether or not the data + compute scaling paradigm generalizes to biological models} and lead to discontinuous performance increases there. The case studies in \autoref{sect:computational} seem to suggest so: brute-force scaling of data generally improves performance. However, deeper scrutiny reveals nuance:

First, protein-language models such as Meta's ESM family obey the same power-law curves that govern GPT-style LLMs: between 2021 and 2025, ESM grew from 650M\sjcitep[]{rives2021biological} to 15B\sjcitep[]{lin2023evolutionary} and recently 98B\sjcitep[]{hayes2025simulating} parameters. ESM-3 was trained on 771B tokens across 2.78B proteins, amended with synthetic data generated by AlphaFold, and trained over \SI{1.07e24}{\flops} of compute. Since ESM is de facto a domain-specific LLM trained on protein language, it is not surprising that transformer scaling laws apply to it. Google DeepMind's AlphaFold relied more on architectural innovation, such as introduction of attention with Evoformer blocks, end-to-end diffusion, than on raw parameter or data scaling. DeepMind has not released the precise training data used for AlphaFold 1-3, but it is generally thought that the jumps in size were not as large as for GPT and ESM: AlphaFold 1 was already trained on a large fraction of available PDB and MSA data, and AlphaFold 3's weights are only \SI{1}{\giga\byte} in size. When data constrained, model architecture innovations such as end-to-end modeling, introduction of attention and diffusion, are thus still viable paths to raw performance improvement. However, recent benchmarks have also shown that protein/ligand binding models, including AlphaFold 3, suffer performance drops when re-trained without train-test leakage\sjcitep[.]{plinder2024proteinligand} Ligands that are sparsely represented in the training data were especially problematic, suggesting that \textit{"data scarcity remains a major challenge for these methods"}\sjcitep[.]{skrinar2025proteinligand} Optimistically, these findings suggests that generation of additional or higher-quality data will yield further performance improvements in the space.

Second, smarter data engineering can rival or exceed sheer volume. Nucleotide Transformer gained more on human tasks by adding 850 non-human genomes than increasing model size from 500M to 2.5B parameters. Evo 2, another transformer model, showed that a 7B checkpoint trained on carefully re-weighted, gene-proximal sequence matches a 40B model on most clinical benchmarks while requiring only a tenth of the training compute. Larger models mainly unlocked generative capabilities: only the 40B Evo 2 model could synthesize coherent genome-scale DNA sequences.

Third, smaller, application-specific models may outperform native large foundation models. Models like Enformer and ChromBPNet generalise after pre-training on thousands of multi-modal, tissue-specific genomic tracks. However, bespoke, domain-focused networks trained on single-cell data such as DeepMEL for melanoma, DeepFlyBrain for \textit{Drosophila}, and DeepZebrafish for embryogenesis, reach higher precision on enhancer logic with only a few million parameters, suggesting they internalize these systems more efficiently.

One modality is palpably absent these analyses: perturbation effect predictions - either chemical or genetic. The amount of pure genomic data available is large, and we can assume that the hundreds of thousands of genomes used in Evo 2 sample a significant fraction of evolutionary space. The training datasets used for these models are thus large relative to the spaces queried: genBank contains \SI{25}{\tera\bp} from 3.7 billion annotated sequences over \num{581000} species\sjcitep[.]{sayers2024genbank} Comparatively, the quantitative omics space is more sparse: GEO currently contains about \num{250000} datasets across 7.5 million samples, of which around \num{380000} are (sc)RNA-seq, and \num{44000} are (sc)ATAC-seq. Few large, high-quality perturbation datasets exist so far - especially when compared to the total space, which is also extremely combinatorial for both genetic and chemical perturbations. Of course, almost all models discussed predict variant effects, but they are trained on observational data. In order to predict all downstream effects of a given mutation, true perturbation data will be needed at scale. Recent work begins to close this gap: Tahoe delivers 100 million sci-derived single-cell transcriptomes of 50 diverse cancer lines perturbed with 1 to 100 small-molecule conditions (\textasciitilde\num{17800} unique line-drug pairs)\sjcitep[.]{zhang2025tahoe} State, an accompanying transformer model, contrasts this perturbation atlas with 167 M observational cells sourced from public data to predict the effect of perturbations, exceeding mean-baselines for zero-shot ranking of drug effect sizes in unseen cell types by > 15\%\sjcitep[.]{youngblut2025scbasecount, adduri2025predicting} Retraining State on downsampled scBaseCamp could also provide the first true, quantitative scaling laws for omics.

Given the high labor and financial cost associated with (single-cell) sequencing assays, naively launching extreme scaling operations has not been entirely derisked, and, given the observations above, is likely not the only path to improvement. However, we are almost certaily at an inflection point. The cost of both sequencing assays and sequencing itself has been falling rapidly at predictable rates\sjcitep[.]{wetterstrand2023} What was at the start of this work considered a large atlas of \num{100000} cells can now be routinely generated in a few days, and 10 to 100 million cell datasets are being released. It is also likely that the process of data scaling will self-reinforce. For one, better understanding of proteins and gene regulation will lead to development of better molecular tools such as synthetic enzymes, optimal expression systems and vectors, and better perturbation delivery methods. Improved models will also help increase the speed of validation and thus data generation, and may even generate synthetic data for finetuning. Advances in lab automation coupled with automated protocol generation through LLM support (PyLabRobot, Cursor) will further accelerate this process\sjcitep[.]{wierenga2023pylabrobot} Finally, several of the models and datasets discussed here have already been produced by privately funded research institutions (Google DeepMind, Meta, CZI, Calico, Arc, Tahoe). There is thus mounting market evidence that industry confidence in omics data scaling strategies is growing. As predictive performance increases, medicine and consumer products will further spur capital allocation.

While individual efforts may fail locally, it seems more likely than not that the data and model scaling trend in biology will continue, potentially at paces comparable to the scaling of transistors or even next-generation sequencing technology.
