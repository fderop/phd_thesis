\chapter{Discussion}
The work described in this thesis encompasses two parts. First, we developed HyDrop, an open-source droplet microfluidic platform for scATAC-seq and scRNA-seq, which is competitive with commercial solutions. Second, we developed \verb|PUMATAC|, a method-agnostic framework for pre-processing and evaluating scATAC-seq datasets, and used it to perform a systematic benchmark of current methods. We open-sourced all protocols and software, opening possibilities for further improvement. HyDrop has since been reproduced in other labs, and derivative products are available commercially\sjcitep[.]{ranbiotech_gelbeads_nochipdrop} Together, the work touches on all low-level aspects of omics data generation: we optimized reaction chemistries, engineered hardware, wrote software, managed large data structures, and coordinated high-power compute.

Despite our advancements in open-sourcing methods, microfluidic droplet-based assays still require expertise to implement. Automating flow focusing droplet microfluidics is generally challenging, especially with standard automation hard- and software. The 10x Chromium Connect, an OEM Hamilton Nimbus liquid handler paired with an on-deck Chromium microfluidic controller, offers fully automated droplet generation and library prep, but is serial in nature: one batch of up to 8 samples is processed at a time. This approach thus does not increase sample throughput without human intervention, and is best-suited for small experimental scales. Conversely, Tahoe-100M was generated mostly by one person using 11 well-plates of Parse Bio's Evercode WT Mega kits\sjcitep[,]{zhang2025tahoe} which are also amenable to automation since they are plate-native.

Droplet generation itself is also a confounding process that affects the entire sample. If the process fails by the hand of dust, cell or DNA aggregates, air bubbles, chip imperfections, pump controller or gasket failures, etc., the entire sample is affected or lost completely. Manual droplet generation on an Onyx or Chromium followed by partial automation of the library prep is also impractical for many (96+) individual samples due to staggered delivery of samples and general sensitivity to delays. Plate-based single-cell methods can deal with sample multiplexing natively, for example by pre-sorting cells into plates using FACS, freezing the plates, and thawing the plates for processing on demand - a workflow which can be automated end to end. Moreover, the most recent SMART-seq assays have scaled down reagent cost through acoustic nanoliter dispensing and \num{1536} well-plate formats, and combinatorial indexing methods have come closer to 10x Chromium in terms of sensitivity.

Does this mean that droplet-based assays have no practical application for large scale data generation? Of course not. Tahoe-100M's cells were filtered on a minimum of 700 umis and 250 genes: 10x scRNA-seq undoubtedly still offers higher per-cell quality metrics. Furthermore, since the release of the 10x scATAC-seq v2 kits, which can now reach up to \num{100000} unique fragments per cell, it has become clear that the sensitivity ceiling for droplet-based methods has not been attained by open-source droplet or plate-based scATAC-seq methods. For scRNA-seq, Smart-seq remains the sensitivity gold standard, but has yet to be applied to the same scales as even modest droplet-generated atlases - likely also owing to the high sequencing burden associated. Given only a small slowdown in 10x Genomics' annual kit sales\sjcitep[]{10xgenomics2025_10k} relative to the general capital slump in biotech and research post-ZIRP, it appears that end-users are still willing to make the sensitivity/convenience/cost trade-off in favour of commercial droplet-microfluidics. Thus, how should we further develop the technology? Let us examine outstanding problems and their potential solutions:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
	\item \textbf{Sample throughput is limited, even on parallel droplet generation chips.} To address this issue, antibody, lipid or oligonucleotide hashes or pre-indexing have been shown to scale throughput by at least 15x\sjcitep[,]{datlingerultrahighthroughput2021,mylkacomparativeanalysisantibody2022} and can scale both samples and absolute number of cells. These multiplexing methods are generally directly compatible with HyDrop's oligonucleotide sequence designs, but none of them are currently compatible with multiomics assays.
	\item \textbf{Emulsion-making is difficult to automate.} PIP-seq, a recent physical sample partitioning development, could offer a more generalizable sample throughput solution\sjcitep[.]{clarkMicrofluidicsFreeSingleCell2023} Here, droplet partitioning is achieved by vortexing aqueous (cells, enzyme mix, bead mix) and lipid phases until a stable emulsion is formed. This process can be performed in 96-well plates, and can be fully automated! The original PIP-seq article only shows scRNA-seq through this method, and implementing scATAC-seq would pose some challenges: droplet PCR does not pair well with solid or hydrogel beads - the beads must dissolve to prevent emulsion coalescence. HyDrop and 10x achieve this by disulfide bonds which are reduced by contact with the cell and enzyme mix. PIP-seq requires cell and reagent mix to be pre-mixed and vortexed together, so the disulfide approach would not work. However, inDrop has previously used o-nitrobenzyl-mediated UV cleavage to release primers, an idea which could potentially be translated to PIP-ATAC-seq: UV-cleavable beads could be vortexed together with single-cells and enzymes, and the resulting emulsion could then be exposed to UV post-hoc. While this is impractical for manual lab processing, it should not pose a problem for a lights-out automated workflow. Fluent BioSciences, which owns the IP to PIP-seq, and commercialized the assay, was recently acquired by Illumina - promising further development\sjcitep[.]{illumina2024fluent}
	\item \textbf{In-house bead production requires microfluidics and expertise.} After the HyDrop Elife paper was published, we were contacted by several labs who wanted to produce in-house beads, and correspondence revealed that bead production was the largest hurdle to implementation. One common issue was difficulty producing stable emulsions for bead generation, which may possibly be addressed by using PIP-seq-style emulsion generation for bead production as well. Aside from emulsion making, the current barcoding protocol is already fully automatable on existing hardware.
\end{enumerate}

The issues presented above are related mostly to distribution and convenience rather than physical limits of droplet technology. Sensitivity differences between the open-source assay compared to the commercial alternative also play a major role in adoption, and it would thus pay off to tip the balance closer towards the open-source alternative. Since the original publication of HyDrop, an improved version has been developed and open-sourced\sjcitep[,]{dickmanken2025hydrop} offering vastly improved sensitivity as well as more robust bead barcoding protocols. In this updated protocol, bead production was optimized for purity, strongly improving sequencing efficiency and reaching quality parity with 10x. Additional optimisation of extraction and amplification procedures are likely to further push the sensitivity needle in favor of open-source protocols.

Overall, our project feeds into a broader, cross-disciplinary trend of scaling large data explicitly for model training. This trend first began to show results in games like chess and Go\sjcitep[,]{sutton2019bitterlesson} but has recently manifested in more tangible applications such as medical imaging, self-driving cars\sjcitep[,]{waymo2025scaling} and language models\sjcitep[.]{brown2020languagemodelsfewshotlearners} For example, whereas Google's BERT was trained on just \SI{16}{\giga\byte} of uncompressed text in 2019\sjcitep[,]{devlin2019bertpretrainingdeepbidirectional} OpenAI's GPT-3 was trained on a total of \textasciitilde{\SI{700}{\giga\byte}} of compressed data sourced from Common Crawl (\SI{570}{\giga\byte}), Wikipedia, WebText2 and nearly all written books (\SI{120}{\giga\byte}) just a year later\sjcitep[.]{brown2020languagemodelsfewshotlearners} Compute was scaled orders of magnitude more, roughly \SI{7.8e20}{\flops} versus \SI{3.14e23}{\flops}. The resulting performance improvement, which was primarily the result of a large commitment of data and compute, has allowed GPT-3 to penetrate into consumer markets, eventually becoming the most rapidly adopted technology of all time\sjcitep[.]{parveen2024unraveling} Exploring the parameter sensitivity ("scaling laws") of transformer models has shown that their performance continues, and is likely to continue, to scale sublinearly with model dimensions, training dataset size, and compute\sjcitep[.]{kaplan2020scalinglawsneurallanguage, hoffmann2022trainingcomputeoptimallargelanguage}

A question that is much debated today is \textbf{whether the data + compute scaling paradigm will generalize to biological models}. Increasing model and dataset scale has improved performance on several evaluated biological tasks. Gains depend on the task, data composition, and evaluation design\sjcitep[.]{brixi2025genome,adduri2025predicting} Especially transformer-based protein-language models, notably Meta's ESM family, seem to obey the same scaling laws as their natural language counterparts: between 2020 and 2025, the model family iterated from 650M\sjcitep[]{rives2021biological} to 15B\sjcitep[]{lin2023evolutionary} and recently 98B\sjcitep[]{hayes2025simulating} parameters, adding generative features.

% ESM-3 was trained on 771B tokens across 2.78B proteins, amended with synthetic data generated by AlphaFold, and trained over \SI{1.07e24}{\flops} of compute.

% Since ESM is de facto a domain-specific LLM trained on protein language, it is not surprising that transformer scaling laws apply to it.

However, deeper scrutiny reveals more nuance. First, architectural novelty has in some cases outshined pure scaling, especially in regimes where data is scarce. For example, AlphaFold 1 was already trained on a large fraction of available PDB and MSA data, and AlphaFold 3's weights are only \SI{1}{\giga\byte} in size. Introduction of attention with Evoformer blocks and end to end diffusion led to the largest performance jumps. Recent benchmarks have also shown that protein/ligand binding models suffer performance drops when re-trained with more stringent train-test leakage filtering\sjcitep[.]{plinder2024proteinligand} Performance on ligands that were sparsely represented in the training data was especially weak, suggesting that \textit{"data scarcity remains a major challenge for these methods"}\sjcitep[.]{skrinar2025proteinligand}

Second, smarter data engineering can rival or exceed sheer volume. Nucleotide Transformer improved more on human tasks by adding 850 non-human genomes than by increasing model size from 500M to 2.5B parameters. Evo 2, another transformer model, showed that a 7B checkpoint trained on carefully re-weighted, gene-proximal sequences matches a 40B model on most clinical benchmarks while requiring only a tenth of the training compute. Larger models mainly unlocked generative capabilities: only the 40B Evo 2 model could synthesize coherent genome-scale DNA sequences.

Third, smaller, application-specific models routinely outperform large foundation models in constrained contexts. Bespoke, domain-focused networks trained from scratch on single-cell data such as DeepMEL for melanoma, DeepFlyBrain for \textit{Drosophila}, and DeepZebrafish for embryogenesis, reach higher precision than Enformer and ChromBPNet on enhancer logic while only a few million parameters in size.

One modality is palpably absent in the analysis above: perturbation effect prediction. The amount of pure genomic data available is large: genBank contains \SI{25}{\tera\bp} from 3.7B annotated sequences in \num{581000} species\sjcitep[,]{sayers2024genbank} compared to the \textasciitilde{1.8 million} named eukaryotic species\sjcitep[.]{gupta2022earthbiogenome} The training data is thus representative of evolutionary space. Conversely, the quantitative omics space has been sampled much more sparsely: GEO currently contains about \num{250000} datasets across 7.5 million samples, of which around \num{380000} are (sc)RNA-seq, and \num{45000} are (sc)ATAC-seq. High-quality perturbation datasets are even fewer in number. This is especially problematic considering that the effect space is also highly combinatorial for both genetic and chemical perturbations. Note that in our benchmark we have shown high levels of inefficiency in most omics datasets: gigabytes of \verb|.fastq|-formated sequencing data often reduce to sparse matrices of fewer than \SI{50}{\mega\byte} in size.

Recent work begins to close this data sparsity gap: Tahoe has delivered 100 million sci-derived single-cell transcriptomes of 50 diverse cancer lines perturbed with 1 to 100 small-molecule conditions (\textasciitilde\num{17800} unique line-drug pairs)\sjcitep[.]{zhang2025tahoe} \verb|State|, an accompanying transformer model, contrasts this perturbation atlas with 167M observational cells sourced from public data to predict the effect of perturbations, exceeding mean-baselines for zero-shot ranking of drug effect sizes in unseen cell types by > 15\%\sjcitep[.]{youngblut2025scbasecount, adduri2025predicting} Retraining \verb|State| on downsampled scBaseCamp could also provide the first quantitative scaling laws for omics modeling.

Given the high labor and financial cost associated with (single-cell) sequencing assays, and the open question whether the scaling strategy will pay off proportionally, naively launching extreme scaling operations has not been entirely derisked. Considering the nuances above, it is unlikely to be the optimal path forward with our current knowledge. However, we are almost certainly at an inflection point in world-total data generation capacity. The cost of sequencing and quantitative sequencing assays has been falling rapidly at predictable rates\sjcitep[.]{wetterstrand2023} What was at the start of this work considered a large atlas of \num{100000} cells can now be routinely generated in a few days, and 10 to 100 million cell datasets are being released. It is also likely that the process of data scaling will self-reinforce. Better understanding of proteins and gene regulation will lead to development of better molecular tools such as synthetic enzymes, optimal expression systems, and better perturbation delivery methods. Improved models will also improve experimental design through more accurate hypothesis (and thus informative data) generation, and may even generate synthetic data for finetuning. Advances in lab automation coupled with automated protocol generation through LLM support (PyLabRobot) will further accelerate this process\sjcitep[.]{wierenga2023pylabrobot} Finally, several of the models and datasets discussed here have been produced by privately-funded research institutions (Google DeepMind, Meta, CZI, Calico, Arc, Tahoe), and several automated omics assay providers (Plasmidsaurus, Adaptyv Bio) are emerging. There is thus mounting evidence that industry confidence in omics data scaling strategies is growing. Whereas today's biological models gather mostly all available data to produce a state-of-the-art result, a "GPT moment" for biology could spur a data economy in which model trainers generate a demand for specific data. This switch from a supply-driven to a demand-driven economy has effectively already happened in consumer AI, as thousands of people are now employed as data labelers for various data modalities, and new methods for embodied and physical data generation are being commercialized\sjcitep[.]{GrandViewResearch_DataLabeling_2024} As model predictive performance increases, downstream medicine and consumer products will further stimulate capital attention and thus model scale. In that sense, the biotech and pharmaceutical industry may soon become a data economy where information becomes the primary currency of discovery.

Several challenges will slow such a transition down. The most difficult problem lies in identifying which data types will be worth producing at scale. Assuming a fixed budget, and a goal of "predict gene regulation in disease" (essentially, current virtual cell efforts), several factors affect how we should design a data collection effort:

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
	\item \textbf{Which data type(s) to collect?} Within sequencing assays, we would need to decide on modality (DNA versus accessible chromatin, RNA, Hi-C, methylation, ...), bulk versus single-cell, long versus short reads and so on. Beyond sequencing, we also have dozens of imaging techniques, intracellular and surface protein measurements, functional assays, ... For a given measurement, there is currently no better proxy for "information content" than raw compressed bytes generated per condition per money spent. The best quantitative measure might in fact be post-hoc predictive power of a well-designed and saturated model.
	\item \textbf{On which sample?} Omics measurements are generally destructive (although live-cell imaging methods can now monitor selected RNA species\sjcitep[).]{zuo2026livecell} As a result, human studies have  relied predominantly on blood, or on tissue obtained through clinically indicated biopsies, resections, and banked samples. While the translational benefit of testing on human samples is directly evident, these samples are not only scarce, but also difficult to obtain due to regulatory and privacy concerns. From a pure information gathering perspective, it is unclear if focusing exclusively on human tissue is the correct call today. For a given budget, deep sampling of model organisms could yield foundation models which strongly recapitulate biology and transfer to human genomes. For example, yeast retains nucleosomes and histone modifications, including methylation, although it lacks cytosine DNA methylation system found in mammals\sjcitep[.]{carrozza2005set2,capuano2014methylation} Cultivating yeast and rearing flies, across many strains and mutants, is more practical than clinical sample collection at scale, and well-controlled genetic backgrounds are a significant benefit. Patient-derived organoids offer a potential compromise between clinical relevance and experimental scalability. However, while better than cell lines, organoids still fail to reconstruct tissue interactions and microenvironment and are also unstable, often being strongly dependent on quality of input iPSCs\sjcitep[.]{anderssonrolf2026organoids} 
	\item \textbf{Using which type of automation?} Even if up-front funding were limitless, scaling up data collection beyond simply using more personnel/machines is still an underappreciated issue. Most hypothesis testing efforts, today, require many different machines, used over various days, and these combinations are generally unique to a project. Moreover, there is certainly a lot of skill involved in collecting and processing samples. Committing to a single scaled data pipeline, where a tissue or other sample is entered one end, and data comes out the other, will require at least the re-designing of existing machines and their close integration with liquid handlers. Humanoid robots might assist during a transitory period, but for large scaling project, they will most likely not be deployed beyond maintenance. Similar to what droplet microfluidics did for single-cell, automated capillary gel-electrophoresis did for DNA sequencing, flow cytometry did for cell sorting, ... we are likely to see more investment in specialized monotask machines. For example, a machine that could ingest an arbitrary tissue, and return a highly viable single-cell suspension \textit{reliably} still seems beyond reach for now.
	\item \textbf{Investigating which perturbation(s), if any?} Some of the largest current data collection efforts use massively parallel perturbation sampling with single-cell transcriptomic readouts, including drug perturbations and genetic Perturb-seq\sjcitep[.]{tahoe2026perturbation,huang2025xatlas,zhu2026cd4perturbseq} 
	\item \textbf{And how important is the time dimension?}
	
	erturbation experiments add information about how that state responds to intervention. Perturb-seq combines these choices by coupling genetic perturbations to single-cell transcriptomic readouts\sjcitep[.]{dixit2016perturbseq} For a defined prediction task, however, additional molecular or cellular resolution must justify the experimental coverage sacrificed to obtain it. In relatively homogeneous cultures, bulk RNA-seq combined with imaging could be compared with single-cell profiling under the same budget, allocating any savings to more perturbations, genetic backgrounds and time points. In heterogeneous tissues, the value of additional resolution would instead depend on whether identifying cell-type-specific responses changes the predictions or experimental decisions. Neither strategy should be preferred solely on the basis of the number of features measured.

\end{enumerate}
