\section{What is needed?}
Large screens that investigate the effect of silencing specific genes using RNA interference emerged in the early 2000s\sjcitep[.]{berns2004large,boutros2006analysis} Such perturbation screens followed either an arrayed design, where perturbations are individually plated and tracked, or pooled design, where cell lines are transduced with multiplexed shRNA vector libraries and survivors are sequenced. When CRISPR was developed as a molecular tool for rapid and accurate gene editing in 2013\sjcitep[,]{ran2013genome} full genetic knockouts became widely available, directly leading to a pooled genome-wide screen involving 64000 sgRNA's for 18000 gene knockouts just a year later\sjcitep[.]{shalem2014genome, behan2019prioritization, pacini2021integrated}

Around 2014, researchers at the Broad institute realised that single-cell sequencing would allow further parallelization of perturbation screens by reading out perturbations at the single-cell level\sjcitep[.]{dixit2016perturbseq} In their method, named Perturb-seq, each vector is designed to express two transcripts after integrating in the host genome: a first transcript containing the sgRNA, driven by U6, and a second transcript containing a puromycin selection cassette, BFP and a 20 nucleotide long guide barcode (GBC), driven by EF1\textalpha. The GBC transcript is polyadenylated, allowing it to be captured and counted through standard 3' scRNA-seq protocols (10x GEX in this specific case). The \{sgRNA:GBC\} dictionary can then be used to identify the genetic edit present in each captured cell \sjcitep[.]{dixit2016perturbseq} This pooled approach allows rapid parallelisation of perturbation screens with limited labour. Perturb-seq's costs can be further scaled down by multiplexing perturbations or cells: if gene regulatory networks are assumed sparse in number of participants, then the differential expression of a cell responding to multiple perturbations (high MOI), or a droplet containing multiple single-perturbation cells, can be considered a linear combination of the effect of its components. Pooling cells or perturbations in this manner and demultiplexing the individual terms using PCA and LASSO [double check] can reduce cost ten-fold and quantify interaction terms between different perturbations\sjcitep[.]{yao2024scalable}

Perturbation screens have also been coupled with scATAC-seq, but at lower throughput. The transfection protocol still happens in a pooled manner, using an expressed GBC for downstream demultiplexing. However, the single-cell capture happens using Fluidigm's C1, limiting the size of the study to \~4300 cells total. Cells are isolated in microfluidic low-potential wells, where their GBCs are reverse-transcribed. Fluidigm's proprietary scATAC-seq protocol is then followed to generate coupled ATAC-seq profiles\sjcitep[.]{rubin2019coupled} In this study, specific TFs were perturbed (either through CRISPRi or CRISPR knockout) in order to assess their effect on chromatin remodeling. Spear-ATAC\sjcitep[]{pierce2021high} is able to overcome Perturb-ATAC's throughput limitations by using droplet encapsulation (10x Chromium). After pooled lentiviral transduction, cells undergo the regular 10x scATAC-seq workflow, but with an additional primer added to the droplet encapsulation step. This primer anneals to the integrated lentiviral cassette, amplifying its barcode. By using droplet-based methods, Greenleaf lab was able to profile 414 sgRNA knockdowns across 105k cells (sampled between 3 and 21 days post transduction). Analyzing these time series revealed time-dependent effects of the knockdowns. For example, GATA1 knockdown was found to first upregulate STAT5 motif accessibility, but later RUNX motifs. Cell-type specific GRNs were also observed between K562, MCF-7 and GM12878 cells.


One disadvantage of the screening methods above is that most of the vectors described do not contain Cas9, limiting the studies to transgenic and immortal cell lines. Most perturbation studies so far also deal with CRISPRa/i, CRISPR knockouts, but not yet with synthetic gene expression knock-ins. Such studies are considerably more difficult to implement in a pooled manner, since transcript length has an effect on transfection, packaging and transduction efficiency, which bias MOI. These effects call for the titration of viral concentrations and arrayed screening, which is expensive and laborious.

Sample multiplexing for droplet microfluidics has also been achieved through other means. The main strategy is to pre-index or "hash" multiple samples with oligonucleotide barcodes, co-process them through droplet generation together, and use the sample hash to computationally separate cells into sample bins post hoc. A number of different technical approaches to do this exist. Cells or nuclei can be tagged by attaching sample-barcoded oligonucleotides to their membranes, either by antibody-antigen binding\sjcitep[]{stoeckiusCellHashing2018} or lipid/cholesterol embedding\sjcitep[.]{mcginnisMultiSeqSample2019} Within each droplet, the membrane-bound oligonucleotides co-captured together with the associated cell's mRNA or tagmented genome. Antibodies have demonstrated slightly better accuracy, while lipid hashing can be used when high-strength antibodies are not available\sjcitep[.]{mylkaComparativeAnalysisAntibody2022} The increased precision of antibody-based methods could be attributed to the many successive washes involved, but which may also reduce cell viability and integrity.

Another sample multiplexing strategy is scifi-RNA-seq, where cells or nuclei are distributed over 96-well plates and pre-indexed by reverse transcription with a barcoded primer. Cells can then be pooled and run through droplet generation together\sjcitep[.]{datlingerUltraHighThroughput2021} Beyond just sample multiplexing, scifi-RNA-seq offers a major advantage over lipid and antibody hashes: since each cell's nucleic acid species are pre-indexed, multiple cells can be loaded into the same droplet, which can be used to multiply the total number of cells profiled per droplet run. Whereas the 10x Chromium was designed for ~10 000 cells per channel, scifi-RNA-seq increases this to up to 1.53 million cells per channel without apparent reduction in per-cell data quality.

A number of ATAC-seq specific multiplexing techniques depending on the straightforward "indexability" of Tn5 have also been published.

One weakness of droplet methods: difficult to automate.
Droplet-based assays require large amounts of labour and skill to implement. Automating flow-focusing-based droplet microfluidics is generally challenging. The 10x Chromium Connect, a modified Hamilton Nimbus liquid handler with an on-deck Chromium, offers fully automated droplet generation and library prep, but is serial in nature: one batch of up to 8 samples is processed at a time. This approach does thus does not offer increased sample throughput. Droplet generation itself is also a confounding process that affects the entire sample. If the process fails (by the hand of dust, cell or DNA aggregates, air bubbles, chip imperfections, pump controller or gasket failures, ...), the entire sample can be compromised, or will at least suffer a delay. Manual droplet generation on an Onyx or Chromium followed by partial automation of the library prep only is also not very practical for many (96+) samples due to time spent on ice before/after droplet generation for the first/last samples. Native plate-based methods deal with this problem more elegantly, for example by pre-sorting cells into plates using FACS, freezing the plates, and thawing the plates for processing on demand - a workflow which can be automated end to end. Moreover, the most recent SMART-seq assays have scaled down reagent cost through the use of acoustic nanoliter dispensing and 1536 well-plate formats, and combinatorial indexing methods have come closer to 10x Chromium in terms of sensitivity. Does this mean that droplet-based assays have no practical application for the large scale of data generation required to feed the beast?

One development I find very interesting is PIP-seq\sjcitep[.]{clarkMicrofluidicsFreeSingleCell2023} Here, droplet partitioning is achieved by vortexing aqueous and lipid phases until an emulsion is achieved, which can be performed in 96-well plates. It is thus conceivable that the entire workflow can be automated end to end. The original Clark article only shows scRNA-seq through this method. scATAC-seq poses a challenge: recall from our HyDrop work that droplet PCR does not go well with solid or hydrogel beads - the beads must dissolve. HyDrop and 10x achieve this by disulfide bonds which are cleaved when they are reduced by contact with the cell and enzyme mix. PIP-seq requires cell and reagent mix to be mixed and vortexed together, so the disulfide approach would not work. However, inDrop has previously used ortho-nitrobenzyl-mediated UV cleavage to release primers, an idea which could potentially be translated to for PIP-ATAC-seq.
