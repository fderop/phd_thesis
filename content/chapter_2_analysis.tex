The barcode-rank plot (also referred to as "knee or elbow plot") visualises the distribution of reads across all sequenced barcodes in the sequencing dataset. It is obtained by counting the number of reads per barcode, and ranking each barcode in a log-log figure - similarly in concept to a Zipf or Lorenz curve. If the dataset is sequenced to near-saturation, the total number of barcodes will exceed the whitelist, as sequencing/PCR/synthesis errors introduce non-whitelisted species. In the "ideal signal" case, all reads are evenly distributed across a small number of barcodes that matches the number of input cells (e.g. \num{10000} in the figure). This means that there are no quality differences between real cells, and there are no barcodes with low read counts. The opposite scenario ("ideal noise") has an even distribution of all reads across \textit{all} barcodes, and we cannot distinguish cells from noise. Real datasets fall between these two extremes. A high quality dataset exhibits a clear cut-off, where the number of reads per barcode sharply drops off in the transition between real cells and background noise. A dataset of lower quality will exhibit a more gradual transition between real cells and background noise. This can be caused by a variety of mechanisms, notably ambient RNA or non-pure cell barcodes on the beads.

\begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
	\item The number of cells captured in the dataset is given by the barcode rank where true cells are separated from background noise ("knee").

Conventional bulk RNA-seq analysis tools modeled gene counts using Poissonian (edgeR\sjcitep[]{robinsonEdgeRBioconductorPackage2010}) or Negative Binomial (DESeq\sjcitep[]{andersDifferentialExpressionAnalysis2010}) distributions. Statistical tests can then be used to identify statistically significant differentially expressed genes. Compared to bulk RNA-seq and microarray data, single-cell sequencing data is highly zero-inflated: when sampling transcripts in a single cell, the probability of a given gene not being detected is high, leading to large amounts of "dropouts" (false negative gene expression), necessitating more complex approaches. SCDE\sjcitep[]{kharchenkoBayesianApproachSinglecell2014} corrects for dropouts by taking into account aggregate expression levels across all cells to calculate dropout probabilities. These dropout probabilities are then used as priors to model gene expression levels in individual cells. Dropouts are weighted based on their prior probability, and likely relative (fold-change) gene expression levels are inferred. This approach ensures that highly-expressed genes are not penalized, and low-expressed genes are not inflated. [How are differentially expressed genes identified?]

Directly visualising correlations or variance between many RNA-seq or microarray samples is difficult due to the high-dimensionality of this data. Traditionally, this was done by first reducing the dimensionality of the data using PCA, and then visualising the first 2 principal components in a 2D space. Alternatively, heatmaps with dendrograms were used to order correlated samples. The more fine-grained nature of cell states in single-cell data further introduces non-linearity, since gradients of expression in the population are no longer averaged out [I think this is important]. This non-linearity precluded the use of PCA. This problem was partially solved by the introduction of t-SNE, a technique first designed to visualise the MNIST dataset of handwritten digits\sjcitep[.]{maatenVisualizingHighDimensionalData2008} The earliest implementations of t-SNE were used for single-cell mass cytometry data\sjcitep[]{amirViSNEEnablesVisualization2013} (45 protein levels per cell), then single-cell qPCR\sjcitep[]{saadatpourCharacterizingHeterogeneityLeukemic2014} (175 genes) and later single-cell whole transcriptomics\sjcitep[]{macoskohighlyparallelgenomewide2015} (\textasciitilde{\num{20000}}genes). t-SNE preserves local structure (close neighborhood of cells) at the cost of some concessions to global structure. Clusters of single cells can appear closer in the t-SNE embedding than they are in high-dimensional gene space. UMAP is a slightly newer dimensionality reduction algorithm which aims to better preserve global structure\sjcitep[.]{bechtDimensionalityReductionVisualizing2018} Some common criticisms of both t-SNE and UMAP is that they still distort global topologies\sjcitep[.]{chariSpeciousArtSinglecell2023} In addition, both methods are sensitive to hyperparameters (perplexity, number of neighbours) and stochasticity (e.g. initialisation). These tools are thus best used for exploration and visualisation rather than quantitative biological analysis.

By 2019, several single-cell sequencing technologies had emerged. Each had their own strengths and weaknesses, sometimes leading to technical artifacts in datasets even when assessing the same biological sample. Even the same technology used on different days could lead to batch effects due to sample degradation or other technical artifacts between experiments. This translates to cells clustering by dataset batch rather than biological state. In order to remedy this, Satija lab released an analysis package called \verb|Seurat|, which aimed to facilitate integration of multiple single-cell datasets across different technologies and conditions\sjcitep[.]{stuartcomprehensiveintegrationsinglecell2019} [Seurat v1 focused on correlating single-cell gene expression with in situ hybridisation (ISH) data] Seurat uses Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA\sjcitep{hotellingRelationsTwoSets1936}) to calculate linear combinations of genes (canonical correlation vectors, in this case "metagenes") that capture common information between two datasets. Cells are then co-projected in this shared metagene space to create a new "joint embedding" where clustering and differential expression analysis can be performed. The canonical vectors can be used to re-transform differential metagenes back to the original gene set used. Seurat's CCA implementation could also be used for label transfer: if one dataset is labeled or annotated, proximity in the joint embedding space can be used to transfer labels from one dataset to another.

LDA parameters (probabilities) are estimated using a Markov chain monte carlo approach: regions are initially randomly assigned to topics, and topic-region assignment is then updated iteratively in each cell based on two factors: (1) the topic's occurrence in the cell and (2) the region's occurrence in each topic across all other cells. Instead of sampling in the entire [], sampling is only performed in [] (collapsed Gibbs sampling), which reduces total computational complexity compared to sampling in both region-topic and topic-cell distribution. This approach is more efficient than other Bayesian approaches (e.g. variational inference) due to the extreme sparsity of the data. tSNE or UMAP can then be used to visualise the resulting (normalized) topic-cell distributions.

Another advantage of LDA over LSI is that LDA's topics are interpretable, whereas LSI's latent features can be abstract\sjcitep[.]{steyversProbabilisticTopicModels2007} This property is exploited in cisTopic, for example by extracting transcription factor binding motifs from the cis-regulatory topics found by LDA. cisTopic also introduces a region score which rewards regions that are more specific to a topic, and penalises regions that are ubiquitous across topics:

\[
\text{Region score} = \beta_{r,t} \left( \log \beta_{r,t} - \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log \beta_{r,t} \right)
\]

Consider Shannon entropy $H(X) = -\sum_{i} p(x_i) \log p(x_i)$, then the term \(\beta_{r,t} \log \beta_{r,t}\) can be considered the local information content of the region in topic context, while the term \(\frac{\beta_{r,t}}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \log \beta_{r,t}\) is the average log-probability of the region acrosss all topics [double check this, is beta a probability?]. The region score is thus an average-normalized measure of a given region's information content, indicating how specific it is to each topic. Topics can then be scored for TF motif enrichment by counting TF motif occurrence in the score-ranked regions and calculating the area under this rank curve (AUCell\sjcitep[]{aibarscenicsinglecell2017}).
