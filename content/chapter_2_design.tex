\chapter{Design and Development of HyDrop-ATAC and HyDrop-RNA}
\setcounter{figure}{1}  % Reset the figure counter to 0
In this chapter, we will walk through the design and development of HyDrop, our cost-efficient and transparent single-cell ATAC (and single-cell RNA) sequencing platform. We published this work in eLife in 2022\sjcitep[.]{deropHydropEnablesDropletbased2022}

\section{Motivation}
At the start of this project in 2018, single-cell sequencing methods had become established research tools but were still expensive and complicated enough to preclude routine use. At the time, academic scRNA-seq protocols were either low in throughput\sjcitep[,]{islamCharacterizationSinglecellTranscriptional2011, ramskoldFulllengthMRNASeqSinglecell2012, hashimshonyCELSeqSinglecellRNASeq2012, sasagawaQuartzSeqHighlyReproducible2013} or low in sensitivity\sjcitep[,]{macoskoHighlyParallelGenomewide2015, gierahnSeqWellPortableLowcost2017, caoComprehensiveSinglecellTranscriptional2017,kleinDropletBarcodingSinglecell2015} and reserved for use by specialised labs. The majority of single-cell experiments were performed using the 10x Chromium, a commercial solution offered by 10x Genomics which packaged inDrop's droplet microfluidic cell isolation with aspects of SMART-seq's RNA library preparation into a user-friendly kit. This kit provided a reliable - and more importantly \textit{convenient} - way to generate high-quality single-cell RNA-seq libraries\sjcitep[]{mereuBenchmarkingSinglecellRNAsequencing2020}. Many researchers preferred to use this kit, since it struck a comfortable balance between throughput and sensitivity per money spent.

We made three observations:
\begin{enumerate}
	\item At a cost of ~\euro{\num{10000}} per 8 samples of 10 000 cells, exploratory use of the 10x scRNA kit was still impractical.
	\item The kit operated as a black box, with limited possibility for modification.
	\item The kit's sensitivity was around 10 times higher than inDrop and Drop-seq's, implying that the sensitivity ceiling for droplet-based methods had not yet been reached by open-source methods.
\end{enumerate}

In addition, a droplet-based single-cell ATAC sequencing method was not available yet, constraining researchers to either low-sensitivity\supershortnotecite{cusanovichMultiplexSinglecellProfiling2015} or low-throughput\supershortnotecite{buenrostroSinglecellChromatinAccessibility2015,chenRapidRobustMethod2018} methods. We also understood that combined scRNA/ATAC-seq would be necessary in future studies to deconvolve the individual and (cross-) combined effects of gene expression and chromatin accessibility. A better understanding of how to design and build a droplet-based scATAC-seq method would be a good stepping stone to building multiomics technologies. These circumstances prompted us to start development of our own droplet-based sequencing method. Specifically, we wanted to build an open-source platform capable of both scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq, at higher sensitivities than inDrop and Drop-seq.

\section{Design and main technical challenges}
% Besides speed, throughput and reduction in reagent cost, droplet microfluidics provide another key advantage: when cells are physically isolated from one another in droplets, we can fully lyse each cell akin to what happens in a microwell-based experiment. A drawback is that cells cannot be easily washed or accessed anymore after once encapsulated, precluding more complex protocols. The next step in the single-cell indexing process is the introduction of the cell barcode oligonucleotide.

One of the first design choices was whether to use inDrop or Drop-seq style beads. Drop-seq's beads are made out of solid resin surface-coated with oligonucleotides whereas inDrop uses a soft, deformable polyacrylamide hydrogel with oligonucleotide barcodes loaded throughout.

Superficially, both ideas are similar, but inDrop-style beads have one major advantage: they can be densely stacked in a microfluidic channel, leading to deterministic loading. This enables a higher cell capture rate of > 80\% versus < 5\% (see also \autoref{sect:single_cell_sequencing_tech}). In addition, we reasoned that manufacturing and barcoding polyacrylamide hydrogels would be more straightforward than resin beads, for which we would need to rely on commercial suppliers. Both approaches face diffusion-related limitations: in Drop-seq, RNA transcripts need to diffuse from the cell onto the surface of the bead, whereas in inDrop, barcode oligonucleotides need to diffuse from the center of the polyacrylamide bead into the bulk of the reaction. We hypothesised that volumetric oligonucleotide loading and release might outperform a surface-constrained process.

From the onset of the project, we wanted to replace inDrop's photocleavable oligonucleotide linker by a disulfide bond, permitting the release of barcodes by reduction with DTT. This change would remove inDrop's requirement to work in the dark and possibly increase sensitivity. After all, ambient light exposure to light could cause the beads to prematurely release their barcodes - reducing the assay's specificity. This project had already been initiated by dr. Suresh Poovathingal, dr. Kristofer Davie and dr. Jasper Janssens before my arrival at the lab. My year as a master's student was spent building on their progress and continuing the development of this version of beads (see \notefullcite{deropOptimisationInDropScRNAseq2019} for more details). Sequencing revealed that our scRNA-seq libraries aligned well to the reference genome and were, as designed, strongly enriched in 3' ends of transcripts. The "bulk" characteristics of our RNA-seq libraries were thus promising. However, we also observed that the cell barcodes in our scRNA-seq libraries did not sequence efficiently. Between 67\% and 70\% of the barcode reads contained poly-G repeats. Out of the remaining ~30\%, only 0.2\% matched our barcode whitelist, compared to the > 95\% expected for 10x Genomics scRNA-seq datasets. This failure made it impossible to assign transcripts to cell of origin. At first, we thought that the custom sequencing primer used to sequence the cell barcodes might be at fault. We knew that our sequencer (NextSeq500) employed Illumina's 2-channel laser technology. G bases are encoded by the absence of signal, whereas the other three bases are encoded by positive fluorescence in either or both of the two dyes used. Poly-G base calls in the final data could thus be interpreted as the absence of any signal from the sequenced molecule, indicating that the sequencing primer may not have annealed properly - precluding the generation of any signal. We attempted to test this hypothesis by making a batch of mock beads in which the custom priming site was replaced by Illumina's standard primers - which are highly optimized for efficient hybridisation. The resulting dataset yielded barcode reads of which only 33\% had a poly-G problem, but still only around 23\% matched the whitelist - still far below what was necessary to do a successful single-cell sequencing experiment.

In parallel to our scRNA-seq troubles, our initial scATAC-seq development efforts also returned few promising results. We had begun by encapsulating pre-tagmented single nuclei into droplets together with ATAC-seq compatible beads and a variety of polymerases and commercial PCR master mixes. The idea was that PCR-cycling this emulsion would lead to linear amplification of the nuclei's accessible fragments with the ATAC-compatible cell barcode as a primer. However, over the course of several runs, we were not able to produce cell-bead emulsions that were resistant to PCR thermocycling: emulsions that seemed perfectly monodisperse before thermocycling would break and aggregate to a few large waterdroplets in oil after. Droplet merging would most certainly jeopardize the single-cell nature of our ATAC-seq experiments, as it would allow both chromatin and barcoded primers to diffuse and react freely, effectively generating cross-contamination between cells. Changes in the composition of the polymerase mastermixes or the addition of density regulators such as Optiprep (60\% w/v iodoxanol) and PEG-8000 did not solve this problem.

Out of these two major problems, the barcode sequencing problem was deemed most pressing, as both scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq would depend on ability to assign reads to cells. Realising that the two-read barcode sequencing in tandem with the custom sequencing primer overcomplicated the barcode read sequencing, we decided to redesign the barcode structure (\autoref{fig:sequencing_diagram_for_second_bead_design}).

In the updated design, the cell barcode was now read in one go, and did not require the use of a custom sequencing primer. The first sequencing library generated with these beads generated mixed results: on one hand, more than 90\% of the new barcode reads matched our whitelist, but on the other hand, these reads did not aggregate into pure cells in mixed-species experiments: all "cells" contained a mixture of human and mouse reads.

We thought that the diffusion of barcodes from the center of the polyacrylamide hydrogel bead into the droplet was a slow and inefficient process. To test this, we measured the beads' total primer content before and after DTT incubation, using probe fluorescence as a proxy (\autoref{fig:before_after_dtt}).

A strong residual fluorescent signal after even 50 minutes of continuous incubation with 50 mM DTT confirmed that much of the initial primer population was retained within the bead during supposed release (\autoref{fig:before_after_dtt}). Since DTT has a molecular weight comparable to a single nucleotide, and has good water solubility, we did not expect it to be the rate-limiting factor. Instead, we thought that the barcode oligonucleotide itself may be too large to efficiently diffuse out of the hydrogel matrix.

We thought that the best way to improve barcode diffusion into the reaction mix was to make our beads dissolvable like 10x Genomics' beads. We also considered that the poly-acrylamide oligomers that remained after bead dissolution could improve reaction efficiency through molecular crowding. Dissolvable beads would also simplify DNA extraction, as intact beads interfere with bead-based DNA purification (SPRI, Dynabeads, or Ampure XP). We therefore replaced the hydrogel's bis-acrylamide with N,N'-bis(acryloyl)cystamine, a version of the former that included a central reducable disulfide bond (\autoref{fig:acrylamide_polymerisation_dissolvable_beads}).

In parallel, we had sequenced the first HyDrop-ATAC libraries, and were stunned to observe the same problem: a promising aggregate ATAC-seq signal, with no cell types discernable. We reasoned that if both our scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq assays were failing in this manner, a common part between both the assays would be most likely at fault. In both methods, the cell/bead emulsion is broken after the barcoding of RNA transcripts/DNA fragments is complete, and these barcoded transcripts/fragments are size-selected out of the reaction mixture and amplified further. It is imperative that a cell's associated barcode is reserved for that cell only going further. We [[check]] figured that leftover, unused cell barcodes inadvertedly carried over through the size-selection step could cause spurious barcoding events in the downstream PCRs used to amplify and index the libraries. This mechanism of contamination could explain both observations that convinced us that our method was not single-cell: the vague separation between true cells and background noise barcodes on the kneeplot could be caused by all unused barcodes associating with transcripts/fragments post-emulsion breaking, and the lack of cell types could be explained by the dilution of each single-cell profile with random transcripts/fragments due to barcode promiscuity (\autoref{fig:barcode_carryover_mechamism}).

At the time, we had no good mechanistic understanding as to why this change solved the issue of cell purity. We considered that if the total primer load released by the bead into the droplet far exceeds what is needed, it will be more difficult to filter out this large mass of unused primers later on. This idea looped back to our previous observation that a single SPRI purification was not sufficient to remove all free barcode oligos from the emulsion PCR product. However, the double purification had (largely) resolved this issue for some time, so this alone could not have been the full explanation of the mechanism. We then considered that if sub-barcode primers are carried over from the first barcoding round into the second, and from the second into the third, these free and unwanted sub-barcode primers could hybridise with sites left free from the previous barcoding round. This idea was strongly supported by evidence in Sanger sequencing, where we observe that the first sub-barcode locus is very pure, but the middle sub-barcode locus is not, and the third sub-barcode locus is pure again. This could be explained by [[x]]. We figured that one way to circumvent this problem was to reduce the number of barcoding rounds from three (in a 96 x 96 x 96 fashion) to two (384 x 384). A drawback was an increase in up-front cost (now eight 96-well plates were needed instead of three), but we figured that prospects of getting the bead purity up were worth this risk, so we proceeded.

While the barcoding round cross-contamination hypothesis still holds strongly today, we failed to explain fully how acrydite primer concentration was related to this issue. Later on, after HyDrop v1 was already published, I came up with a new hypothesis that made more sense: if a high concentration (e.g. 50 uM) of free acrydite template oligos are incorporated into the matrix of the unbarcoded hydrogel bead, more sites will be available for the sub-barcode primers to hybridize with. While this will lead to increased overall signal, it will also lead to a larger fraction of untemplated acrydite oligos to remain unreacted after each round of sub-barcode. This inefficiency could be driven by either a lack of sub-barcode primers necessary to saturate the acrydite sites, or by inefficiency of the barcoding PCR itself. If any sub-barcodes from the previous rounds are then carried over to the next during splitting-and-pooling, these could  hybridise with such free acrydite stubs. While increasing the concentration of the sub-barcode oligo during PCR could lead to better saturation, it would exacerbate any carry-over problems.
