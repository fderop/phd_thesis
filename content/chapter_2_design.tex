\chapter{Design and Development of HyDrop-ATAC and HyDrop-RNA}
In this chapter, we will walk through the design and development of HyDrop, our cost-efficient and transparent single-cell ATAC (and single-cell RNA) sequencing platform. We published this work in eLife in 2022\sjcitep[.]{deropHydropEnablesDropletbased2022}

\section{Motivation}
When I began this research as a master student in 2018, single-cell sequencing methods had already broken through as established research tools, but were still expensive and complicated enough to prohibit them being considered routine experiments. At the time, non-commercial single-cell RNA sequencing methods were either low in throughput\sjcitep[,]{islamCharacterizationSinglecellTranscriptional2011, ramskoldFulllengthMRNASeqSinglecell2012, hashimshonyCELSeqSinglecellRNASeq2012, sasagawaQuartzSeqHighlyReproducible2013} or low in sensitivity\sjcitep[,]{macoskoHighlyParallelGenomewide2015, gierahnSeqWellPortableLowcost2017, caoComprehensiveSinglecellTranscriptional2017,kleinDropletBarcodingSinglecell2015} (see also \autoref{sect:single_cell_sequencing_tech}) and reserved for use by specialised labs. The majority of single-cell experiments were performed using a commercial solution offered by 10x Genomics, which packaged the working principles of inDrop's droplet microfluidic scRNA-seq protocol\supershortnotecite{kleinDropletBarcodingSinglecell2015} into a user-friendly kit \& benchtop device combination. The 10x kit provided a reliable way to generate high-quality single-cell RNA-seq libraries\sjcitep[]{mereuBenchmarkingSinglecellRNAsequencing2020} but costed in the order of \euro{\num{10000}} per 8 samples, limiting exploratory use. However, its sensitivity - which was around 10 times higher than inDrop and Drop-seq - demonstrated that the sensitivity ceiling of droplet microfluidics had not been reached by open-source methods. Moreover, a droplet-based single-cell ATAC sequencing method was not yet available to the public, again limiting researchers to either low-sensitivity\supershortnotecite{cusanovichMultiplexSinglecellProfiling2015} or low-throughput\supershortnotecite{buenrostroSinglecellChromatinAccessibility2015,chenRapidRobustMethod2018} methods. We wanted to tackle both challenges by developing an open-source platform for droplet-based single-cell sequencing, aiming to not only reduce the costs of single-cell sequencing, but also provide a platform for further technological development. Our initial approach was simple, but naive: we began by taking the detailed inDrop protocols that had been recently published\sjcitep[,]{zilionisSinglecellBarcodingSequencing2017} and implementing several differences that we thought would improve performance, and use the same concepts to build a single-cell version of ATAC-seq. Both of these goals proved more complicated than we expected.

\section{Initial design choices}
Besides speed, throughput and reduction in reagent cost, droplet microfluidics provide another key advantage: when cells are physically isolated from one another in droplets, they can undergo chemical reactions completely separately. We can thus fully lyse each cell akin to what happens in a pure microwell-based experiment (a drawback is that cells cannot be easily washed or accessed anymore after they are encapsulated, precluding more complex protocols). The next step in the single-cell indexing process is the introduction of the cell barcode oligonucleotide. inDrop and Drop-seq addressed this problem in two different ways: Drop-seq co-encapsulated the cell together with a hard, resin bead of which the surface carried the barcode oligonucleotides. inDrop opted for a polyacrylamide hydrogel which carried and released the barcoded primer using a UV-photocleavable linker.

Both of these solutions faced a similar diffusion problem: in Drop-seq, mRNA species had to diffuse from the bulk of the droplet onto the surface of the bead, whereas in inDrop, barcode oligonucleotides had to diffuse from the center of the polyacrylamide bead into the bulk of the reaction. Both bead strategies were considered, but inDrop had an additional advantage: its soft, hydrogel polyacrylamide beads could be densely stacked in the microfluidic chip, leading to a deterministic loading frequency that surpassed Drop-seq's stochastic, Poissonian loading (see also \autoref{sect:single_cell_sequencing_tech}). Due to the higher bead occupancy (> 80\% versus < 1\%) and subsequently higher cell capture rate, we opted to use inDrop's approach. In addition, we wanted to replace inDrop's photocleavable oligonucleotide linker by a disulfide bond, permitting the release of barcodes by reduction with DTT. This change would remove inDrop's requirement to work in the dark. Much of my first year at the lab was spent on the development of this iteration of barcoded beads, and formed the basis of my master thesis (see \notefullcite{deropOptimisationInDropScRNAseq2019} for more details). By the end, we had accomplished the following:

In short, the aggregate characteristics of our scRNA-seq libraries were promising. However, these experiments also revealed that the cell barcodes in our scRNA-seq libraries did not sequence well. Between 67\% and 70\% of our barcode reads were read as poly-G repeats. Out of the remaining ~30\%, only 0.2\% matched our barcode whitelist, compared to the > 95\% expected for 10x Genomics scRNA-seq datasets. This sequencing failure meant that it was impossible to assign transcripts to cell of origin. At first, we thought that the custom sequencing primer used to sequence the cell barcodes might be at fault. We knew that in our NextSeq550, which uses Illumina's 2-channel laser technology, a G base read is encoded by the absence of signal, whereas the other three bases were encoded by fluorescent signal in either or both of the two wavelengths used. Poly-G base calls in the final data could thus be interpreted as the absence of any signal from the sequenced molecule, indicating that the sequencing primer may not have annealed properly. We attempted to test this hypothesis by making a batch of mock beads in which the custom priming site was replaced by Illumina's standard primers - which are highly optimized for efficient hybridisation. The resulting dataset yielded barcode reads of which only 33\% had a poly-G problem, but still only around 23\% matched the whitelist - still far below what was necessary to do a successful single-cell sequencing experiment.

In parallel to our scRNA-seq troubles, our initial scATAC-seq development efforts returned few promising results. Over the course of several runs, we were not able to produce cell-bead emulsions that were stable during PCR thermocycling: emulsions that seemed perfectly monodisperse before thermocycling would break and aggregate to a few large waterdroplets in oil. Changes in the composition of our polymerase mastermixes and the addition of density regulators such as Optiprep (60\% w/v iodoxanol) and PEG did not solve this problem. Droplet merging would most certainly jeopardize the single-cell nature of our ATAC-seq experiments, as they allowed both untagged nucleic acid material and unused beads to diffuse and react freely.

These two major issues became the focus of the first year of my PhD. The barcode sequencing problems were most pressing, as they would affect both the scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq development. Realising that the two-read barcode sequencing in tandem with the custom sequencing primer complicated the barcode read sequencing too much, we decided to redesign the barcode structure (\autoref{fig:sequencing_diagram_for_second_bead_design}).

The strong residual fluorescence after even 50 minutes of continues incubation in DTT and subsequent washing confirmed our expectation that much of the initial primer population was retained within the bead during the supposed release (\autoref{fig:before_after_dtt}). DTT is expected to diffuse well into the hydrogel matrix, since it has a high solubility and a molecular mass similar to just a single nucleotide, so its diffusion could therefore not have been the rate-limiting factor. It appeared that the best way to improve barcode diffusion into the reaction mix was to make the hydrogel matrix itself dissolve. Moreover, some reports had shown that molecular crowding (the displacement of water by large macromolecules) could improve reverse transcription [[ref]]. We hypothesized that the acrylamide oligomers that remained after bead dissolution could contribute positively through this mechanism. 10x Genomics' scRNA-seq kit, which employed the dissolving bead strategy, reported a ~tenfold higher sensitivity compared to inDrop, another hint towards the possible benefits of dissolvable beads. We therefore decided to add [[bac]] to the acrylamide polymerisation reaction, replacing the regular [[bis-acrylamide]] crosslinker with a cleavable disulfide moeity (\autoref{fig:acrylamide_polymerisation_dissolvable_beads}).

Of course, these results motivated us to proceed, and we quickly raced to synthesize a new batch of 96x96x96 ATAC beads. Just like our latest batch of scRNA-seq beads, the new scATAC-seq beads exhibited a remarkably strong fluorescence when labeled with complementary fluorescent probes. We performed a scATAC-seq run on PBMC, which was shallowly sequenced to test the first library generated with the 96x96x96 design. We then generated an additional scATAC-seq library on Drosophila larval brain to assess how the method would handle small input cells. Both of these libraries were sequenced deeply. Analysis of this data harbored a nasty surprise for us: we were not able to separate any cell types in either the PBMC or the larval brain cells, similarly to the TSA/DMSO scRNA-seq experiments I had performed earlier.

Again, the aggregate characteristics of our libraries matched the expectations for 3' RNA-seq (\autoref{fig:pbmc_larval_brain}a), but the distribution of these reads across cell barcodes seemed random. Moreover, the barcode-ranking of the number of transcripts as visualised in "knee" or "elbow" plots did not reveal the clean cut-off between true cells and background noise barcodes we observed in 10x Genomics-generated libraries ((\autoref{fig:pbmc_larval_brain}b)). These background noise barcodes were presumed to originate from ambient barcodes present in the bead stock, or ambient RNA present in droplets without a cell.

Putting both of these observations together, I realised that the significantly stronger total barcode content of our beads compare to the 10x beads might not necessarily play to our advantage [[maybe frame it differently, as at this point we were actually suspecting the purification]]: after barcoding RNA transcripts/DNA fragments in the emulsion, the droplets are merged, and the barcoded transcripts/fragments are size-selected out of the reaction mixture. I figured that leftover, unused cell barcodes carried over through this filtration step could cause spurious barcoding events in the subsequent library prepartion PCR. This mechanism of contamination could explain both of our observations: both cell barcodes associated with true cells and unused cell barcodes would go on and barcode random transcripts, leading to the broad and randomized distribution of transcripts across barcodes as opposed to the clear cutoff  between true cells and noise in an idealised scenario (\autoref{fig:barcode_carryover_mechamism}).

At this point, it dawned upon us that something could be seriously wrong with the fundamental nature of our beads. We began to doubt whether the much stronger fluorescence (and thus total primer content) of our beads played to our advantage as much as we had initially thought. Logically, if the total primer load in the droplet far exceeds what is needed, it will be more difficult to filter these unused primers out, and we already had a theoretical basis for how these unused primers would lead to spurious barcoding.

Still, there was a high level of background noise barcodes. We began to question whether the barcode sequences on our beads were pure. If a supposedly uniquely barcoded bead contained a distribution of different barcodes, none of our post-hoc cell barcodes represented a cell, but rather a mixture of transcripts/fragments originating from many cells.
