\chapter{Design and development of HyDrop-ATAC and HyDrop-RNA}
\label{ch:design_development}
\setcounter{figure}{1}
In this chapter, we will walk through the design and development of HyDrop, our cost-efficient and transparent single-cell ATAC (and single-cell RNA) sequencing platform. We published this work in eLife in 2022 (\autoref{fig:hydrop}, \autoref{app:hydrop})\sjcitep[.]{derophydropenablesdropletbased2022}

\section{Method design and prior work}
% Besides speed, throughput and reduction in reagent cost, droplet microfluidics provide another key advantage: when cells are physically isolated from one another in droplets, we can fully lyse each cell akin to what happens in a microwell-based experiment. A drawback is that cells cannot be easily washed or accessed anymore after once encapsulated, precluding more complex protocols. The next step in the single-cell indexing process is the introduction of the cell barcode oligonucleotide.

One of the first design choices was whether to use inDrop or Drop-seq style beads. Drop-seq's beads are made out of solid resin surface-coated with oligonucleotides whereas inDrop uses a soft, deformable polyacrylamide hydrogel with oligonucleotide barcodes loaded throughout. Both ideas are superficially similar, but inDrop's soft hydrogel beads have one major advantage: they can be densely stacked in a microfluidic channel, leading to deterministic loading, enabling a higher cell capture rate of > 80\% versus < 5\% (see also \autoref{sect:single_cell_sequencing_tech}). We also considered that manufacturing and barcoding polyacrylamide hydrogels would be more straightforward than resin beads, the latter which we would need to source from commercial suppliers. Based on these considerations, the choice was made to use polyacrylamide hydrogel beads.

Next, we considered the bead barcoding reaction. In Drop-seq, oligonucleotide barcodes are synthesized directly on the resin bead in 12 rounds of split-pool oligonucleotide synthesis (4\textsuperscript{12}). In inDrop, oligonucleotide sub-barcodes are annealed and copied to a primer stub by non-cycled isothermal extension in 96\textsuperscript{3} split-pooling. We reasoned that a cycled linear barcode amplification reaction rather than an isothermal extension could lead to higher bead barcoding reaction efficiency, subsequent higher density of barcodes within the bead, and higher in-droplet target capture as a result.

We also wanted to replace inDrop's photocleavable oligonucleotide linker by a disulfide bond, permitting the release of barcodes by reduction with DTT. This change would remove inDrop's requirement to work in the dark and possibly increase sensitivity. After all, ambient light exposure to light could cause the beads to prematurely release their barcodes - reducing the assay's specificity. By the end of the year, the state of our development had reached the following milestones:\sjcitep[]{deropOptimisationInDropScRNAseq2019}

\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
	\item We designed and manufactured a microfluidic chip for the generation of the stable acrylamide monomer emulsions needed to produce polyacrylamide hydrogel beads using soft lithography\sjcitep[.]{xiaSoftLithography1998} We characterised the relationship between oil and water flow rates on this chip, and predicted flow rates that would generate \SI{50}{\micro\metre} diameter droplets with minimal size variance using iterative linear model optimisation (\autoref{fig:bead_diameter_optimisation}). The resulting beads were indeed of a stable \SI{53}{\micro\metre} diameter with a standard deviation of \SI{5}{\micro\metre}.

	      \svgfigure{im/indrop_model.pdf_tex}
	      {Bead diameter optimisation}
	      {Bead diameter optimisation strategy. From \notefullcite{deropOptimisationInDropScRNAseq2019}.}
	      {fig:bead_diameter_optimisation}

	\item We barcoded these polyacrylamide hydrogel beads using split-pooling, producing 384 \texttimes 384 possible cell barcodes across all beads.

	\item We replaced inDrop's photocleavable oligonucleotide linker with a disulfide bond, and we successfully validated that our beads, after incubation with DTT, released primers into the supernatant.

	\item We designed a new cell encapsulation microfluidic chip which included several layers of passive filters due to prior experiences with flow-obstructing dust particles. Using this updated design, we were able to reliably encapsulate single cells with reagent mixes across several experiments.

	\item To implement scRNA-seq on our now functional droplet platform, we tested several different cDNA generation and subsequent sequencing library preparation strategies, focusing on total cDNA and library yield as optimisation targets. Ultimately, we settled on a protocol that was based on in-droplet Maxima H Minus reverse transcription, PCR amplification of cDNA by KAPA polymerase, and NEB Next Ultra II FS DNA sequencing library preparation. This protocol was different from both inDrop, which uses a more involved reverse transcription \textrightarrow{} second-strand synthesis \textrightarrow{} in-vitro transcription \textrightarrow{} random hexamer reverse transcription \textrightarrow{} PCR workflow, and Drop-seq, which relies on Tn5-based sequencing library prep from cDNA.
\end{enumerate}

In short, we had set up a workflow for producing hydrogel beads, barcoding those beads using linear amplification, and using them in scRNA-seq experiments that produced sequencing-ready cDNA libraries. In parallel to the scRNA-seq research, we had also begun preliminary scATAC-seq trials. This required the production of a new type of beads, this time with oligos that annealed to the Tn5 insertion adapter sequence instead of to poly(A) tails. Our scATAC-seq protocol relied on encapsulating pre-tagmented single nuclei into droplets together with the new ATAC-seq compatible beads and a variety of polymerases and commercial PCR master mixes. PCR cycling these emulsions should then lead to pre-amplified scATAC-seq libraries.

\section{Execution and troubleshooting}
What followed was a long series of troubleshooting issues which were often shared between the scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq protocols. In rough chronological order, we dealt with the following:

\textbf{Problem:} The bulk data characteristics of our earliest scRNA-seq libraries were very promising: expected cDNA and final library fragment size distributions were as expected, \textasciitilde{40}\% of reads aligned to reference transcriptome and these reads were 3' enriched. However, we were experiencing difficulties sequencing the cell barcodes: between 67\% and 70\% of the barcode reads contained poly-G repeats. Out of the remaining ~30\%, only 0.2\% matched our barcode whitelist, compared to the > 95\% expected for 10x Genomics scRNA-seq datasets. This failure made it impossible to assign transcripts to cell of origin.

\textbf{Solution:} At first, we thought that the custom sequencing primer used to sequence the cell barcodes might be at fault. We knew that our NextSeq500 used Illumina's 2-channel laser technology. \verb|G| bases are encoded by the absence of signal, whereas the other three bases are encoded by positive fluorescence in either or both of the two dyes used. Poly-G base calls in the final data could thus be interpreted as the absence of any signal from the sequenced molecule, indicating that the sequencing primer may not have annealed properly - precluding the generation of any signal. We attempted to test this hypothesis by making a batch of mock beads in which the custom priming site was replaced by Illumina's standard primers - which are highly optimized for specificity. The resulting dataset yielded barcode reads of which now only 33\% had a poly-\verb|G| problem, but still only around 23\% matched the whitelist - still far below what was necessary to do a successful single-cell sequencing experiment. Next, we redesigned the barcode structure so that the cell barcode was now read in one go, and did not require the use of a custom sequencing primer. More than 90\% of the new barcode reads matched our whitelist. The remaining 10\% could be attributed to incorporation errors during vendor oligonucleotide synthesis, PCR, and sequencing errors.

Later on, this change also had an important effect on our scATAC-seq assay: it allowed us to sequence both ends of the chromatin fragments as opposed to only a single end. Since ATAC-seq quantifies Tn5 insertion events, this increases the counting sensitivity and positional accuracy of called peaks. Note that insertion events within the same peak region are correlated due to proximity, so total information content is not doubled by sequencing both ends of all fragments. Another welcome effect of paired-end sequencing is that it improves cross-contamination estimation: when two cell barcodes are statistically enriched in identical fragments (exact same insertion sites with base-pair accuracy), they are highly likely to be cross-contaminated, a feature which can be used to estimate bead purity\sjcitep[.]{lareau2020inference}

% Both inDrop and Drop-seq's approaches face diffusion-related limitations: in Drop-seq, RNA transcripts need to diffuse from the cell onto the surface of the bead, whereas in inDrop, barcode oligonucleotides need to diffuse from the center of the polyacrylamide bead into the bulk of the reaction. We hypothesised that volumetric oligonucleotide loading and release might outperform a surface-constrained process. Of course, the 10x scRNA-seq used dissolvable beads, and we thought that this change was a major contributor to its high sensitivity.

\textbf{Problem:} Our first generation of beads had barcode oligos linked to their hydrogel matrix using a cleavable disulfide linker. We hypothesized that diffusion of barcodes from the center of the polyacrylamide hydrogel bead into the droplet would be a slow and reaction rate-limiting process. To test diffusion, we measured the beads' total primer content before and after DTT incubation, using probe fluorescence as a proxy. A strong residual fluorescent signal after even 50 minutes of continuous incubation with 50 mM DTT confirmed that much of the initial primer population was retained within the bead. Since DTT has a molecular weight comparable to a single nucleotide, and has good water solubility, we did not expect its diffusion to be the rate-limiting factor. Of course, 10x Genomics' beads were dissolvable, and we reasoned that this contributed to their assay's increased sensitivity.

\textbf{Solution:} We included N,N'-Bis(acryloyl)cystamine (BAC) in the hydrogel polymerization reaction, replacing classic bisacrylamide and making the gel polymer's crosslinks reversibly cleavable under reducing conditions with DTT. These beads dissolved in under 10 minutes of exposure to 10 mM DTT at room temperature.

\textbf{Problem:} Cell-plus-bead-in-oil emulsions did not survive PCR thermocycling, which was necessary for amplifying accessible genome fragments for our scATAC-seq efforts. During PCR, microdroplets coalesced into large drops, meaning that bulk rather than single-cell libraries were be generated.

\textbf{Solution:} We tested various changes to the protocol, including different PCR master mixes (NEB, KAPA, Terra, Q5), PCR programs (number of cycles, temperatures) and density regulators (PEG-8000, Iodoxanol), but this issue was ultimately solved as a welcome side-effect of making the beads dissolvable. We then realised that the thermocycling induced convective flows in the emulsions, leading to bead collisions which broke the emulsion. When the beads were dissolved within the droplets, this effect disappeared and emulsions remained stable throughout PCR cycling.

\textbf{Milestone:} At this point, we were able to sequence cell barcodes, align them to a whitelist, align the genomic reads to a reference (both transcriptomic and genomic), and keep stable emulsions throughout the process. Our scRNA-seq data was 3' transcriptome enriched, and our scATAC-seq data aggregated into peaks and exhibited typical ATAC-seq fragment length distribution, with nucleosome and helical pitch. However, data in both our scATAC-seq and scRNA-seq libraries did not aggregate into pure cells in mixed-species experiments: all "cells" contained a mixture of human and mouse reads. In addition, rather than sharp cutoffs between cell barcodes and low-signal noise barcodes, we observed a gradual transition from cells to noise. This would prove to be the most challenging issue during the entire HyDrop research campaign.

\textbf{Solution attempt 1:} We reasoned that the poor separation between true cells and background noise barcodes, and the observed cross-species contamination both practically meant that any cell barcode could associate with any cell during our assay. One hypothesis was that somewhere along the process, unused cell barcodes were associating with un-cell-barcoded (or potentially even cell-barcoded) DNA/RNA. In the droplet protocol, emulsion reverse transcription (scRNA) or emulsion PCR (scATAC) was followed by emulsion breaking and a bead cleanup to remove primers before subsequent PCR. We decided to run the pre-PCR library on Agilent BioAnalyzer, both before and after bead cleanup, and immediately observed very strong primer peaks that exactly matched the size of our expected cell barcodes released by the beads. This meant that after emulsion breaking, unused barcodes were free to associate randomly with un-barcoded DNA/RNA present, that bead cleanup was not sufficient, and that a large amount of free barcodes were passed into the amplification PCR. Here, these free barcodes could randomly capture un-tagged cDNA or ATAC fragments, or even out-compete PCR primers annealing to correctly barcoded DNA molecules.

We thus introduced a second magnetic purification after emulsion breaking, which strongly reduced free cell barcode contamination, but only mildly improved the species mixing we observed in our libraries. For scATAC-seq, we also tried using exponential in-droplet PCR by adding a reverse primer to the reaction, hoping to improve barcode consumption at the risk of introducing PCR biases, but this did also not improve purity.

\textbf{Solution attempt 2:} Thus far, we had not yet confirmed that our PCR-produced beads were pure: cross-contamination could also occur during bead production, leading to mixed-barcode beads rather than clonal beads. These contaminated beads would generate randomly barcoded fragments, explaining the observed noise downstream. This idea was strongly supported by evidence in Sanger sequencing, where we observe that the first sub-barcode locus was very pure, but the second was not, and the third was more pure again. This could mean that throughout barcoding, there is cross-contamination of the individual wells, leading to a mixture of primers from round one reaching the second barcoding stage and causing secondary barcoding contamination there. In order to test this, we performed a pilot run with two rounds (384\textsuperscript{2}) of barcoding rather than three (96\textsuperscript{3}). This change improved species purity in our species-mixing experiments, but still not to usable levels, and did not convince us to stick with this approach.

\textbf{Solution attempt 3:} At this point in time, we were very close to giving up. It had been more than a year since we began tackling the contamination problem, and, the diagonal we observed on the species-mixing plots began resembling a large middle finger (\autoref{fig:middlefinger}).

Finally, we reduced the acrydite primer load during bead generation from \SI{50}{\micro\molar} to \num{1.5}, \num{6}, \num{12.5} and \SI{6.25}{\micro\molar}, drastically reducing the total number of available sites for barcoding. Beads made with the 6 and \SI{12.5}{\micro\molar} primer concentrations immediately produced species-pure scRNA-seq, and later scATAC-seq libraries, whereas the \SI{1.5}{\micro\molar} condition did not produce detectable cDNA.

Most likely, this change affected purity in two ways: first, the polymerase reaction is now more likely to saturate the number of available barcode sites, reducing the number of available "previous-round" priming sites in the next round. Second, a lower total primer load released into the droplet is more likely to be purified out completely. Later research (post-publication) also indicated that washing bead wells individually during barcoding rather than in a pooled manner further increased bead purity and thus sensitivity. Individual dilutive washing of the sub-barcoding primers before pooling prevents these barcodes from annealing with remaining un-templated sites from the previous round of barcoding, improving barcode purity.

In parallel we had also implemented a number of small novelties. For example, we added Pitstop 2, a selective clathrin-mediated endocytosis inhibitor which also permeabilizes the nuclear membrane by destabilizing nucleoporin complexes\sjcitep[,]{mulqueen2020improved} to the tagmentation reaction. This change slightly increased total yield and peak fidelity. We also added guanidinium isothiocyanate, a strong chaotrope known to denature proteins and solubilize nucleic acids, to the emulsion after PCR, and this further improved library yield. Next, we titrated several different sources of Tn5 and found no discernible difference in performance or fragment biases between commercial (Illumina, diagenode) and open-source Tn5\sjcitep[.]{picellitn5transposasetagmentation2014} Since Nextera Tn5 comprises 46\% of the droplet reaction cost, savings made by using in-house Tn5 are considerable. In addition, we tested a number of optimisations for HyDrop-RNA. Exonuclease I (Exo I) has been used in single-cell RNA-seq workflows to remove excess primers and barcodes that could lead to non-specific priming\sjcitep[.]{macoskohighlyparallelgenomewide2015} Addition or exclusion of Exo I did not appear to make a difference in sensitivity. Introduction of two riboguanosines in the template-switching oligo, which improve thermal annealing stability to 3' \verb|CCC| motif of the untemplated cDNA, also did not appear to increase sensitivity\sjcitep[.]{picellifulllengthrnaseqsingle2014} Second-strand synthesis through Bst2.0 or Klenow fragment showed improvements\sjcitep[,]{stickels2020highly} but the best cDNA amplification strategy proved to be PCR thermocycling supplemented with GTP\sjcitep[.]{hagemannjensensinglecellrnacounting2020}

\section{Key results}
After the above troubleshooting, HyDrop achieved functional scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq: mixed-species experiments returned pure (\textasciitilde{90}\%) single-cell profiles, and we were able to assign cell types to our first experiments on mixed-cancer cell lines (MCF-7 and PC3). We applied HyDrop-ATAC to flash-frozen mouse cortex, generating \num{7996} high-quality single-nucleus ATAC profiles in one run. We also applied HyDrop-RNA to the same cohort of mouse cortex samples, and to FAC-sorted Drosophila neurons, capturing \num{7996} and \num{973} cells respectively. The latter was particularly exciting, since this data contributed directly to ongoing research at the time by profiling rare cells not observed in 10x scATAC-seq data on the same sample\sjcitep[.]{janssens2022decoding} HyDrop-RNA achieved median \num{3389} UMIs and \num{1658} genes per cell in mouse cortex. HyDrop-ATAC recovered \num{4148} median fragments per cell, with high TSS enrichment scores. These sensitivities were sufficient for confident cell-type annotation in both modalities, revealing 19 and 30 distinct cell types in the scATAC and scRNA-seq dataset respectively.
