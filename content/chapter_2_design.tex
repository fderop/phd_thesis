\chapter{Design and Development of HyDrop-ATAC and HyDrop-RNA}
In this chapter, we will walk through the design and development of HyDrop, our cost-efficient and transparent single-cell ATAC (and single-cell RNA) sequencing platform. We published this work in eLife in 2022\sjcitep[.]{deropHydropEnablesDropletbased2022}

\section{Motivation and necessity}
When I began this research as a master student in 2018, single-cell sequencing methods had already broken through as established research tools, but were still expensive and complicated enough to prohibit them from being considered routine experiments. At the time, non-commercial single-cell RNA sequencing methods were either low in throughput\sjcitep[,]{islamCharacterizationSinglecellTranscriptional2011, ramskoldFulllengthMRNASeqSinglecell2012, hashimshonyCELSeqSinglecellRNASeq2012, sasagawaQuartzSeqHighlyReproducible2013} or low in sensitivity\sjcitep[,]{macoskoHighlyParallelGenomewide2015, gierahnSeqWellPortableLowcost2017, caoComprehensiveSinglecellTranscriptional2017,kleinDropletBarcodingSinglecell2015} and reserved for use by specialised labs. Most single-cell experiments outside of these specialised labs were performed using a commercial solution offered by 10x Genomics. The 10x Genomics product packaged the working principles of the then recently published inDrop\supershortnotecite{kleinDropletBarcodingSinglecell2015} into a user-friendly kit plus benchtop device combination, and was capable of generating high-quality single-cell RNA-seq libraries using droplet microfluidics\sjcitep[.]{mereuBenchmarkingSinglecellRNAsequencing2020} A single 10x Genomics Chromium reagent package (enough for 8 samples) cost in the order of \euro{\num{10000}}, limiting exploratory use, but the existence of this droplet-based technology demonstrated that the sensitivity of open-source droplet microfluidic methods could be improved several fold. Moreover, a droplet-based single-cell ATAC sequencing method was not yet available to the public, again limiting researchers to either low-sensitivity\supershortnotecite{cusanovichMultiplexSinglecellProfiling2015} or low-throughput\supershortnotecite{buenrostroSinglecellChromatinAccessibility2015,chenRapidRobustMethod2018} methods. We wanted to tackle both challenges by developing an open-source platform for droplet-based single-cell sequencing, and showcase this method by producing a high-quality scRNA-seq assay, and the first droplet-based scATAC-seq. We hoped that our method would not only reduce the costs of single-cell sequencing, but also provide a platform for further technological development. Our initial approach was simple, but naive: we would take the detailed inDrop protocols that had been recently published\sjcitep[,]{zilionisSinglecellBarcodingSequencing2017} implement several differences that we thought would improve performance, and use the same concepts to build a single-cell version of ATAC-seq. Both of these goals proved far more complicated than we expected.

\section{Initial design choices}
The first design choice we faced was the method of cell separation. The throughput of pure microwell-based methods was too low (hundreds of cells per day), and virtual separation using combinatorial indexing strategies (up to tens of thousands of cells per day) generally had low sensitivity, and would require custom barcoded Tn5 to perform scATAC-seq. Droplet microfluidics, which can encapsulate thousands of cells into individual compartments in minutes, appeared to be an attractive middle ground solution. Besides high throughput, droplet encapsulation provides another key advantage: when cells are physically isolated from one another in droplets, they can undergo chemical reactions completely separately. We can thus fully lyse each cell akin to what happens in a pure microwell-based experiment. We reasoned that an environment in which barcoded primers and cell contents are mixed would provide better sensitivity than techniques where diffusion of either the barcoded primer (sciRNA) or the cell's nucleic acids (Drop-seq, Seq-Well) limit the barcoding reaction rate.

inDrop attempted to solve this problem by delivering barcoded primers from the bead to the lysed cell in the droplet using a photocleavable linker. However, our earliest inDrop experiments proved that release of the primer was very inefficient.

We reasoned that for delivery of the primer into the droplet to occur efficiently, the entire bead must first dissolve, a concept which had already been demonstrated by 10x Genomics in their Chromium solution. We generated barcoded hydrogel beads that can dissolve and release their embedded barcoded oligonucleotide. Polyacrylamide beads incorporating disulfide cross-linkers and short oligonucleotide polymerase chain reaction (PCR) priming sites were generated by droplet microfluidics similar to a recently published method (Wang et al., 2020). A custom droplet microfluidic chip (Figure 3—figure supplement 1) was employed to produce beads of approximately 50 um diameter. These hydrogel beads were then barcoded using a modified three-round split-pool linear amplification synthesis method (Klein et al., 2015; Zilionis et al., 2017), resulting in 96 x 96 x 96 (884,736) barcode possibilities (see Supplementary file 1 - "Molecular sequence description of HyDrop bead barcoding" for an in-depth visualization of the nucleic acid sequences in every step). The terminal sequence used in the final round of barcoding can be varied depending on the assay the beads will be used for (see Methods, Figure 1a). A sequence complementary to the Tn5 transposase adapter was used to capture tagmented chromatin fragments in scATAC-seq and a unique molecular identifier (UMI) (Kivioja et al., 2011)+ poly (dT) sequence was used to capture and count poly (A)+ mRNA in scRNA-seq (protocols described further). The barcoded beads were stored in a glycerol-based freezing buffer at -80°C in order to prevent loss of primers over time (Figure 1—figure supplement 1).
